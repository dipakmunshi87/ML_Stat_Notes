The method families

Six ways in, and what each one is for

Decomposition residuals. Split the series into trend, seasonal and remainder — STL, or a robust running median plus a per-phase median — then apply a robust univariate rule to the remainder alone:

xt=Tt+St+Rt,flag t when 0.6745 ∣Rt−R~∣MAD⁡(R)>3.5.x_t=T_t+S_t+R_t,\qquad \text{flag } t \text{ when } \frac{0.6745\,|R_t-\tilde R|}{\operatorname{MAD}(R)}>3.5.xt​=Tt​+St​+Rt​,flag t when MAD(R)0.6745∣Rt​−R~∣​>3.5.

Simple, interpretable, and hard to beat on seasonal business series. It also turns a contextual anomaly into a point anomaly, which is exactly why it works: subtracting the seasonal term is defining the context.

Forecast residuals. Fit ARIMA, exponential smoothing, a state-space model or a neural forecaster, and flag large one-step prediction errors. The state-space route is the one worth preferring, because a Kalman filter gives you a predictive variance as well as a prediction:

zt=yt−y^t∣t−1Ft,Ft=ZPt∣t−1Z⊤+H.z_t=\frac{y_t-\hat y_{t\mid t-1}}{\sqrt{F_t}},\qquad F_t=Z P_{t\mid t-1}Z^{\top}+H.zt​=Ft​​yt​−y^​t∣t−1​​,Ft​=ZPt∣t−1​Z⊤+H.

Those standardised innovations are, under the model, independent and unit variance. The threshold is then calibrated rather than chosen, which is the same advantage Mahalanobis distance had on page 1 and almost nothing else in this subject has.

Change-point detection. CUSUM, PELT, Bayesian online change-point detection. A different question — when did the regime change rather than which point is odd — and frequently the one actually being asked.

The matrix profile, which deserves more attention

For every subsequence of length mmm, compute the zzz-normalised Euclidean distance to its nearest non-trivial match anywhere else in the series. Low values are motifs, repeated patterns. The highest value is the discord: the most unusual subsequence in the series.

It is essentially parameter-free apart from mmm, it needs no training and no model of the data, and it is the most direct attack on collective anomalies that exists. stumpy implements it efficiently.

Two more

Spectral residual, borrowed from visual saliency detection: take the Fourier transform, subtract a smoothed version of the log spectrum, transform back. Fast, and good on streaming univariate data.

Deep sequence models. LSTM or transformer forecasters, or autoencoders over windows. Necessary when the series is multivariate with real cross-channel structure, and overkill otherwise.

The framing that makes this tidy

All six reduce to the same two steps: build a prediction of what the series should be doing at time ttt, and score the disagreement. They differ only in what “should” means — the seasonal average, the forecast, the nearest matching subsequence, the current regime. Choosing a method is choosing a definition of context.

The innovation form is worth writing out because it is the cleanest example in the whole subject of a threshold that comes from the model rather than from the analyst. If someone asks how you would set a threshold on a temporal detector, this is the answer that sounds like statistics rather than like guessing.

The first trap

Point adjustment, and how it manufactured a literature

A widely used evaluation protocol for time-series anomaly detection says: if any single point inside a true anomalous segment is flagged, mark the entire segment as correctly detected. The stated justification is operational — an alert anywhere inside the event is enough to act on it.

The consequence is that the metric stops measuring detection. Consider a segment of length LLL and a detector that flags each timestamp independently with probability qqq. The chance the segment is scored as fully detected is

1−(1−q)L,1-(1-q)^{L},1−(1−q)L,

which for L=46L=46L=46 and q=0.05q=0.05q=0.05 is 0.910.910.91. Flagging one timestamp in twenty at random therefore “detects” nine segments in ten, and each detection is credited with LLL true positives rather than one. Recall goes to nearly one, and because the true positives have been multiplied by the segment length, precision survives as well.

This is not a subtle bias. Published work has shown that random scoring beats reported state-of-the-art results under this protocol, and that a substantial part of the apparent progress in deep time-series anomaly detection is an artefact of it.

The demonstration on the next slide computes both numbers, for a real detector and for a coin flip, on the same series.

The question to ask

If someone shows you an F1 above 0.90.90.9 on a time-series anomaly benchmark, ask whether point adjustment was used. It is one sentence, and it is the single most useful thing you can say in a discussion of these results.

What to use instead

Range-based precision and recall, which credit partial overlap of a detected range with a true range, with explicit weights for existence, size and position.

Event-level counting. One true positive per detected event, one false positive per spurious alert, and detection latency reported separately. Crude, honest, and it matches how the alerts are actually consumed.

Precision at kkk on events, with kkk set by the review budget. Page 5 makes the case for this as the default.

The second trap, in one line

Temporal leakage. Never shuffle. Random cross-validation on a time series lets the model see the future; use rolling-origin or blocked splits. And fit every transformation — scaling, imputation, feature selection, the seasonal decomposition itself — on the training window only. Fitting a scaler on the whole series before splitting is the most common version, and it quietly leaks the anomalies into the training statistics.

The formula is the useful thing to have ready: the probability that a random detector at rate qqq is credited with a segment of length LLL is 1−(1−q)L1-(1-q)^L1−(1−q)L, which is close to one for any realistic segment length. Segment length and flag rate are both in the analyst's control, which is what makes the protocol so easy to game without intending to.

Deployment

Yesterday's normal is not today's

A detector is not a result, it is a running process, and the distribution it was fitted to will move. Distinguish the kinds, because they need different responses:

  • Covariate shift — p(x)p(x)p(x) changes, the relationship does not. Often benign for a one-class detector, and fatal for a threshold calibrated on a quantile.
  • Concept drift — p(y∣x)p(y\mid x)p(y∣x) changes. What counted as an anomaly no longer does. This one always requires relabelling.
  • Seasonality you did not model — the cheapest and most embarrassing cause of a detector that starts alerting every December.

The practical requirements are unglamorous and rarely budgeted: monitor the score distribution rather than only the alert count, because a shifting score distribution with a stable alert count is a detector about to fail; keep a labelled holdout refreshed by review; define the retraining trigger before deployment rather than after the first incident; and keep a frozen baseline detector running alongside, so you can tell drift from a bad release.

For genuinely streaming data, River provides online detectors and alibi-detect covers drift detection as a first-class concern. Robust random cut forests support insertion and deletion, which is what “online” has to mean for a partition-based model.

The monitoring that actually catches problems

Track the quantiles of the score, not the count above the threshold. If the ninety-ninth percentile of the score doubles while the alert count stays flat, your threshold has stopped meaning what it meant, and you will find out from an incident rather than a dashboard.

The feedback loop

Whatever the detector flags gets reviewed, and the reviews become the labels, and the labels train the next detector. It will therefore learn to agree with itself and to ignore the anomaly classes it never flagged. Breaking the loop costs something: review a random sample of unflagged data, on purpose, and budget for it.

The latency question

Detection delay is usually as important as accuracy and almost never reported. A method that finds the event four hours later than another at the same precision is a worse method. Report it.

The frozen-baseline recommendation is the one people skip and then regret. Without it, every change in alert volume is ambiguous between the world changing and your last deployment being wrong.

Carried forward

What to take to the last page

Choosing a method is choosing a context

Raw values, seasonal remainder, forecast error, nearest matching subsequence — each defines what the series should have been doing. The anomaly type you care about decides which definition you need, and no single one covers all three.

Prefer a calibrated threshold when you can get one

A state-space model's standardised innovations are unit variance under the model, so the threshold follows from the model rather than from a quantile you picked. That is worth real effort.

Distrust temporal benchmark numbers

Ask about point adjustment, and ask how the splits were made. Between those two questions you can account for most of the apparent progress in the field.
