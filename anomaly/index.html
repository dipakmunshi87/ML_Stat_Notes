Anomaly set · what each method assumes, and where each one breaks

Finding the points
that do not belong

Almost every method here is a variation on one sentence: build a description of normal, then measure how badly each point fits it. The methods differ in what they are willing to assume about “normal”, and every one of them fails in a way you can predict from that assumption alone.

Five pages, six demonstrations, each computing its numbers in your browser while you watch. Nothing on these pages is a stored result.

The words, and why they are not synonyms

These get used interchangeably, and the differences are not pedantic — each one implies a different problem, and often a different method.

Outlier A point far from the bulk of the data. Purely descriptive. It says nothing about cause.
Anomaly A point believed to have been generated by a different process. This carries a causal claim that “outlier” does not.
Novelty A new point of a kind absent from training, where the training set is assumed clean. The one-class setting.
Out‑of‑distribution The deep-learning framing: the input comes from a different distribution than the training data. Distinct from “unusual but in‑distribution”.
Distribution shift The whole distribution moves rather than individual points being odd. Covariate shift moves p(x)p(x)p(x), label shift moves p(y)p(y)p(y), concept drift moves p(y∣x)p(y \mid x)p(y∣x).
Extreme value A genuine tail event of the true distribution — not an error. The province of extreme value theory, not of deletion.
Leverage point Regression-specific: an outlier in xxx, measured through the hat matrix H=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤H = X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}H=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤. A large residual is an outlier in yyy, which is a different thing.
The distinction worth making unprompted

Outlier detection assumes the data you have is contaminated and asks you to find the bad points inside it. Novelty detection assumes what you have is clean and asks you to judge new points against it. Scikit-learn enforces exactly this split in its API, and it decides which algorithms are even applicable.

The dangerous case

A high-leverage point with a small residual. It drags the fit towards itself and then sits neatly on the line it moved. Nothing about it looks wrong. Cook's distance measures the influence that residuals hide.

If you have done inference, you have done this

Scoring by −log⁡p(x)-\log p(x)−logp(x) under a fitted model is a likelihood ratio test against a diffuse alternative. Mahalanobis distance with a χp2\chi^2_pχp2​ threshold is that test written out for a Gaussian. Most of what follows is the same idea with progressively weaker assumptions about ppp — and progressively less to say about how confident the answer is.

Three supervision regimes, and why it is the first question

This decides the method family before any other consideration does.

RegimeLabels availableWhat it actually is
SupervisedBoth classes labelled Not anomaly detection at all — it is extremely imbalanced classification. If you have enough labelled anomalies, use a classifier and say so. The catch is real, though: it can only find anomalies that resemble the ones already labelled.
Semi-supervised
(one-class)		 Clean normal data only The most common realistic case. Learn a model of normal and score deviation from it: one-class SVM, Deep SVDD, autoencoder reconstruction error, a density model.
UnsupervisedNone, and the data is contaminated Assume anomalies are rare and different, and find them in the data as given. Isolation forest, LOF, robust covariance. Requires a contamination assumption, which is usually the weakest link in the entire pipeline.
The second question, which matters as much

Where did the labels come from? If they were produced by the detector currently in production, then evaluating against them measures agreement with the incumbent rather than correctness — and a genuinely better method will score worse. This is not a hypothetical; it is the normal state of affairs.

And the third

What does a miss cost relative to a false alarm? In fraud or safety they differ by orders of magnitude, and the answer fixes the operating point. Without it, every threshold is arbitrary, however carefully it was computed.

Three kinds of anomaly

The structural taxonomy from Chandola, Banerjee and Kumar (2009), still the one everybody cites. It matters because the three need different machinery, and the third is the one that gets missed.

Point

A single observation is unusual on its own. The easy case, and the one most tutorials stop at. A robust univariate rule finds it.

Contextual

Unusual given its context, unremarkable otherwise. Thirty degrees is ordinary in July and an anomaly in January. You have to define the context, which is a modelling decision rather than a detection one.

Collective

No individual point is unusual, but the sequence is. An ECG where every reading is in range and the rhythm is wrong. The hardest of the three, and what sequence models are actually for.

Demonstrated on page 4

The time-series page carries one of each in a single series and scores it three ways. A robust rule on the raw values finds the point anomaly and nothing else; a rule on the decomposition residual finds the contextual one too; only the matrix profile finds the collective one. The average precisions come out at roughly 0.070.070.07, 0.900.900.90 and 0.960.960.96 — on the same data, with the same labels.

What you need before starting

Less than you might think, and it is worth being precise about which parts do the work.

  • Quantiles and the median. Nearly all the robustness on page 1 comes from replacing a mean with a median. The breakdown point — the fraction of the sample you can corrupt before an estimator becomes arbitrary — is 000 for the mean and 1/21/21/2 for the median, and that single fact explains most of the page.
  • Covariance, and its inverse. Mahalanobis distance is Euclidean distance after whitening: d2=(x−μ)⊤Σ−1(x−μ)d^2 = (x-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)d2=(x−μ)⊤Σ−1(x−μ). Under multivariate normality d2∼χp2d^2 \sim \chi^2_pd2∼χp2​, which is where a principled threshold comes from.
  • Nearest neighbours. Enough to see why “far from its neighbours” stops discriminating when the dimension is large.
  • Precision and recall. Page 5 is entirely about these two and their denominators. If you can say what sits under each one, you can see every trap on that page coming.

And the second

Under heavy imbalance the false-positive rate is nearly useless, because its denominator is the enormous normal class:

FPR=FPFP+TN,precision=TPTP+FP.\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN}},\qquad \mathrm{precision}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}}.FPR=FP+TNFP​,precision=TP+FPTP​.

At one anomaly in a thousand, an operationally fatal number of false alarms barely moves the first quantity and destroys the second. That is the whole reason ROC curves look good on problems where nothing works.

The honest summary, before the detail

Start here

A robust univariate look at every variable, then an isolation forest. Two lines of code, few parameters, and a baseline that is hard to beat on tabular data. Escalate only when it fails, and be able to say how you knew it failed.

On deep methods

They earn their place when the input is high-dimensional and unstructured — images, audio, raw traces — where representation learning does what hand-crafted features cannot. On tabular data of moderate dimension, the evidence that they beat an isolation forest is weak, and several independent benchmark studies find well-tuned classical methods competitive or better.

On the numbers you will be shown

Ground truth is usually missing, the classes are wildly imbalanced, and some of the evaluation protocols in common use are themselves broken. Treat a reported F1 above 0.90.90.9 on a time-series benchmark as a question rather than a result — page 5 explains what to ask.
