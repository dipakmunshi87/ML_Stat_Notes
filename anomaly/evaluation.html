The methods are
the easy part

Ground truth is usually missing, the classes are wildly imbalanced, the labels were often produced by the detector you are trying to replace, and some of the protocols in common use are broken. Almost every anomaly-detection result you will be shown is compromised by something on this page.

The denominators

Why one curve flatters and the other does not

Start with the one to never report. At 0.1%0.1\%0.1% contamination, predicting “normal” everywhere gives an accuracy of 99.9%99.9\%99.9%. Accuracy is a statement about the majority class and the majority class is not the question.

The ROC curve plots recall against the false-positive rate. Precision-recall plots precision against recall. The whole difference is in what sits underneath:

FPR=FPFP+TN⏟all normal points,precision=TPTP+FP⏟everything you flagged.\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\underbrace{\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN}}_{\text{all normal points}}}, \qquad \mathrm{precision}=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\underbrace{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}}_{\text{everything you flagged}}}.FPR=all normal pointsFP+TN​​FP​,precision=everything you flaggedTP+FP​​TP​.

The false-positive rate divides by the entire normal class. With a million normal points and a thousand anomalies, ten thousand false alarms — an unusable detector, ten times more noise than signal — is a false-positive rate of 0.010.010.01. The ROC curve barely notices. Precision notices immediately, because it divides by what you actually flagged.

There is also a fixed point worth knowing: the baseline for average precision is the contamination rate itself,

APrandom=number of anomaliesn,\mathrm{AP}_{\text{random}}=\frac{\text{number of anomalies}}{n},APrandom​=nnumber of anomalies​,

whereas random ROC-AUC is 0.50.50.5 whatever the imbalance. So an AP of 0.30.30.3 at 0.2%0.2\%0.2% contamination is a factor of 150150150 better than chance, and an ROC-AUC of 0.890.890.89 is a factor of 1.81.81.8. The second number is less impressive and more misleading.

The answer to give

“Precision-recall under heavy imbalance, because the false-positive rate has the whole normal class in its denominator and hides an operationally fatal number of false alarms. Precision does not.” It is a standard question and that is the whole answer.

Precision at kkk, which is usually the real metric

If an analyst can review fifty alerts a day, then precision at fifty is the business metric and everything else is a proxy for it. It needs no threshold, it needs no calibration, and it is directly interpretable: of the fifty things we looked at, how many were real.

Ask “what is the review budget?” and you have answered the threshold question better than most.

On F1

F1 requires a threshold, so it measures your threshold choice and your ranking together and does not tell you which one moved. Report AP or precision at kkk for the ranking, and report the operating point separately with its actual counts. And never report F1 on a time series without saying whether point adjustment was used.

The factor-above-chance framing is the most useful way to report AP to people who do not work with it: “average precision of 0.30.30.3 against a base rate of 0.0020.0020.002” is immediately meaningful, where 0.30.30.3 on its own sounds poor.

Thresholds

Where the number should come from

Almost every method in this set returns a score with no natural scale, so the threshold is a separate decision and deserves to be made explicitly. There are four defensible sources, roughly in order of preference.

Cost. If a miss costs CFNC_{\text{FN}}CFN​ and a false alarm costs CFPC_{\text{FP}}CFP​, then flagging is worth it when the posterior odds beat the cost ratio:

Pr⁡(anomaly∣x)>CFPCFP+CFN.\Pr(\text{anomaly}\mid x)>\frac{C_{\text{FP}}}{C_{\text{FP}}+C_{\text{FN}}}.Pr(anomaly∣x)>CFP​+CFN​CFP​​.

State the ratio out loud. In fraud or safety the two differ by orders of magnitude, and once that is written down the operating point follows rather than being chosen.

Capacity. If the queue can absorb fifty alerts a day, take the top fifty. This needs no calibration and cannot be gamed, and it is the honest answer whenever a human consumes the output.

A distributional threshold. Mahalanobis distance against χp2\chi^2_pχp2​ from page 1; standardised Kalman innovations from page 4; or a generalised Pareto fit to the tail of the score distribution, which lets you say “this score is exceeded once per ten thousand observations” with an interval attached. Far better founded than taking the top one per cent because one per cent sounded reasonable.

A contamination quantile. The default in most libraries, and the weakest: it requires knowing the anomaly rate, which is usually the thing you were trying to find out. If you use it, test the sensitivity of your conclusions to it.

The sentence that fixes most threshold arguments

“How many alerts per day can be reviewed, and what does a miss cost relative to a false alarm?” Everything else is arithmetic once those two numbers exist, and the discussion usually reveals that nobody had asked.

Two thresholds, not one

A useful pattern: a high threshold that pages someone immediately, and a lower one that adds to a review queue. It matches the cost structure better than a single cut and it is easy to explain to whoever owns the queue.

The mistake to avoid

Tuning the threshold, the contamination parameter, kkk in LOF or the autoencoder bottleneck while looking at test results. This is acute in anomaly detection because there is usually so little labelled data that the test set is the validation set, and every parameter ends up quietly fitted to it. Hold something back, or report the number as what it is: optimistic.

The posterior-odds form assumes a calibrated probability, which almost nothing here produces. In practice the cost ratio fixes the rank at which you cut rather than a probability, which is why capacity and cost usually collapse into the same decision.

The labels

Where the ground truth came from

This is the failure that invalidates results most often and gets mentioned least.

  • Labels from the incumbent detector. If the “ground truth” is the set of alerts the current rule raised and an analyst confirmed, then an evaluation against it measures agreement with the incumbent. A genuinely better method — one that finds anomaly classes the old rule never raised — will score worse. Ask how the labels were obtained before you believe any comparison.
  • Labels that are late and incomplete. Fraud is confirmed weeks later; equipment faults are confirmed at the next inspection. The unlabelled points are a mixture of “normal” and “not yet discovered”, and treating them as negatives systematically understates precision.
  • Contamination in the clean set. Every semi-supervised method assumes the training data is anomaly-free. It rarely is. Check, and if you cannot check, prefer a method with a stated breakdown point.
  • The feedback loop. What the detector flags gets reviewed, reviews become labels, labels train the next detector. Review a random sample of unflagged data on purpose, and budget for it, or the system will converge on agreeing with itself.
Stability, and why benchmark gaps are usually noise

The effective sample size of an anomaly benchmark is the number of anomalies, not the number of rows. Thirty anomalies in a hundred thousand records is a sample of thirty, and the standard error on any recall estimate is correspondingly enormous.

So repeat across seeds and splits before believing a ranking. The next slide measures the spread on a concrete case: the run-to-run standard deviation of average precision for a single method is about 0.080.080.08, which means a reported gap of 0.030.030.03 between two methods on one dataset is not a result. Gaps of that size are exactly what most benchmark tables are built on.

What to ask of any reported result

How were the labels obtained? What is the base rate? Is that AP or ROC-AUC, and what is chance? How was the threshold set, and was anything tuned on the test set? Over how many seeds, and what was the spread?

Five questions. Most reported results do not survive the first two.

The incumbent-labels problem is worth raising unprompted in any discussion of deploying a new detector: it predicts, correctly, that the new method will look worse on the existing evaluation and that this is not evidence against it.

The last row is the one that catches people out. If the answer to “how many labelled anomalies do you have” is “four thousand”, then this is not an anomaly detection problem and treating it as one throws away the labels.

The catalogue

Failure modes that recur

In the estimator

Masking — the outliers inflate the scale and hide each other.

Swamping — a few extreme points distort the fit so normal points get flagged.

Corrupted covariance — the anomalies write the matrix used to detect them.

Fix: robust estimators with a stated breakdown point.

In the model

The autoencoder that reconstructs everything — too much capacity, or a code no narrower than the data.

Likelihood in high dimension — dominated by low-level statistics, not semantics.

Representation collapse — the network makes the score easy by destroying what it is scoring.

Irrelevant features — the signal is in two of a hundred coordinates and every method searches all hundred.

In the evaluation

Accuracy — a statement about the majority class.

ROC under imbalance — hides a fatal false-alarm count.

Point adjustment — lets a coin flip beat a detector.

Temporal leakage — shuffling, or fitting the scaler on the whole series.

Incumbent labels — measures agreement, not correctness.

Tuning on the test set — unavoidable-feeling, and still wrong.

And one that is nobody's fault

Concept drift. Yesterday's normal is not today's. A deployed detector needs monitoring and a retraining policy defined in advance, not a training run. Track the score distribution rather than the alert count, keep a frozen baseline running alongside, and review a random sample of unflagged data so the feedback loop does not close.

The pattern across all of them

Every entry in the left-hand column is the same mistake: a quantity estimated from data that contains the thing it is meant to find. Every entry in the right-hand column is the same mistake too: a denominator chosen so that the answer looks better than it is. Recognising those two shapes is most of the skill.

If asked for the single most common cause of an anomaly-detection project failing, the honest answer is not a bad method. It is an evaluation that could not have detected the method being bad.

The strongest thing you can say about this subject

Not which method you would use, but which question you would ask first, and how you would know whether the answer was real. That applies here more than almost anywhere else in machine learning, because the ground truth is usually missing, the classes are wildly imbalanced, and some of the evaluation protocols in common use are themselves unreliable.

Aggarwal really is the one book. It is also unusual in the literature for being explicit about where each method family fails, which is the part that is hard to get from papers.
