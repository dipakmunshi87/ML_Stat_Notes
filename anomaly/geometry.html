Part 2 of 5 · distance, density, isolation

Stop asking where
the middle is

Page 1 assumed normal data has a centre and a spread. Drop that and two quite different ideas appear: compare each point's neighbourhood with its neighbours' neighbourhoods, or give up on modelling normality altogether and ask how few random cuts it takes to separate the point from everything else.

The two neighbourhood scores

Distance to the neighbours, and distance relative to them

The simplest neighbourhood score is the distance to the kkkth nearest neighbour,

skNN(x)=d ⁣(x, x(k)),s_{k\text{NN}}(x)=d\!\left(x,\,x_{(k)}\right),skNN​(x)=d(x,x(k)​),

with x(k)x_{(k)}x(k)​ the kkkth closest point. It is a stubbornly good baseline and it has one clear failure: it is global. A point sitting in a sparse but perfectly legitimate region of the data gets the same score as a point sitting nowhere at all.

The local outlier factor repairs exactly that. Define the reachability distance from xxx to a neighbour ooo as

dreachk(x,o)=max⁡ ⁣{ dk(o),  d(x,o) },d_{\text{reach}}^{k}(x,o)=\max\!\left\{\,d_k(o),\;d(x,o)\,\right\},dreachk​(x,o)=max{dk​(o),d(x,o)},

where dk(o)d_k(o)dk​(o) is ooo's own kkkth-neighbour distance — a smoothing device that stops a very close pair from producing an unstable density. The local reachability density is the reciprocal of the average of those,

lrdk(x)=(1∣Nk(x)∣∑o∈Nk(x)dreachk(x,o))−1,\mathrm{lrd}_k(x)=\left(\frac{1}{|N_k(x)|}\sum_{o\in N_k(x)}d_{\text{reach}}^{k}(x,o)\right)^{-1},lrdk​(x)=​∣Nk​(x)∣1​o∈Nk​(x)∑​dreachk​(x,o)​−1,

and LOF is the ratio of the neighbours' densities to the point's own:

LOFk(x)=1∣Nk(x)∣∑o∈Nk(x)lrdk(o)lrdk(x).\mathrm{LOF}_k(x)=\frac{1}{|N_k(x)|}\sum_{o\in N_k(x)}\frac{\mathrm{lrd}_k(o)}{\mathrm{lrd}_k(x)}.LOFk​(x)=∣Nk​(x)∣1​o∈Nk​(x)∑​lrdk​(x)lrdk​(o)​.

The ratio is the whole point. A value near 111 means the point is as densely surrounded as its neighbours are, wherever in the data it happens to sit. Substantially above 111 means it is not.

Why the ratio changes everything

Absolute density is not comparable across a dataset with structure. Relative density is. That single substitution is the difference between a score that says “this region is sparse” and a score that says “this point does not belong to the region it is in”.

What LOF costs you

It is notoriously sensitive to kkk. Too small and it picks up sampling noise; too large and the neighbourhood spans two genuine clusters and the ratio becomes meaningless. There is no principled way to set it without labels, which is an honest weakness — move the slider on the next slide and watch it matter.

It is also O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) as written, and the score has no calibrated scale: 1.41.41.4 is not a probability of anything.

The relatives

DBSCAN clusters and labels the leftovers as noise; those are your anomalies, free of charge if you were clustering anyway. It needs one global ε\varepsilonε, so it fails on exactly the varying-density data LOF handles.

Kernel density estimation scores by estimated density directly. Transparent, and fine below about five dimensions, after which bandwidth selection becomes the entire problem.

Scikit-learn's LocalOutlierFactor needs novelty=True before it will score unseen points — the outlier-versus-novelty distinction made concrete in an API.

A different idea entirely

Do not model normal. Isolate.

Liu, Ting and Zhou's observation in 2008 was that anomalies have two properties — there are few of them and they are different — and that both make them easy to separate. So separate them: pick a random feature, pick a random split inside its observed range, recurse. Anomalies fall out of the partition early.

Score by the average depth at which a point becomes its own leaf, normalised by the depth you would expect anyway. With c(n)c(n)c(n) the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree,

c(n)=2Hn−1−2(n−1)n,Hm=ln⁡m+γ,c(n)=2H_{n-1}-\frac{2(n-1)}{n},\qquad H_m=\ln m+\gamma,c(n)=2Hn−1​−n2(n−1)​,Hm​=lnm+γ,

the published score is

s(x)=2− E[h(x)] / c(n).s(x)=2^{-\,\mathbb{E}[h(x)]\,/\,c(n)}.s(x)=2−E[h(x)]/c(n).

It runs from 000 to 111: near 111 means isolated in very few cuts, near 000 means buried deep in the bulk, and 0.50.50.5 is the ambiguous middle. Anything much above 0.60.60.6 deserves a look.

What makes it the right default for tabular data is the cost. There are no distances to compute, so nothing degrades with dimension the way page 8 describes. Each tree sees a subsample of 256256256 points regardless of how much data you have, so it is linear in nnn with a small constant, and the only parameters are the number of trees and the subsample size, neither of which is sensitive.

Why subsampling helps rather than hurts

Counter-intuitively, small subsamples improve the score. A large sample lets dense normal regions swamp the tree, and the anomalies you care about get hidden among near-duplicates — swamping and masking again, in a new costume. Drawing 256256256 points per tree keeps the partitions coarse enough for isolation to mean something.

The blind spot

Every cut is perpendicular to an axis. So the method sees “extreme in some single feature” very well, and “odd only in combination” poorly. Its score contours come out rectangular, with ghost regions of low score in the corners where no data lives.

The extended isolation forest replaces the axis-aligned cut with a random hyperplane — a random direction and a random offset — which removes the artefact at no real cost. The demonstration on the next slide draws both.

The streaming cousin

Robust random cut forest scores by how much a new point increases the model's description complexity, and supports insertion and deletion. It is what you reach for when the data arrives as a stream and the model must move with it.

γ≈0.5772\gamma\approx 0.5772γ≈0.5772 is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. The default subsample of 256256256 is from the original paper and has held up remarkably well; the number of trees matters less than people expect, with 100100100 being plenty.

Combining them

Ensembles, and the normalisation nobody does

Averaging several detectors is reliably better than choosing one, for the ordinary reason: the errors are less correlated than the signal. This is one of the few places in anomaly detection where a free improvement is genuinely available.

The catch is that the scores are not comparable. A LOF ratio lives near 111, an isolation score lives in [0,1][0,1][0,1] with a meaningful midpoint, a kkk-NN distance is in the units of your features and a Mahalanobis distance is a χ2\chi^2χ2 variate. Averaging them raw lets whichever has the largest variance decide the answer. Normalise first, either by standardising

s~i=si−med⁡(s)1.4826MAD⁡(s)\tilde s_i=\frac{s_i-\operatorname{med}(s)}{1.4826\operatorname{MAD}(s)}s~i​=1.4826MAD(s)si​−med(s)​

— robustly, since the whole point is that some scores are extreme — or by replacing each score with its rank, which throws away magnitude and keeps only order. Rank averaging is the safer default and is what most libraries mean by score combination.

Feature bagging is the same idea over random subspaces: fit the detector on a random subset of features, repeat, combine. It is the standard high-dimensional trick and it is on the next slide for a reason.

A sensible default ensemble

Isolation forest, kkk-NN distance and LOF, rank-averaged. Three cheap detectors with genuinely different failure modes: one that ignores distance, one that is global, one that is local. If all three agree, you have something.

Maximum rather than mean

Taking the maximum of the normalised scores rather than the mean is sometimes better, because an anomaly need only be anomalous in one view. It also multiplies your false alarms by the number of detectors, so it belongs with a review budget you have actually measured.

What an ensemble cannot fix

If every member shares the same assumption, combining them combines the blind spot too. Three distance-based detectors in forty dimensions is one mistake made three times.

Aggarwal's Outlier Analysis has the careful treatment of score normalisation and combination; it is the part of the subject with the least folklore and the most actual theory.

High dimension

Where “far away” stops meaning anything

Beyer and colleagues' 1999 result is the one to know. Under mild conditions, as the dimension ppp grows the nearest and farthest points from a query converge to the same distance:

dmax⁡−dmin⁡dmin⁡  ⟶  0.\frac{d_{\max}-d_{\min}}{d_{\min}}\;\longrightarrow\;0.dmin​dmax​−dmin​​⟶0.

The mechanism is easy to see. Squared Euclidean distance is a sum of ppp independent contributions, so its mean grows like ppp and its standard deviation like p\sqrt{p}p​; the relative spread falls as 1/p1/\sqrt{p}1/p​. Every point becomes equally far from every other, and “far from its neighbours” stops discriminating.

Two consequences follow, and the second is worse than the first.

  • kkk-NN distance and LOF degrade because distance is their score, and the signal in two informative coordinates is swamped by the accumulated variance of the uninformative ones.
  • Anomalies increasingly live in a subspace: normal in every single marginal, and anomalous only in some projection. Screening variable by variable cannot find them, and neither can a method that insists on using the whole space at once.

It is worth being precise about isolation forests here, because they are often oversold. They are immune to distance concentration, since they never compute a distance. They are not immune to irrelevant features: a cut is made on a random axis, so if two coordinates out of a hundred carry the signal, ninety-eight cuts in a hundred are made on noise. The demonstration on the next slide shows all three single-space detectors failing together.

The remedy is not a better detector. It is to stop searching the full space: subspace and feature-bagging ensembles, which fit the detector on random small subsets of features and combine the ranks; angle-based methods, since angles concentrate more slowly than distances; and — the one that actually works when the input is images or raw traces — learning a representation first and detecting in that, which is page 3.

The volume picture

Almost all of the volume of a ppp-dimensional ball is within a shell of thickness ∼1/p\sim 1/p∼1/p of its surface, and almost all of a Gaussian's mass sits near the sphere of radius p\sqrt{p}p​. High-dimensional “clouds” are not clouds; they are thin shells. Intuition trained on two dimensions is not merely imprecise here, it is the wrong shape.

The trap this creates in practice

A detector that works on a curated ten-feature table often fails outright on the same problem with four hundred engineered features, and the diagnosis looks like a bug. It is not a bug. Cut the dimension, or change method family.

The shell facts are standard and worth being able to derive: the ratio of the volume of a ball of radius 1−ϵ1-\epsilon1−ϵ to one of radius 111 is (1−ϵ)p(1-\epsilon)^p(1−ϵ)p, which goes to zero for any fixed ϵ\epsilonϵ as ppp grows.

Carried forward

What to take to the next page

Local beats global when density varies

If the data has regions of genuinely different density — and real data usually does — a global distance score will spend its budget on the sparse-but-normal parts. LOF exists for this and nothing else does it as directly.

Isolation forest is the right default

Linear time, two insensitive parameters, no distance computation, and it survives dimension. Start there on tabular data, and use the oblique variant if the anomalies might be defined by a combination of features rather than by any single one.

Count your dimensions before you choose

Past a few tens of features, distance-based scores are measuring noise. Reduce, bag over subspaces, or learn a representation. Do not simply add features and hope.
