Part 3 of 5 · reconstruction and generative scores

Learn what normal
looks like, then fail to copy

Fit something that can reproduce normal data and nothing else, then score each point by how badly it survives the round trip. It is the most natural idea in the subject, it is what makes deep anomaly detection possible at all, and it has a failure mode that destroys it silently: a model with enough capacity reproduces the anomalies too.

Residual scores

Project, reconstruct, measure what is left

Take the leading qqq principal components, with VqV_qVq​ their matrix, and reconstruct each centred point by projecting onto them and back:

x^=μ+VqVq⊤(x−μ),s(x)=∥x−x^∥.\hat x=\mu+V_qV_q^{\top}(x-\mu),\qquad s(x)=\left\|x-\hat x\right\|.x^=μ+Vq​Vq⊤​(x−μ),s(x)=∥x−x^∥.

The score is the part of the point that the subspace cannot represent. The assumption is explicit and unusually easy to state: normal data lies close to a qqq-dimensional linear subspace.

Two things follow. First, this is Mahalanobis distance's cousin — both are quadratic forms in the whitened coordinates, and PCA residual error is what you get by keeping only the smallest eigen-directions. Second, when the data lies on a curved manifold the residual is large for perfectly normal points at the ends of the curve, and the score stops working. That is the entire motivation for autoencoders, and the next slide measures it.

Robust PCA solves a different and rather beautiful version of the problem. Decompose the data matrix into a low-rank part and a sparse part,

X=L+S,min⁡L,S  ∥L∥∗+λ∥S∥1,X=L+S,\qquad \min_{L,S}\;\|L\|_{*}+\lambda\|S\|_1,X=L+S,L,Smin​∥L∥∗​+λ∥S∥1​,

with ∥⋅∥∗\|\cdot\|_{*}∥⋅∥∗​ the nuclear norm. The sparse component is the anomalies — they are not scored and thresholded, they are separated out as part of the fit. It is convex, it has recovery guarantees, and where it applies it is hard to beat.

The autoencoder, in one line

Replace the linear encoder and decoder with networks. Where PCA finds the best qqq-dimensional plane, an autoencoder with a qqq-dimensional code finds the best qqq-dimensional surface. With linear activations and a squared loss it recovers the PCA subspace exactly, which is the right way to remember what it is doing.

The failure that matters

Nothing in the training objective asks the network to reconstruct anomalies badly. It asks it to reconstruct the training data well. If the architecture is capable enough — in particular if the code is not genuinely narrower than the data's intrinsic dimension — the map it learns approaches the identity, and the identity reconstructs everything perfectly.

Mitigations: size the bottleneck deliberately, use a denoising objective so the network must learn the data manifold rather than a copy, add memory-augmented or sparsity constraints, and — always — check the ratio of anomalous to normal error rather than the loss.

And the quieter failure

Training data is assumed clean. If the anomalies are in the training set, the network learns to reconstruct them as part of the job. This is the semi-supervised assumption of page 1 in a new costume, and it is the more common of the two failures in practice.

The linear-activation result is worth being able to state: a linear autoencoder trained with squared error spans the same subspace as PCA, though not with orthonormal axes unless you constrain it.

Boundaries instead of residuals

Enclose the normal data, and call the outside anomalous

The one-class support vector machine of Schölkopf and colleagues separates the data from the origin in a feature space with the largest possible margin, solving

What these assume is clean training data, and they are not gracefully robust to violation — a contaminated training set drags the boundary outwards and there is no median anywhere in the formulation to stop it. They also scale poorly, roughly between O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) and O(n3)O(n^3)O(n3), and they are sensitive to both ν\nuν and the kernel width.

Boundary or residual?

A residual score says how far off the manifold a point is. A boundary score says inside or outside, with a margin. If you need a ranked list of the hundred most suspicious records, the residual is more directly useful; if you need a decision with a stated training-time false-positive rate, ν\nuν gives you one.

The trivial-solution family

Deep SVDD's collapse is one instance of a pattern worth recognising. Any method that learns a representation and scores in it can make the score easy by destroying the representation. Contrastive and self-supervised anomaly methods all have a version of this, and the architectural restrictions in their papers are usually there to prevent it rather than to improve accuracy.

A familiar shape

The one-class SVM is a level set of a kernel density estimate in disguise: with a Gaussian kernel the decision function is a weighted sum of kernels centred on the support vectors. It is page 2's kernel density estimate with most of the data thrown away, which is why it scales better at prediction time and no better at fitting time.

The ν\nuν-property is the thing to remember about one-class SVMs: it bounds both the fraction of outliers and the fraction of support vectors. It is the closest thing in the method family to a calibrated knob.

Probabilistic scores

Score by likelihood — and why that can fail

If you have a model of the density, the score writes itself:

s(x)=−log⁡pθ(x).s(x)=-\log p_\theta(x).s(x)=−logpθ​(x).

Gaussian mixtures give it cheaply and interpretably, and break in high dimension or when a mixture component gets captured by the anomalies. Normalising flows and autoregressive models give exact likelihoods; energy-based models give them up to a constant. Variational autoencoders give a bound, and the reconstruction probability is a perfectly reasonable score.

The trouble is that in high dimension the likelihood of a deep generative model is not measuring what you want. Nalisnick and colleagues showed in 2019 that a model trained on CIFAR-10 assigns higher likelihood to SVHN images than to its own training data. The density is dominated by low-level statistics — smoothness, complexity, how compressible the image is — rather than by anything semantic. A flat grey image is enormously likely under almost any image model, and it is not a plausible photograph.

There is also a purely geometric reason, worth knowing separately. In ppp dimensions a Gaussian puts almost none of its mass near its own mode; the mass sits in a shell at radius ≈p\approx\sqrt{p}≈p​. So the most likely point is not a typical point, and testing whether a sample is typical is not the same as asking whether its density is high.

The result to be able to cite

Nalisnick et al., Do Deep Generative Models Know What They Don't Know?, ICLR 2019. Higher likelihood for out-of-distribution data than for in-distribution data, in a well-trained model. It is the single most useful thing to know in this corner of the subject, because it separates people who have read the literature from people who have read a tutorial.

What to do instead

Likelihood ratios. Score log⁡pθ(x)−log⁡p0(x)\log p_\theta(x)-\log p_0(x)logpθ​(x)−logp0​(x) against a background model that captures the nuisance statistics. The complexity term cancels.

Typicality tests. Ask whether −log⁡pθ(x)-\log p_\theta(x)−logpθ​(x) is close to the model's expected entropy, rather than whether it is large. Two-sided rather than one-sided.

Input complexity corrections. Divide out a generic compressor's estimate of the input's complexity — crude, and effective.

The low-dimensional caveat on the caveat

None of this is a reason to distrust likelihood scores in five dimensions with a fitted mixture. The failure is specific to high-dimensional deep models where the density is dominated by structure you do not care about. Know the boundary of the result as well as the result.

The typicality framing is due to Nalisnick's follow-up work and to the information-theoretic literature on typical sets; the shell fact is the same one from page 2 and is worth linking explicitly when explaining this.

Deep methods, honestly

When the network earns its place

The case for deep anomaly detection is not that networks are better estimators. It is that when the input is high-dimensional and unstructured — images, audio, spectrograms, raw sensor traces — there are no hand-crafted features to give a classical method, and representation learning supplies them. That is a real and important argument, and it is narrower than it is usually stated.

  • Self-supervised approaches are currently the strongest family for images. Train on a pretext task — predicting an applied rotation, contrastive matching — and use the model's confidence as the score. They work because the pretext task forces semantic structure into the representation in a way reconstruction does not.
  • GAN-based methods (AnoGAN, GANomaly) score by how hard a point is to generate. Conceptually appealing; training instability makes them a poor first choice and they have largely been overtaken.
  • Pretrained-feature methods are the unglamorous winner in practice: take a frozen network trained on something else, embed your data, and run kkk-NN distance in the embedding. Frequently beats purpose-built deep detectors, and it is three lines of code.
The summary worth saying out loud

On tabular data of moderate dimension, the evidence that deep methods beat an isolation forest or kkk-NN is weak. Several independent benchmarking studies have found well-tuned classical methods competitive with or better than deep ones, and the deep methods cost more to train, more to operate and more to explain. Saying this shows judgement. Claiming deep learning is always better shows the opposite.

The operational cost nobody budgets

An alert has to be explained to whoever acts on it. “Reconstruction error 0.420.420.42” is not an explanation. A rule on two features is. If your detector feeds a human review queue, interpretability is a functional requirement rather than a preference, and it is worth losing some average precision for.

The sizing question to ask first

How many labelled anomalies exist? Below a handful, you are in the unsupervised regime and should stay classical. Above a few hundred, this is imbalanced classification and you should train a classifier. Deep one-class methods live in the awkward middle, with plenty of clean normal data and almost no labelled anomalies.

Ruff and colleagues' unifying review of shallow and deep anomaly detection is the best single entry point to this comparison, and it is notably even-handed about where deep methods do not help.

Carried forward

What to take to the next page

Check the ratio, not the loss

A reconstruction model with a small training loss can have no discriminating power at all. The number that matters is the anomalous error divided by the normal error, and you can compute it on a handful of known cases.

The bottleneck is the assumption

Choosing the code dimension is choosing the intrinsic dimension of normal data. Too wide and you learn the identity; too narrow and normal points look anomalous. It is a modelling decision, not a hyperparameter to tune on the test set.

Likelihood is not a score in high dimension

For deep generative models, use ratios against a background model or typicality tests rather than raw density. In low dimension with a fitted mixture, none of this applies.

Where next

Page 4 adds time. Most real anomaly work is temporal, the three kinds of anomaly from the introduction become concretely different problems, and two of the standard evaluation protocols turn out to be broken.

The PCA error ratio of about two is the useful contrast: it is not that linear PCA has no signal, it is that the signal is swamped by the legitimately large residuals at the ends of the curved manifold.
