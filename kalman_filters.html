The Kalman filter,
from the ground up

A recipe for the best running estimate of something you can’t measure directly, from a stream of noisy measurements — and the Extended Kalman Filter, which does the same when the world is nonlinear.

Invented by Rudolf E. Kálmán around 1960 and famous for guiding Apollo to the Moon, the Kalman filter is one of the most-used algorithms in all of engineering and data analysis. This deck builds it from scratch: the intuition, the full derivations, worked numbers, live animations of real filter runs, how it relates to least squares / Bayesian filtering / smoothing, what it is good at and what defeats it — and then the nonlinear EKF.

What it does

Fuses a model of how things change with measurements to track a hidden state and its uncertainty.

Why it’s special

For linear-Gaussian problems it is the optimal estimator — and it’s recursive, so it runs in real time on a stream.

What you’ll get

Every equation derived, six live demos, runnable code, and honest limits.

Estimate a hidden state from noisy sensors

You care about a quantity that changes over time — the position and velocity of a car, the true price behind noisy ticks, the temperature of a reactor, the pose of a robot. You cannot see it directly. You get noisy, partial measurements, one after another. You want, at every instant, the best estimate of the hidden state and an honest sense of how sure you are.

Two hard facts make this interesting:

  • Every measurement is noisy. Trust one blindly and your estimate jitters.
  • You have a model of how the state evolves (a car keeps moving; temperature drifts slowly). Trust the model blindly and you ignore reality.

The Kalman filter is the principled way to blend the two — weighting the model prediction and the new measurement by how much each can be trusted, and carrying a full uncertainty along the way. It does this recursively: it only needs the previous estimate and the new measurement, never the whole history — which is why it runs forever in constant memory, in real time.

The one-sentence version. Predict where the state should be from your model; then nudge that prediction toward the new measurement, by an amount set by their relative uncertainties. Repeat.

The common shape: a quantity evolving in time, observed noisily and indirectly, needed in real time with error bars. Whenever you see that shape, a Kalman filter (or one of its cousins) is probably the right first tool.

Everything rests on two equations

Write the hidden state as a vector x (e.g. position and velocity). The linear-Gaussian state-space model has a process (dynamics) equation and a measurement equation:

xk = F xk−1 + wk,   wk ~ 𝒩(0, Q)
zk = H xk + vk,   vk ~ 𝒩(0, R) F = how the state evolves · H = how the state maps to what you measure · Q,R = process & measurement noise

Read it plainly: F pushes the state one step forward (a car’s new position = old position + velocity×time); w is the slop in that model (unmodelled acceleration). H extracts what your sensor actually sees (maybe only position, not velocity); v is sensor noise. The matrices Q and R are the two dials you tune: how much you distrust the model vs the measurement.

Concrete example — tracking a moving object with a position sensor. State x = (p, v). Then F = [[1, Δt],[0,1]] (new position = position + velocity·Δt; velocity unchanged) and H = [1, 0] (you measure position only). The filter will recover the unmeasured velocity too — the payoff of having a model.

Why two Gaussians multiply to one

The whole filter runs on a single fact: the product of two Gaussians is a Gaussian, and its mean is the precision-weighted average of the two. This is the “fuse two noisy opinions” step, and it is the update. In 1-D, if the model predicts 𝒩(a, σa²) and the measurement says 𝒩(b, σb²), the fused estimate is:

μ = (σb² a + σa² b)/(σa²+σb²),   1/σ² = 1/σa² + 1/σb² precisions (1/σ²) add; the mean leans toward whichever opinion is more certain; the result is always tighter than either input

Drag the measurement and its noise and watch the two Gaussians fuse into a sharper posterior — this little picture is the beating heart of the Kalman update:

Grey = model prediction, gold = measurement, blue = fused posterior. Confident measurement (small σb) → posterior jumps to it; noisy measurement → posterior stays near the model.

Predict, then update — derived

Take the scalar case (state is one number x, model xk=xk−1+w, measurement z=x+v). One cycle has two moves.

Predict — push the belief through the model
x̂− = x̂,   P− = P + Q the mean coasts forward; the variance grows by Q because the model is imperfect
Update — fuse in the measurement (the Gaussian product)

The prediction is 𝒩(x̂−, P−); the measurement likelihood is 𝒩(z, R). Multiply and match to a Gaussian using the fusion formula above. Writing the result as “prediction plus a correction”:

K = P− / (P− + R)  (the Kalman gain)
x̂ = x̂− + K (z − x̂−),   P = (1 − K) P− the correction is the gain times the innovation z − x̂− (how surprising the measurement was)

That is the entire scalar filter: two lines to predict, three to update, forever. The next page unpacks what the gain K means.

A dial between model and measurement

The gain K = P−/(P−+R) is a number between 0 and 1, and it is pure intuition:

  • Trusted measurement (small R) → K → 1 → the estimate jumps almost all the way to the measurement.
  • Noisy measurement (large R) → K → 0 → the estimate barely moves; you lean on the model.
  • Very uncertain state (large P−, e.g. at start-up) → K → 1 → grab the data; you had nothing better.

Equivalently, in precision form the update is a precision-weighted average — certainties add:

1/P = 1/P− + 1/R after fusing, you are always more certain than before the measurement (P shrinks) — unless the model added more than the measurement removed

So the filter is a self-adjusting thermostat: when it is unsure, it listens to sensors; when it is confident (and the sensors are noisy), it holds its course. It computes exactly how much to listen, at every step, from the covariances alone.

The gain is not tuned by hand. It falls out of the arithmetic of the two uncertainties. You only choose Q and R; K takes care of itself.

From numbers to vectors and covariances

Real states are vectors (position and velocity, or a whole grid). The single number x becomes a vector, the variance P becomes a covariance matrix, and multiplication becomes matrix algebra. The covariance is the key upgrade: its diagonal holds the uncertainty of each component, and its off-diagonals hold the correlations the filter learns between them.

P = 𝔼[(x − x̂)(x − x̂)⊤] a symmetric positive-definite matrix; its uncertainty “ellipse” is what the animations draw

That correlation structure is what lets a position-only sensor sharpen the estimate of an unmeasured velocity: once the filter has seen the position move, the covariance encodes that position and velocity are linked, so a new position measurement also informs velocity. This is the quiet magic of the multivariate filter — information flows to states you never directly observe.

Reading an uncertainty ellipse — in 2-D, P draws an ellipse: its size is how unsure you are, its orientation is the correlation. A long thin diagonal ellipse says “I know the sum of these two well, but not each alone.”

The whole algorithm on one card

The multivariate Kalman filter is exactly five equations per time step — two to predict, three to update. Memorise these and you have the filter:

# PREDICT (push belief forward through the dynamics) x̂⁻ = F x̂ # 1. predicted state mean P⁻ = F P Fᵀ + Q # 2. predicted covariance (grows by Q) # UPDATE (fold in the measurement z) y = z − H x̂⁻ # 3. innovation (measurement surprise) S = H P⁻ Hᵀ + R # innovation covariance K = P⁻ Hᵀ S⁻¹ # 4. Kalman gain x̂ = x̂⁻ + K y # 5. corrected state P = (I − K H) P⁻ # corrected covariance

The shapes tell the story: y and S live in measurement space (compare prediction to data there), the gain K maps a measurement-space surprise back into state space, and the correction Ky is added to the predicted state. Everything else is bookkeeping of the covariance.

That’s it. Every Kalman filter in the world — phone GPS, spacecraft, trading desk — is these five lines wrapped around a specific F, H, Q, R. The next three pages prove they are optimal.

The filter is just Bayes’ rule for Gaussians

Keep a Gaussian belief p(xk−1|z1:k−1) = 𝒩(x̂, P). Two exact operations turn it into the belief at the next step.

Predict = marginalise over the dynamics

Push a Gaussian through a linear map x→Fx and add independent noise Q. A linear function of a Gaussian is Gaussian, and independent covariances add:

x̂⁻ = F x̂,   P⁻ = F P Fᵀ + Q
Update = condition on the measurement

The joint of state and measurement is Gaussian. The rule for conditioning a joint Gaussian (x, z) on the observed z gives a Gaussian whose mean and covariance are exactly:

x̂ = x̂⁻ + P⁻Hᵀ(HP⁻Hᵀ+R)⁻¹(z − Hx̂⁻),  P = P⁻ − P⁻Hᵀ(HP⁻Hᵀ+R)⁻¹HP⁻

Recognise the factor P⁻Hᵀ(HP⁻Hᵀ+R)⁻¹ — that is the Kalman gain K. So the five equations are nothing but “propagate a Gaussian, then condition a Gaussian.” Because the operations are exact for linear-Gaussian models, the Kalman filter returns the exact posterior — not an approximation.

Consequence — for a linear-Gaussian system the Kalman filter is the optimal estimator by every sensible criterion at once: the posterior mean (minimum mean-squared error), the maximum-a-posteriori point, and the best linear unbiased estimator all coincide.

The gain as the least-squares optimum

Here is the derivation that needs no Gaussian assumption — only means and covariances. Look for the best linear update of the form x̂ = x̂⁻ + K(z − Hx̂⁻), and choose K to minimise the expected squared error 𝔼‖x − x̂‖² = tr(P).

Write the posterior covariance for a general K

Substituting the measurement model and simplifying (the “Joseph form”):

P = (I − KH)P⁻(I − KH)ᵀ + K R Kᵀ
Differentiate the trace and set it to zero
∂ tr(P)/∂K = −2(I − KH)P⁻Hᵀ + 2KR = 0
Solve for K
K = P⁻Hᵀ(HP⁻Hᵀ + R)⁻¹ the same gain again — now as the minimiser of the estimation error

This is the deeper reason the Kalman filter is trusted even when the noise is not Gaussian: among all estimators that are a linear function of the data, none has smaller error covariance. It is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE). If the noise happens to be Gaussian, “best linear” upgrades to “best, full stop.”

The Joseph form, and keeping P honest

The tidy update P = (I − KH)P⁻ is correct in exact arithmetic but fragile in floating point: rounding can make P lose symmetry or slip out of positive-definiteness, and a filter whose covariance goes negative diverges. The robust replacement is the Joseph form, which is symmetric by construction and valid for any gain:

P = (I − KH) P⁻ (I − KH)ᵀ + K R Kᵀ a few more multiplies, far better numerical behaviour — the default in production filters

Other standard safeguards:

  • Square-root / UD filters. Propagate a factor of P (Cholesky) instead of P itself, guaranteeing positive-definiteness and doubling the effective precision.
  • Symmetrise each step: P ← ½(P + Pᵀ).
  • Well-conditioned units. Scale states so positions and velocities have comparable magnitudes; otherwise S is ill-conditioned to invert.
Failure to watch for — a filter that looks confident (tiny P) but is wrong. If P collapses faster than the errors do, the gain goes to zero and the filter stops listening to data — “smug divergence.” The consistency checks on the next-but-one page catch this.

How to tell if your filter is lying

The innovation y = z − Hx̂⁻ — the gap between what you measured and what you predicted — is the filter’s report card. If the filter’s model of its own uncertainty is right, the innovations must be white (uncorrelated in time) and have covariance exactly S. Two cheap tests:

NIS = yᵀ S⁻¹ y  should average the measurement dimension m
NEES = (x − x̂)ᵀ P⁻¹ (x − x̂)  should average the state dimension n (needs ground truth)
  • NIS too big → innovations are larger than S says → you are over-confident (Q or R too small). The filter will diverge.
  • NIS too small → you are under-confident (Q or R too large). Estimates are sluggish and noisy.
  • Innovations correlated in time → your model F is wrong, not just the noise levels.
Always plot the innovations. A calibrated Kalman filter has white, unit-variance (in S) innovations. This one diagnostic catches almost every practical failure — and it needs no ground truth.

The two dials, and the trade-off they set

You never tune the gain directly — you set Q (how much you trust the model) and R (how much you trust the sensor), and the filter computes everything else. What matters is really their ratio:

  • Q too small (“sluggish”): the filter believes its model too much, reacts slowly, and lags behind manoeuvres.
  • Q too large (“jumpy”): the filter distrusts its model, chases every noisy measurement, and is jittery.
  • Just right: enough model to smooth the noise, enough responsiveness to follow the truth.

Same measurements, three settings — flip between them and watch lag trade against jitter:

Sluggish cuts corners on the bends (lag); jumpy hugs the noisy dots (jitter); balanced threads between. R is often measurable from the sensor; Q is the real art — or estimate both from data.
Estimating the noises — you can learn Q and R from data rather than guess: expectation-maximisation, autocovariance least-squares, or simply widening Q until the innovations pass the NIS test. Adaptive filters retune them online.

Use the future to fix the past

The Kalman filter is causal: its estimate at time k uses data only up to k — right for real-time use, but it necessarily lags and stays noisy near manoeuvres. If you have the whole record and only need the answer afterwards (offline analysis, reprocessing), you can do better with a smoother: run the filter forward, then a second pass backward that lets later data refine earlier estimates. The classic is the Rauch–Tung–Striebel (RTS) smoother.

Ck = Pkf Fᵀ (Pk+1−)⁻¹,   x̂ks = x̂kf + Ck(x̂k+1s − x̂k+1−) a backward recursion that blends each filtered state with the smoothed future
Blue = causal filter, green = RTS smoother. The smoother is tighter and lag-free — but it can only be computed once the future data is in.
Real-time → filter. Post-hoc → smoother. The smoother is strictly more accurate because it conditions on more data; the price is that it is non-causal.

The same filter, upside down

There is a dual formulation that tracks the information matrix Y = P⁻¹ and information vector ŷ = P⁻¹x̂ instead of covariance and mean. In these coordinates the update becomes a simple addition:

Y = Y⁻ + HᵀR⁻¹H,   ŷ = ŷ⁻ + HᵀR⁻¹z each measurement just adds its information — fusing many sensors is trivial, and “no information” (total ignorance) is Y=0

This makes two things easy that the standard form finds awkward: fusing many sensors (add their information terms in any order) and starting from no prior (set Y=0 instead of an infinite covariance). The trade is reversed: prediction is cheap in covariance form and awkward here, so you pick the coordinates that suit your problem. It also exposes the filter’s kinship with recursive least squares, whose normal-equations accumulation is exactly this information update.

Recursive least squares — if the state does not move (F=I, Q=0), the Kalman filter is recursive least squares: each measurement updates the running least-squares solution and its covariance. The Kalman filter is RLS plus a model of how the answer drifts.

When the gain stops changing

For a time-invariant system the covariance often converges to a fixed P∞, after which the gain K is constant. That fixed covariance solves the (discrete algebraic) Riccati equation:

P∞ = F P∞ Fᵀ + Q − F P∞ Hᵀ(H P∞ Hᵀ + R)⁻¹ H P∞ Fᵀ solve once offline; then the filter is a fixed-gain linear recursion — extremely cheap to run

With a constant gain the filter becomes a fixed linear time-invariant system, and in the frequency domain it is exactly the Wiener filter — the optimal filter Norbert Wiener derived in the 1940s for stationary signals in noise. So the Kalman filter is the Wiener filter’s time-domain, recursive, non-stationary generalisation: it handles start-up transients, time-varying models and finite data that the Wiener filter cannot.

Two famous filters, one idea. Wiener: optimal, stationary, frequency-domain, batch. Kalman: optimal, non-stationary, time-domain, recursive. At steady state they are the same filter.

Gaps, varying rates, and outliers

A quiet strength of the recursive form: it copes gracefully with messy real data.

  • Missing measurements. No data this step? Just predict and skip the update — the estimate coasts on the model and its uncertainty grows until data returns. Nothing else changes.
  • Irregular / multi-rate sampling. Let F and Q depend on the actual gap Δt since the last step. Different sensors arriving at different rates each just do an update when they arrive (natural in the information form).
  • Outliers. A wild measurement produces a huge innovation. Gate it: reject any measurement whose normalised innovation squared (NIS) exceeds a chi-square threshold, or downweight it (robust / Huberised updates). Without gating, one bad point can wrench the estimate.
  • Constraints & known inputs. Control inputs enter as Fx + Bu; equality constraints can be enforced by projection or pseudo-measurements.
Why this matters — real streams have dropouts, jitter, and glitches. The Kalman filter’s predict/update split handles all three with almost no extra code, which is a big part of its practical dominance.

The world is often nonlinear

The linear Kalman filter assumes the state evolves and is measured through matrices F and H. Plenty of problems refuse to fit:

  • Range & bearing sensors. A radar or camera measures distance r = √(x²+y²) and angle θ = atan2(y,x) — nonlinear functions of position.
  • Rotations & attitude. Orientation composes through trigonometry, not addition.
  • Physical dynamics. Pendulums, orbits, chemical kinetics, epidemic models — all nonlinear in the state.

Write the general model with functions instead of matrices:

xk = f(xk−1) + w,   zk = h(xk) + v now the belief pushed through f or h is not Gaussian, so the exact filter is intractable

We need an approximation that keeps the Kalman machinery. The oldest and still most-used answer: linearise the functions at the current best estimate — the Extended Kalman Filter.

Linearise, then run the Kalman equations

The EKF’s idea is a first-order Taylor expansion. At each step, replace the nonlinear f and h by their Jacobians (matrices of partial derivatives) evaluated at the current estimate, then plug into the ordinary Kalman equations.

The Jacobians (local linear approximations)
Fk = ∂f/∂x |x̂,   Hk = ∂h/∂x |x̂⁻
Predict (mean uses the true f; covariance uses the Jacobian)
x̂⁻ = f(x̂),   P⁻ = Fk P Fkᵀ + Q
Update (innovation uses the true h; gain uses the Jacobian)
y = z − h(x̂⁻),  S = HkP⁻Hkᵀ+R,  K = P⁻HkᵀS⁻¹,  x̂ = x̂⁻+Ky,  P = (I−KHk)P⁻

Note the careful split: the means are propagated through the exact nonlinear functions (so you don’t throw away the nonlinearity where it matters most), while the covariances are propagated through the local linear approximation (so the Kalman gain formula still applies). The result is no longer exact or optimal — it is a principled, cheap approximation that works remarkably well when the functions are smooth over the width of the uncertainty.

Capturing that geometry is the whole point of using a filter rather than converting-then-averaging.

Where linearisation bites

The EKF is a workhorse, but it is an approximation, and it fails in characteristic ways you should recognise:

  • Strong nonlinearity over the uncertainty. If f or h curves sharply across the width of P, the tangent line is a poor stand-in — the covariance is wrong and the filter can diverge.
  • Bad initialisation. Linearising around a wildly wrong estimate gives a wrong Jacobian, which gives a worse estimate — a vicious cycle. Good starts matter more than for the linear filter.
  • Bias. Because it ignores second-order curvature, the EKF’s mean is slightly biased and its covariance is typically over-optimistic (too small) — watch the NIS test.
  • Jacobians. Deriving and coding them is error-prone; a wrong Jacobian is a silent, common bug. Use automatic differentiation or a numerical Jacobian to check.
  • Discontinuities / angle wrap. Innovations in angles must be wrapped to (−π,π] or the filter lurches at the ±180° seam.
Rule of thumb — if the EKF struggles, the problem is usually too nonlinear for a single tangent line. Reach for a derivative-free alternative (next page) before adding hacks.

Better nonlinear filters

Unscented KF (UKF)

Instead of linearising, push a small set of deterministic sigma points through the true f and h, then re-fit a Gaussian. No Jacobians, accurate to higher order, and usually more robust and no more expensive than the EKF. Often the better default for nonlinear problems.

Particle filter

Represent the belief by weighted samples and propagate them through the exact model. Handles arbitrary nonlinearity and non-Gaussian, multimodal posteriors — at much higher cost, and with degeneracy issues in high dimension.

Ensemble KF (EnKF)

A Monte-Carlo Kalman update using an ensemble to represent the covariance — the workhorse of numerical weather prediction and ocean/geophysical data assimilation, where the state is millions of variables.

Iterated / hybrid

The iterated EKF relinearises the update a few times (a Gauss–Newton step) for sharp measurement models; hybrids combine a KF backbone with sampling where needed.

Ladder of choices. Linear → Kalman. Mildly nonlinear → EKF/UKF (prefer UKF). Strongly nonlinear or multimodal → particle filter. Huge state → EnKF. Same predict/update skeleton throughout.

Strengths and honest limits

Plays to its strengths when…

  • dynamics & measurements are (near) linear and noise is roughly Gaussian — then it is optimal;
  • you need real-time, constant-memory estimates on a stream;
  • you want uncertainty (covariance) alongside the estimate;
  • you must fuse multiple sensors or infer unmeasured states;
  • data is gappy or irregular — predict-and-skip handles it.

Struggles when…

  • the model is strongly nonlinear (EKF/UKF/PF instead);
  • the posterior is multimodal — a single Gaussian cannot represent “A or B” (particle filter);
  • noise is heavy-tailed or has outliers — needs gating / robust variants;
  • Q and R are unknown and mis-set — the filter is only as honest as its noise model;
  • the model F is wrong — no gain tuning fixes a mis-specified dynamics.
Its power and its risk are the same thing: it commits to a single Gaussian belief and a model you supply. When those assumptions hold it is unbeatable and cheap; when they don’t, it can be confidently wrong — so always check the innovations.

Everyday data-analysis uses

  • Phone & vehicle navigation. Fuse GPS (accurate, slow, drops out) with accelerometers & gyros (fast, drifting). The KF gives a smooth pose that survives tunnels — the textbook sensor-fusion win.
  • Robotics & self-driving. Localization, SLAM, and multi-object tracking (each track is a little KF; the EKF/UKF handles range-bearing lidar/radar).
  • Finance & econometrics. Time-varying CAPM betas and volatility; Kalman-filtered pairs-trading spreads (the hedge ratio as a drifting state); structural time-series decomposition (trend + seasonal + cycle) for nowcasting GDP.
  • Forecasting & smoothing. Dynamic linear models generalise exponential smoothing with principled uncertainty and missing-data handling.
  • Engineering & science. Battery state-of-charge in every EV; process control; fusing thermometers; orbit determination; weather/ocean data assimilation (EnKF) at the scale of millions of state variables.
  • Computer vision. Smoothing detections into tracks; visual-inertial odometry behind AR headsets.
The through-line — wherever a quantity drifts in time and is seen noisily and indirectly, a Kalman filter turns the stream into a calibrated running estimate. It is arguably the most widely deployed statistical algorithm outside the average itself.

LINEAR KALMAN FILTER state x, covariance P; models F,H,Q,R predict: x = Fx ; P = F P Fᵀ + Q update: y = z - Hx ; S = H P Hᵀ + R K = P Hᵀ S⁻¹ ; x = x + Ky ; P = (I-KH)P # Joseph for stability EXTENDED KF nonlinear f,h; Jacobians F=∂f/∂x, H=∂h/∂x predict: x = f(x) ; P = F P Fᵀ + Q update: y = z - h(x) ; (then S,K,x,P as above) RTS SMOOTHER C = Pᶠ Fᵀ (Pᶜ⁻)⁻¹ ; xᵀ = xᶠ + C(xᵀ₊₁ - xᶜ₊₁⁻) DIAGNOSTIC innovations y must be white with covariance S (check NIS)
