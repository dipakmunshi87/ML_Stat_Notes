Turning an integral you cannot do into an optimisation you can — and the two places it quietly lies to you.

Dr. Dipak Munshi · every number on this page is computed in your browser while you watch.

Bayesian inference asks for a posterior. Usually you cannot have one, because the normalising integral is intractable. Markov chain Monte Carlo attacks that by sampling. Variational inference does something different and, at first sight, cheeky: it gives up on the exact posterior, picks a family of distributions it can handle, and finds the member of that family closest to the truth. Inference becomes optimisation.

That trade has a clear benefit and a clear cost. The benefit is speed and scale — it runs on minibatches, on a GPU, on datasets where MCMC is hopeless. The cost is that the answer is only as good as the family you chose, and nothing in the output tells you how good that was. This page derives the method from scratch and then shows you, in four worked examples, exactly where it succeeds and where it fails.

1. The problem, stated honestly

Bayes' theorem for a latent variable z given data x:

p(z | x) = p(x | z) p(z)p(x) ,  p(x) = ∫ p(x | z) p(z) dz

The numerator is easy — you wrote down the likelihood and the prior yourself. The denominator is an integral over every possible value of z. In one dimension you could do it on a grid. In fifty dimensions there is no grid: a mere ten points per axis is 1050 evaluations, which is more than the number of atoms in the Earth.

The one-sentence framing

Everything in Bayesian computation is a strategy for avoiding that integral. MCMC avoids it by constructing a chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior, needing only ratios in which p(x) cancels. Variational inference avoids it by turning the problem into optimisation, where the unknown constant becomes an additive term that does not affect where the optimum is.

2. The ELBO, derived

Choose a family of distributions qφ(z) with parameters φ — say Gaussians, with φ = (μ, σ). We want the member closest to the posterior. "Closest" in the Kullback–Leibler sense:

KL(qφ ‖ p) = ∫ qφ(z) log qφ(z)p(z | x) dz = Eq [ log qφ(z) − log p(z | x) ]

This looks useless — it contains the very posterior we cannot compute. But substitute p(z|x) = p(x,z) / p(x) and watch what happens:

KL = Eq[ log qφ(z) − log p(x,z) ] + log p(x)

The intractable term p(x) has come out of the expectation entirely, because it does not depend on z. Define the remainder, with a sign flip, as the evidence lower bound:

ELBO(φ) ≡ Eqφ [ log p(x,z) − log qφ(z) ]

and the whole thing rearranges into the identity that the method rests on:

log p(x) = ELBO(φ) + KL(qφ ‖ p)
Why this is the whole trick

The left-hand side is a constant — it does not depend on φ at all. KL is non-negative. So pushing the ELBO up pushes the KL down by exactly the same amount. Maximising the ELBO is minimising the distance to the posterior, and you never have to evaluate the posterior to do it.

It also explains the name: since KL ≥ 0, the ELBO is a lower bound on the log evidence. The gap between them is precisely how wrong your approximation is — which is useful in principle and maddening in practice, because you cannot measure the gap without knowing what you are trying to compute.

The ELBO splits in a way worth seeing, because it shows the tension the optimiser is resolving:

ELBO = Eq[ log p(x | z) ] − KL(qφ ‖ p(z))

The first term rewards explaining the data; the second penalises straying from the prior. Fit against regularisation, falling out of the algebra rather than being bolted on.

3. Where the "stochastic" comes from

The ELBO is an expectation, and expectations are integrals — we appear to have swapped one intractable integral for another. Two things rescue it.

Monte Carlo, for the expectation

We can sample from qφ, because we chose it to be something simple. So estimate the expectation with a handful of draws — often just one:

ELBO ≈ 1S ∑s=1S [ log p(x, z(s)) − log qφ(z(s)) ] ,  z(s) ∼ qφ

Minibatching, for the data

If the likelihood factorises over N independent observations, a random subset of size M gives an unbiased estimate once scaled by N/M. This is what makes the method scale: the cost per step stops depending on the size of the dataset.

Both estimates are noisy but unbiased, which is all stochastic gradient ascent requires. Hence stochastic variational inference.

4. The reparameterisation trick

One obstacle remains, and it is the one that held the field back for years. We need the gradient of an expectation whose distribution depends on the parameters we are differentiating:

∇φ Eqφ(z)[ f(z) ]

You cannot simply push the gradient inside, because φ controls where the samples come from. There are two ways out.

The score-function estimator (REINFORCE) uses the identity ∇q = q ∇log q, giving E[f(z) ∇φ log qφ(z)]. It works for anything, including discrete variables. Its variance is often enormous.

The reparameterisation trick moves the randomness out of the parameters. For a Gaussian:

z = μ + σ ε ,  ε ∼ N(0, 1)

Now the randomness lives in ε, whose distribution has nothing to do with φ. The expectation is over ε, so the gradient walks straight inside:

∇φ Eε[ f(μ + σε) ] = Eε[ ∇φ f(μ + σε) ]
Why it matters so much

The reparameterised gradient uses the shape of f — it differentiates through the function itself. The score-function estimator only ever evaluates f and multiplies by a score; it is blind to the geometry. The practical difference is variance, often by orders of magnitude, and variance is what decides whether stochastic optimisation converges in a thousand steps or a million. 5. Example one — a case with a known answer 1D

Always start where the truth is available. Take a Gaussian prior and a Gaussian likelihood: z ∼ N(0, 1), and N observations xi ∼ N(z, σn2). This is conjugate, so the posterior is Gaussian and we can write it down exactly:

σpost2 = 11 + N/σn2 ,  μpost = σpost2 · ∑xiσn2

The variational family is also Gaussian, so the true posterior is a member of the family. There is no approximation error to speak of, and a correct implementation must find it. If it does not, the bug is in the optimiser, not the method — which is precisely why this test comes first.

FIGURE 3 · SVI on a conjugate Gaussian — the answer is known in advance
What to notice

Run it with one sample per step. The ELBO trace is visibly noisy — that is the Monte Carlo estimate, not instability — yet μ and σ still converge to the exact values. Unbiased noisy gradients are enough. Raise the sample count and the trace smooths, but the answer does not improve, because it was never biased. That is a useful instinct to carry: more samples buy you a quieter ride, not a different destination.

6. Example two — where KL chooses a side 1D

Now a posterior the Gaussian family cannot represent: a mixture of two well-separated modes. A single Gaussian must do something, and what it does is characteristic.

The reason lies in the direction of the KL divergence. We minimise KL(q‖p), which is an expectation under q:

KL(q ‖ p) = ∫ q(z) log q(z)p(z) dz

Wherever q puts mass and p does not, the ratio explodes and the penalty is severe. But wherever p has mass and q is zero, the integrand is q log(q/p) → 0 — the integral simply does not look there. Missing an entire mode is free.

FIGURE 4 · a bimodal posterior — watch which mode survives
Mode-seeking, and why it is not a bug

KL(q‖p) is mode-seeking: it prefers to be confidently right about part of the posterior than diffusely right about all of it. The reverse direction, KL(p‖q), is mode-covering — it would stretch a single Gaussian across both humps, putting most of its mass where the posterior has none. Neither is "correct"; they answer different questions. But the one VI uses is the one that can silently discard half your posterior, and the ELBO will look perfectly healthy while it happens.

Start the optimiser from the left, from the right, and from the middle. Same model, same data, different answers. If your reported uncertainty depends on the initialisation, you have not found the posterior — you have found a local optimum of a bound.

7. Example three — the mean-field trap 2D

In more than one dimension the usual choice is the mean-field family: a product of independent one-dimensional distributions,

qφ(z) = ∏j qj(zj)

It is cheap, it is easy to sample, and it scales to millions of parameters — which is why it is everywhere. It also assumes the posterior has no correlations. Real posteriors almost always do: two parameters that trade off against each other produce a tilted ellipse, and a product of independent Gaussians can only produce an axis-aligned one.

The consequence is not a small cosmetic error. Fit an axis-aligned ellipse inside a tilted one by minimising KL(q‖p), and the best it can do is tuck itself inside the narrow waist. For a bivariate Gaussian with correlation ρ, the mean-field solution has marginal standard deviations smaller than the truth by exactly

σMFσtrue = √1 − ρ2

At ρ = 0.9 that is a factor of 0.436 — your error bars are less than half their true size, and nothing in the fit complains.

FIGURE 5 · mean-field vs full-rank on a correlated posterior
The single most important practical fact about VI

Mean-field variational inference underestimates posterior variance, and the stronger the correlations the worse it gets. The central values are usually fine. The error bars are not. If you are reporting a detection significance, a confidence interval, or anything that gets compared against a threshold, this is the failure that will bite you — and it bites silently, because a too-narrow posterior looks like a precise measurement.

The fix is to enrich the family: a full-rank Gaussian (a full covariance, via its Cholesky factor) costs d(d+1)/2 parameters instead of 2d, and recovers the correlation exactly in this example. Normalising flows go further still. Run the comparison above and read the measured ratio against √(1−ρ2).

8. Example four — when the family is simply wrong 2D

The correlated Gaussian was a case where a richer family fixes everything, because the truth was still Gaussian. Now a posterior that is not Gaussian in any coordinate system you would naturally choose — the banana, which arises whenever two parameters enter the model multiplicatively or through a nonlinear transformation. This shape is common in practice: amplitude and slope, mass and distance, any product degeneracy.

FIGURE 6 · a banana posterior — both families fail, differently
The lesson the ELBO cannot teach you

Both fits converge. Both ELBOs flatten out and stop improving. Neither approximation is close to the posterior, and the ELBO value does not tell you that — because the gap between the ELBO and the true log evidence is exactly the thing you cannot compute.

A converged ELBO means the optimiser has stopped moving. It does not mean the answer is right. Those are different claims, and conflating them is the most common mistake made with this method.

9. So how do you know whether to believe it?

Since the ELBO cannot certify the answer, the checks have to come from outside:

  1. Run a short MCMC chain on a reduced problem. Not the full model — a cut-down version you can afford to sample properly. If VI and MCMC disagree there, they will disagree at scale too.
  2. Compare mean-field against full-rank. If the error bars move substantially when you enrich the family, the mean-field answer was wrong and you have measured by how much. If they barely move, that is weak evidence the family is adequate.
  3. Restart from several initialisations. Example two showed different starting points giving different posteriors. That is cheap to test and immediately damning.
  4. Check calibration on simulated data. Generate data from known parameters, fit, and ask how often the true value falls inside the nominal 68% interval. If a 68% interval contains the truth 40% of the time, you have the mean-field trap and now you have measured it.
  5. Importance-weight the result. Using q as a proposal and reweighting by p/q both improves the estimate and diagnoses it — if a handful of samples carry all the weight, q is a poor match.
When SVI is the right tool

Use it when the model is large, the data is larger, you need an answer today, you will act on the central value, and you have the means to check the uncertainty separately. It is the workhorse behind variational autoencoders and most large-scale Bayesian deep learning for exactly these reasons.

Do not use it — or do not use it alone — when the error bar is the result. Detection significances, exclusion limits, anything compared against a threshold. There the mean-field variance collapse is not a technicality; it is the difference between a discovery and a null result.
