Bayesian Optimisation

How to optimise a function so expensive that you can only afford thirty evaluations of it — and why the kernel you chose matters more than the algorithm.

Dr. Dipak Munshi · every number on this page is computed in your browser

Some functions are cheap. You can evaluate them a million times, take gradients, and let a standard optimiser do its work. Other functions are not: a simulation that runs overnight, a laboratory experiment, a full training run of a large model, an observing campaign you have to apply for. There you might get thirty evaluations in total, you have no gradients, and every point you spend is one you cannot spend elsewhere.

Bayesian optimisation is built for exactly that regime. It keeps a probabilistic model of the function itself, updates it after every evaluation, and uses it to decide where to look next — balancing going where the model says the optimum is against going where the model admits it does not know. This page builds it from scratch, then shows you four examples including the one where it fails badly, quietly, and for a reason that is entirely your fault.

1. The setting, and when this is the right tool

You want to minimise f(x) over some bounded domain, where:

  • f is a black box — you can evaluate it, and that is all.
  • Evaluations are expensive: minutes, hours, or money.
  • There are no gradients, and finite differences would cost d+1 evaluations per step, which you cannot afford.
  • f may be noisy, and is probably multimodal.
  • The dimension is modest — say under twenty.
Getting this judgement right matters more than any tuning

If evaluations are cheap, use a gradient method, or differential evolution, and do not bother with any of this: the bookkeeping will cost more than the function does. If the dimension is in the hundreds, Bayesian optimisation will not help you either, for reasons in §8. Its territory is expensive, low-dimensional, gradient-free — hyperparameter tuning, experimental design, calibrating an expensive simulator, choosing where to point an instrument.

Two pieces make it work, and they are worth naming separately because they fail in different ways:

expensive f → surrogate (cheap probabilistic model) → acquisition (where to look next)

2. The surrogate: a Gaussian process in one page

A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions. The idea is less exotic than it sounds: assume that the function values at any finite set of points are jointly Gaussian, with a covariance that depends only on how far apart the points are.

k(x, x′) = σf2 exp (− (x − x′)22ℓ2 )

That is the squared-exponential kernel. It encodes one assumption: points closer together than the lengthscale ℓ have similar function values. Everything the model believes before seeing data is in that sentence.

Given n observations y at points X, with noise variance σn2, the posterior at a new point x* is Gaussian with

μ(x*) = k*⊤ (K + σn2I)−1 y ,  σ2(x*) = k(x*,x*) − k*⊤ (K + σn2I)−1 k*

Two things are worth noticing. The mean is a weighted average of the observed values, with weights set by the kernel. And the variance does not depend on y at all — only on where you have looked. The model's uncertainty is a statement about coverage of the domain, not about what it found there. That fact is what makes the next section possible.

Add four or five points, then drag the lengthscale. Short ℓ and the posterior snaps back to the prior between observations — the model believes the function can do anything in the gaps. Long ℓ and it draws a smooth curve through everything, confident far from any data. Neither is more correct; they are different assumptions, and the data you have cannot easily distinguish them. Hold on to that: it is the whole of §7.

3. The acquisition function: where to look next

The surrogate gives a mean and a standard deviation everywhere. The acquisition function turns that into a single number per candidate point, and we evaluate f wherever it is largest. Every acquisition function is a different answer to the same question: how much is uncertainty worth, compared with a promising mean?

Write f+ for the best value seen so far, and let Z = (f+ − μ(x) − ξ) / σ(x) for a minimisation problem.

Probability of improvement

PI(x) = Φ(Z)

The probability that this point beats the incumbent — by any margin at all. Intuitive, and too greedy: a point that improves by 10−9 with near-certainty scores higher than one that might improve enormously but could also fail. It gets stuck.

Expected improvement

EI(x) = (f+ − μ − ξ) Φ(Z) + σ φ(Z)

The expected size of the improvement, not merely its probability. Those two terms are the trade-off made explicit: the first rewards a promising mean, the second rewards uncertainty. Where σ = 0 — a point you have already measured — EI is exactly zero, so it never wastes an evaluation. This is the default, and deservedly so.

Lower confidence bound

LCB(x) = − [ μ(x) − κ σ(x) ]

Crude and effective: be optimistic by κ standard deviations. The trade-off is now a knob you turn rather than a consequence of the maths, which is either a virtue or a nuisance depending on whether you know what to set it to. It has the best theoretical regret guarantees of the three.

Thompson sampling

Draw one random function from the posterior and minimise that. Exploration comes from the randomness of the draw rather than from an explicit bonus. Trivially parallel, which matters when you can run several expensive evaluations at once.

The test function is Forrester's, f(x) = (6x−2)2 sin(12x−4) on [0, 1], a standard benchmark. Its global minimum is −6.02074 at x = 0.7572, and there is a decoy local minimum near x = 0.15 to catch anything too greedy.

What the loop looks like when it is working

Watch the shaded band. Early on it is wide everywhere and the acquisition sends you to unexplored regions. As points accumulate the band collapses where you have looked, and the acquisition switches to refining the best region. That transition from exploration to exploitation is not scheduled — it emerges, because uncertainty is spent as it is resolved. Try Probability of Improvement and watch it get stuck in the decoy.

4. Do the acquisition functions actually differ? 1D

It is easy to assert that EI beats PI and that both beat random. Easier still to test it: run each strategy many times from different random starts on the same problem and compare the distribution of outcomes, not one run.

The table gives the median regret and the failure rate — the fraction of runs that never escaped the decoy minimum. Both matter, and the second is the one usually omitted. A method with a slightly worse median but a much lower failure rate is the better choice when you get one attempt at an expensive campaign, which is the situation this whole method exists for.

This mirrors a comparison I ran on multimodal objectives, scoring Bayesian optimisation against Nelder–Mead, differential evolution, MCMC and neural surrogates on evaluation budget and failure rate across restarts rather than on the best value found. The ranking changes when you score it that way.

5. Two dimensions: a real benchmark 2D

The Branin function is the standard two-dimensional test case. It has three global minima, all with value 0.397887, which makes it a fair test of whether a method finds an optimum rather than getting lucky:

f = a(x2 − b x12 + c x1 − r)2 + s(1 − t) cos(x1) + s

Switch the view

Flip between the true function, the surrogate's mean, its uncertainty, and the acquisition surface. Early on the surrogate is nothing like the truth and the acquisition is driven almost entirely by uncertainty. After fifteen or twenty points the mean has learned the three valleys, and the acquisition concentrates. You are watching the model's beliefs, not the function — and the difference between those two pictures is the whole reason this works when it works, and fails when it fails.

6. The failure nobody warns you about 2D

Every treatment of Bayesian optimisation explains the exploration–exploitation trade-off. Far fewer say plainly that the method's performance is dominated by the kernel hyperparameters, and that getting them wrong does not produce an error — it produces a confident, well-behaved, wrong answer.

The lengthscale is an assumption about how fast the function varies. Set it too long and the surrogate cannot represent the structure: it smooths the optimum away and the uncertainty band collapses over regions it has never visited, so the acquisition stops exploring them. Set it too short and every observation is local news: the posterior returns to the prior between points, the uncertainty is large everywhere, and the search degenerates towards random.

What to do about it

Do not fix the hyperparameters by hand. Fit them by maximising the marginal likelihood after each batch, or be properly Bayesian and marginalise over them. Every serious library does this by default — and it is worth knowing that this is what it is doing, because with very few observations the marginal likelihood is itself poorly determined, and early in a run the fitted lengthscale can be nonsense.

Standardise your inputs and outputs. A single isotropic lengthscale across parameters measured in different units is meaningless. Scale every input to [0, 1], centre and scale the output, or use a separate lengthscale per dimension (ARD), which also tells you which parameters matter.

Sanity-check against random search. If Bayesian optimisation is not beating random sampling on your problem, something is wrong with the model, not with your luck — and random search is a genuinely strong baseline in higher dimensions.

7. Honest limitations

  • Dimension. Performance degrades badly above roughly 20 parameters. The volume of the domain grows exponentially while your budget does not, and the surrogate cannot cover it. Remedies exist — additive kernels, random embeddings, trust regions — and all of them buy improvement by assuming extra structure.
  • Cost of the surrogate. Fitting a Gaussian process is O(n3) in the number of observations. Fine at n = 100, painful at 10,000 — though if you can afford 10,000 evaluations of your function, you did not need this method.
  • Noise. Everything above assumed you told the model the right noise level. Get it wrong and either the surrogate interpolates noise as though it were signal, or it dismisses real structure as noise.
  • The inner optimisation. Maximising the acquisition function is itself a multimodal optimisation problem. It is cheap, so you do it with many restarts — but it is another place to be quietly wrong.
  • Constraints and mixed variables. Unknown constraints, integer or categorical parameters, and multiple competing objectives all have extensions, and none of them is as clean as the story above.

The sentence worth keeping

Bayesian optimisation is not an optimiser that happens to be Bayesian. It is a model of your function, plus a rule for what to ask next. When it works, it is because the model was a reasonable description of the function. When it fails, it is almost always because the model was not — and the algorithm has no way of telling you so.

8. Software and reading

Libraries. BoTorch (PyTorch, the current research standard) and GPyTorch beneath it; Ax for a higher-level interface; scikit-optimize for something simple; Optuna and Hyperopt for hyperparameter work specifically, where the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator is a different surrogate with the same loop; GPflow if you prefer TensorFlow; Dragonfly for higher dimensions and parallel evaluation.
