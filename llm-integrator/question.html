Part 1 of 8 · the question

Can a language model
do physics?

Take a mass on a spring. Sample its position at even intervals, round each number to the nearest of 64 values, and show the resulting integers to a small language model. Never mention Hooke's law, energy, or that the numbers are a trajectory.

Then run it forward and use it as an integrator. It turns out to learn the law almost exactly, and to fail anyway — for a reason you can write down in one line.

The same loop

Generation is time stepping

A language model produces one token, appends it to its own input, and repeats. An integrator produces one state, appends it to its own history, and repeats.

That is not an analogy. It is the same operation, so stability, accuracy order, error growth and conservation all mean exactly what they usually mean.

The whole set turns on this one observation, which is why it is the second slide.

Why this rule and not F=maF=maF=ma

The model never sees a derivative, a force or a time. It sees a list of integers. The only thing it could possibly learn is the rule relating one integer to the previous ones — and for evenly sampled simple harmonic motion, that rule is the box above.

The experiment

What exactly is being asked

1 · Make the data

Draw a random amplitude and phase, evaluate the cosine at 9 evenly spaced times, and round each value to the nearest of 64 equally spaced bins between −1.3-1.3−1.3 and +1.3+1.3+1.3. Bin width 0.0410.0410.041.

The result is nine integers. That is the entire dataset format.

2 · Train

A small GPT — causal attention, a softmax over the 64 bins, 9,808 parameters — is trained to predict the next integer from the previous eight. Cross-entropy, Adam, about thirty seconds in your browser.

It is told nothing else. Not the frequency, not the step, not that there is a spring.

3 · Interrogate it

Then two questions. Did it find the rule? — fit xn+1=axn+bxn−1x_{n+1}=ax_n+bx_{n-1}xn+1​=axn​+bxn−1​ to its own predictions and compare aaa with 1.9601331.9601331.960133. Is it a good integrator? — roll it out for a thousand steps and watch the amplitude.

The point of the exercise

These two questions have different answers, and the gap between them is the interesting part. A model can learn a physical law to three decimal places and still be useless as an integrator — and the reason is not machine learning, it is numerical analysis.

Rounding into bins is what makes it a language model rather than a regression; page 2 is about what that costs.

Read rows two and four together

Leapfrog is a far cruder method than the model: two lines of arithmetic, no training, larger error at every single step. Over thirty-two periods it holds its amplitude to one part in a thousand, while the model's own rule loses ninety-two per cent of it.

Being nearly right at every step is not the same as being right after a thousand steps, and the difference has a name.
