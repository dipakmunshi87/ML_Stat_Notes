Two parts in ten thousand, from a model that was shown nothing but integers and never told what they meant. As a statement about learning a physical law from data, that is a real success and should be said plainly.

What it had to work out

That only the last two values matter, out of the eight it was given. That the relationship between them is linear. And then the value of the single coefficient, to four digits — a number that depends on ω\omegaω and Δt\Delta tΔt, neither of which it was ever shown.

Scale helps, as you would expect

Thirty-three times the parameters buys about thirty times the accuracy in the coefficient. Nothing surprising there — it is the usual picture, and it is worth confirming before arguing that more scale will not fix the problem on page 6.

What is left over

How much the linear rule does not explain

A fit is only meaningful with its residual. Fitting x^n+1=axn+bxn−1\hat x_{n+1}=ax_n+bx_{n-1}x^n+1​=axn​+bxn−1​ to the model's answers over several hundred histories leaves a root-mean-square residual of about 0.02 — roughly half a bin width.

So the two-term rule accounts for essentially all of its behaviour, and the leftovers are the size of the rounding. Two things are worth noting:

  • It uses only the last two values. Give it eight; it leans on two, which is correct — the physics does not need more.
  • Its map is linear. Not by construction: attention, a softmax and a feed-forward layer are all nonlinear. It became linear because the data is.
The honest caveat

A linear fit was chosen because we already know the true rule is linear. On a problem where you did not know the answer, the fit would be a hypothesis to test, not a measurement — and a small residual would be evidence for it, not proof.

What it did not learn

Nothing about ω\omegaω or Δt\Delta tΔt separately — only the combination cos⁡(ωΔt)\cos(\omega\Delta t)cos(ωΔt) the data contains. Train it at one sampling rate and it is useless at another.

That last box is the sharpest limit on the "it learned physics" claim, and it is worth making before the energy argument rather than after.

A trap in the other direction

0.6% in the coefficient is 15% in the period

It is tempting to read "the coefficient is right to 0.6%" as "the physics is right to 0.6%". It is not, and the reason is ordinary error propagation.

To get the frequency you must invert a=2cos⁡(ωΔt)a=2\cos(\omega\Delta t)a=2cos(ωΔt), and

δ(ωΔt)=δa2sin⁡(ωΔt).\delta(\omega\Delta t)=\frac{\delta a}{2\sin(\omega\Delta t)} .δ(ωΔt)=2sin(ωΔt)δa​.

At ωΔt=0.2\omega\Delta t=0.2ωΔt=0.2 the denominator is only 0.3970.3970.397, so the error is amplified by a factor of two and a half. Putting in δa=0.011\delta a = 0.011δa=0.011:

δ(ωΔt)=0.028⟹15% on the period.\delta(\omega\Delta t)=0.028 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 15\%\ \text{on the period}.δ(ωΔt)=0.028⟹15% on the period.

Which is exactly the phase drift visible within two periods on page 5. The coefficient is well determined; the physics it implies is not.

Why it is badly conditioned here

Because cos⁡\coscos is flat near zero. Sampling finely — small ωΔt\omega\Delta tωΔt — makes the recurrence easy to learn and the frequency hard to recover from it. Sampling coarsely does the opposite.

So the sampling rate trades one kind of accuracy against another, and neither the model nor the loss knows which you wanted.

The general form

When a learned model reports a parameter, ask what you have to do to that number to get the physical quantity, and how the error propagates through it. A well-fitted coefficient can be a badly determined observable.

This point is not in the written note; it came out of noticing the phase drift in the live rollout and working out where it came from.

The catch

One number that is not close enough

Write the rule as a matrix acting on the pair (xn,xn−1)(x_n,x_{n-1})(xn​,xn−1​):

(xn+1xn)=(ab10)(xnxn−1).\begin{pmatrix}x_{n+1}\\x_n\end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\1&0\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}x_{n}\\x_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}.(xn+1​xn​​)=(a1​b0​)(xn​xn−1​​).

Its determinant is −b-b−b, and for the exact rule b=−1b=-1b=−1, so the determinant is exactly 111. The eigenvalues are a complex pair of modulus −b=1\sqrt{-b}=1−b​=1: the map is a pure rotation, and rotations preserve amplitude forever.

Now put in the learned b=−0.99526b=-0.99526b=−0.99526. The modulus becomes

∣λ∣=0.99526=0.99763.|\lambda|=\sqrt{0.99526}=0.99763 .∣λ∣=0.99526​=0.99763.

A quarter of one per cent below one. Every step multiplies the amplitude by that, and after a thousand steps the factor is 0.997631000=0.0930.99763^{1000}=0.0930.997631000=0.093.

This is the whole failure

Not the coefficient aaa, which is close enough to be harmless. It is bbb, which controls the determinant, and therefore whether the map is a rotation or a spiral.

The exact rule needs b=−1b=-1b=−1 exactly. Not to four digits — exactly. Any deviation is the difference between a quantity that is conserved and one that is not.

The determinant being exactly 1 is what "symplectic" means for a one-degree-of-freedom system, which is the bridge to page 6.

Summary

Two questions, two answers

Did it learn the physics?

The coefficient, yes. Recovered from integers alone to 0.6% in a thirty-second browser model and to 0.018% in a larger one. It works out which inputs matter and matches the linearity of the data without being told to.

The frequency, less well. Because inverting the cosine is badly conditioned here, that 0.6% becomes a 15% error in the period.

Is it a good integrator?

No. Because one of the two coefficients has to be exact rather than accurate, and no amount of training makes an optimiser produce exactly −1-1−1. There is nothing in the loss that asks for it.

What this is really about

The gap between these two answers is not a fact about neural networks. It is the difference between accuracy and structure, and it is as old as numerical integration. A scheme can be less accurate at every step and still be better over a long run, if it has the right structure.

Page 5 runs it out. Page 6 names the structure.

"Accurate versus structure-preserving" is the sentence to leave with.
