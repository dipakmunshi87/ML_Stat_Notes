The one line

Everything is decided by one number

Iterating a linear map nnn times multiplies by λn\lambda^nλn. If ∣λ∣=1|\lambda|=1∣λ∣=1 the map is a rotation and the amplitude never changes. If ∣λ∣=0.99763|\lambda|=0.99763∣λ∣=0.99763, then after a thousand steps ∣λ∣1000=0.093|\lambda|^{1000}=0.093∣λ∣1000=0.093.

The exact rule has ∣λ∣=1|\lambda|=1∣λ∣=1 exactly, because its determinant is exactly one. The learned rule misses by a quarter of a per cent, and a thousand steps does the rest.

It is worth stressing that this needs no approximation: the map really is linear, so the eigenvalue really does decide everything.

Note that the error in aaa alone moves the eigenvalues along the circle — it changes the frequency but not the amplitude. Only the error in bbb moves them off it.

The separation of roles — aaa sets the frequency, bbb sets whether energy is conserved — is the single most useful thing on this page.

Two different errors

One coefficient must be exact

An error in aaa is survivable: your oscillation runs slightly fast or slow, and the energy is fine. An error in bbb is not: it compounds.

Why "exact" rather than "accurate"

bbb has to be −1-1−1 to machine precision, because there is no such thing as being slightly conserved. Nothing in a gradient-descent objective produces an exact value. The larger model in the companion note reached b=−0.99953b=-0.99953b=−0.99953, five times closer, and still lost a fifth of its amplitude over the same run.

Scaling the model moves the eigenvalue toward the circle. It never puts it on the circle.

The familiar version

This is the same statement as "a symplectic integrator has det⁡=1\det=1det=1 by construction". Leapfrog is built so that its determinant is one identically, whatever the step size — not because it is more accurate, but because of how it is assembled.

The a/b split maps onto phase error versus energy error, which every computational physicist already knows in another notation.

The fix

What leapfrog has that the model does not

A symplectic map preserves area in the position–velocity plane. Leapfrog is built from two shears and a translation, each of which has determinant exactly one — so their product does too, in exact arithmetic and in floating point.

That is not accuracy, it is structure. Leapfrog's error at any single step is larger than the model's; it simply cannot leak amplitude, because it has no way to change the area.

The area picture is the one physicists remember; the determinant is the one they can check.

Could it be fixed?

Three honest answers

Build the structure in

Do not let the network output the next state. Let it output the Hamiltonian, or a generating function, and take the step with a symplectic scheme. Then the determinant is one by construction and no amount of training error can change it.

This is what Hamiltonian and symplectic networks do. The cost is that you must know the structure in advance.

Put it in the loss

Add a penalty on the change in a conserved quantity, or train on multi-step rollouts rather than single steps, so that the compounding error becomes visible to the optimiser.

This helps, and is not exact: a penalty makes the violation small, not zero.

Do not use it as an integrator

Often the right answer. Use the learned model where its strength lies — interpolating a complicated one-step map, or supplying a closure term — and let a proper integrator do the time stepping.

Most successful learned solvers are hybrids of this kind.

What will not fix it

More parameters, more data, more training. All three move ∣λ∣|\lambda|∣λ∣ closer to 1 and none of them puts it there, because nothing in "predict the next value" refers to the determinant. The defect is invisible to the objective, so making the objective smaller does not remove it.

The three cards are roughly in order of how much you have to know about your system in advance.

The transferable statement

A learned time-stepper inherits every property of a numerical scheme, including the one that matters most over long runs: whether it preserves the structure of the problem. Accuracy per step and conservation over many steps are different things, and optimising the first does not deliver the second.
