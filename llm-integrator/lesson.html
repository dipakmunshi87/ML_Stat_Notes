What does and does not carry over.

The generalisation has to be made carefully: some of it transfers exactly and some of it does not.

Where it appears

The same failure, in production

Learned weather models

Trained to predict the next six hours, then rolled out for ten days — forty steps of a map they were never scored on. Fields lose variance over long rollouts; multi-step training and noise injection exist precisely because of it.

Machine-learned force fields

Fitted to energies and forces, then used for millions of steps of molecular dynamics. Energy drift is a standard diagnostic, and a well-fitted force field can still heat or cool a simulation that should conserve.

Learned solvers and closures

A network standing in for one step of a fluid or plasma solver. Tiny single-step error, unstable rollout — the most commonly reported difficulty in the field, and it is this problem.

Emulators used inside a chain

An emulator called 10610^6106 times inside a sampler is not rolled out, so it escapes this trap. The same logic applies to its bias, though: a small systematic error invisible in one call shifts a posterior when repeated.

Video and world models

Generate a frame, feed it back, repeat. Objects drift, sizes creep, quantities that should be constant are not. The complaint is the same one: the objective is per-step and the failure is cumulative.

Anything autoregressive

If your model consumes its own output, it is an integrator, and the question "what does it conserve?" is a fair one to ask of it.

The weather and force-field examples are the ones where the community already has standard mitigations, which is itself evidence that the problem is real.

The checks

What would have caught it

  1. Plot a conserved quantity over the longest run you care about. Not the loss, not the one-step error. Energy, mass, momentum, norm — whatever your system should keep. Flat or not flat is the whole test.
  2. Extract the map and look at its eigenvalues. Where the dynamics are nearly linear, fit a linear map to the model's answers and compute ∣λ∣|\lambda|∣λ∣. It predicts the long run before you do it.
  3. Compare against a cheap, structured method. Leapfrog, or the standard structure-preserving scheme for your problem. If the crude classical method holds and the learned one does not, that is your answer.
  4. Separate the discretisation from the model. Run the exact rule through the same pipeline. The gap is the model's contribution; without that control you cannot tell them apart.
  5. Check several random starts. Page 3 found ∣λ∣|\lambda|∣λ∣ on both sides of 1 across eight identical runs. One run tells you about one run.
The cheapest one first

Number 1. It takes one plot and it would have revealed everything on these pages. It is skipped constantly, because the training curve looks healthy and the one-step error is small — and both of those are true and neither is the question.

What a good loss curve does not tell you

Nothing about stability, nothing about conservation, nothing about what happens at step one thousand. A loss is an average over single steps. The failure lives in the compounding, which the average cannot see.

Check 2 is the one worth advertising: a linear probe of a nearly linear system gives you the rollout behaviour in advance.

Honest generalisation

What carries over to real language models

What does carry over

The loop. A large language model generating text is doing the same thing: consuming its own output, step after step. Whatever small bias exists per token compounds over a long generation.

The blind spot. Training scores one token at a time. Anything that only appears after hundreds of tokens — drifting style, a quantity that stops adding up, a constraint silently dropped — is invisible to the objective, exactly as the eigenvalue was here.

The remedy. Measure the thing you care about over the length you care about, rather than trusting a per-step score.

What does not carry over

Text has no conserved quantity, no eigenvalue, and no exact rule to be measured against. The clean linear-algebra argument of page 6 works because the system is a harmonic oscillator, and it does not survive the trip to natural language.

So this set is a precise statement about learned dynamics, and a loose analogy about language. Treat it as the first, and be careful with the second.

The version worth keeping

Ask what a model is supposed to preserve, and then check whether it does, over the horizon you will actually use. Physics gives you conserved quantities to check. Where they exist, use them — they are the sharpest instrument you have.

Being explicit about where the analogy stops is what separates this from the usual "LLMs and physics" argument.

The sentence

A learned time-stepper is a numerical scheme, so judge it as one: not by how accurate a single step is, but by what it conserves over the run you intend to use.
