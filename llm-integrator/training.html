How to ask

Reading a law out of a network

The model does not output a number. It outputs a probability for each of the 64 bins. To turn that into a position, take the mean:

x^n+1=∑kpk x(k),\hat x_{n+1}=\sum_{k} p_k\,x^{(k)},x^n+1​=k∑​pk​x(k),

where x(k)x^{(k)}x(k) is the centre of bin kkk. Now feed it many different pairs (xn−1,xn)(x_{n-1},x_n)(xn−1​,xn​), collect its answers, and fit

x^n+1=a xn+b xn−1\hat x_{n+1}=a\,x_n+b\,x_{n-1}x^n+1​=axn​+bxn−1​

by least squares. If the model has found the physics, aaa should come out at $2\cos(\omega\Delta t)= 1.960133and and andbatexactly at exactly atexactly-1$.

Two numbers, extracted from a network, directly comparable with theory. That is what makes this experiment sharp rather than impressionistic.

Why this is a fair question

We are not asking the model to be linear — we are measuring how linear it turned out to be. The residual of the fit says how much of its behaviour the two-term rule fails to capture, and it is small.

And then one more number

Write the fitted rule as a matrix acting on (xn,xn−1)(x_n,x_{n-1})(xn​,xn−1​). Its eigenvalues λ\lambdaλ decide everything about long runs: ∣λ∣=1|\lambda|=1∣λ∣=1 is a rotation that keeps the amplitude forever, ∣λ∣<1|\lambda|<1∣λ∣<1 spirals in, ∣λ∣>1|\lambda|>1∣λ∣>1 spirals out. The exact rule has ∣λ∣=1|\lambda|=1∣λ∣=1 exactly.

Probing a trained model on a grid of inputs and fitting an interpretable form to the answers works far more widely than this example.

The good news

Within a thousand steps it has the coefficient to about one per cent, from nothing but integers. It was never told the frequency, the sampling interval, or that the numbers came from a physical system at all. Whatever else is true, it found the law.

The bad news

Look at the last column. It never settles on 1. It wanders either side — above means the model would gain energy, below means lose it. Accuracy improves steadily while ∣λ∣|\lambda|∣λ∣ jitters, because the training objective does not know that column exists.

The non-monotonic accuracy between 2,000 and 3,000 steps is real, not a typo: this is a small model on a stochastic objective.

The honest reading

Nothing in "predict the next token" pins the eigenvalue to the unit circle. It lands within about one and a half per cent of it — on whichever side the random start happens to leave it.

So one model will slowly bleed energy and the next will slowly gain it, and you cannot tell which without running it out. Both are wrong, and they are wrong in opposite directions.

Why this matters beyond the toy

Every report of a learned model "discovering physics" should be read with this column in mind. Fitting the law well is a statement about one step. Whether the long run is stable is a different statement, and it is not implied.
