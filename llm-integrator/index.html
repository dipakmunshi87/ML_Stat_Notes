LLM set · one worked example, done to the end

A language model
as an integrator

Sample a mass on a spring at even intervals, round the positions into 64 bins, and train a small GPT to predict the next integer. Never mention Hooke's law, energy, or that the numbers are a trajectory. Then run it forward by feeding it its own output — which is exactly how text is generated, and exactly how an integrator advances.

It learns the law almost exactly. It is still a bad integrator. The gap between those two sentences is worth eight pages, and every number here is computed in your browser while you watch.

Why this is not a metaphor

A mass on a spring, sampled every Δt\Delta tΔt, obeys an exactly linear two-term recurrence — from the cosine addition formula and nothing else:

xn+1=2cos⁡(ωΔt) xn−xn−1.x_{n+1}=2\cos(\omega\Delta t)\,x_n-x_{n-1}.xn+1​=2cos(ωΔt)xn​−xn−1​.

With ω=1\omega=1ω=1 and Δt=0.2\Delta t=0.2Δt=0.2 the coefficient is 1.960133…1.960133\ldots1.960133…, so we know the exact answer to any number of digits and can ask precisely what the model found.

Written as a matrix acting on (xn,xn−1)(x_n,x_{n-1})(xn​,xn−1​), that rule has determinant exactly 111: its eigenvalues sit exactly on the unit circle, so it is a rotation and the amplitude can never change. The model learns a determinant of 0.995260.995260.99526 instead. Over a thousand steps, 0.997631000=0.090.99763^{1000}=0.090.997631000=0.09.

That one line is the whole story, and it is numerical analysis rather than machine learning.

What the model is shown

Eight integers. No units, no time, no mention of a spring, and no hint that they are a trajectory. Its entire task is to name the ninth.

The sentence to remember

A learned time-stepper is a numerical scheme, so judge it as one: not by how accurate a single step is, but by what it conserves over the run you intend to use.

Four results from these pages

It really does learn the law

From integers alone, with no mention of a spring, the browser model recovers the recurrence coefficient as 1.9715 against the exact 1.9601; a model thirty times larger reaches 1.95978 — two parts in ten thousand. It works out for itself that only the last two values matter, and that the relationship between them is linear.

And it is still a bad integrator

Rolled out for a thousand steps — thirty-two periods — its amplitude falls by 44%, and its own rule iterated without rounding falls by 90%. Leapfrog, two lines of arithmetic with no training at all, holds its amplitude to 0.01% over the same run.

One number explains all of it

The exact rule has ∣λ∣=1|\lambda|=1∣λ∣=1 exactly, so it is a rotation and cannot lose amplitude. The learned rule has ∣λ∣=0.99763|\lambda|=0.99763∣λ∣=0.99763. A defect of a quarter of one per cent, raised to the power one thousand, is 0.09 — which is the observed collapse, predicted in advance.

Which way it fails is a coin toss

Across eight identical runs differing only in their random start, ∣λ∣|\lambda|∣λ∣ landed between 0.9757 and 1.0179 — three of the eight outside the circle, so those models gain energy rather than lose it. Nothing in "predict the next token" pins the eigenvalue to 1.

Five habits worth having

Do

  1. Plot a conserved quantity over the longest run you will actually use.
  2. Keep a classical structure-preserving integrator running alongside, from day one.
  3. Fit a linear map to your model's answers and look at its eigenvalues.
  4. Run the exact rule through your own pipeline as a control.
  5. Repeat everything from several random starts before believing it.

Don't

  1. Judge a time-stepper by its one-step error — the failure is in the compounding.
  2. Read a well-fitted coefficient as a well-determined physical quantity.
  3. Assume the discretisation is hurting you; here it was helping.
  4. Expect more parameters to fix a structural defect. They move it, not remove it.
  5. Report one run. The direction of failure changed between runs here.

About this collection

Eight pages, each a single self-contained HTML file. Equations are pre-rendered text; the diagrams are vector graphics; the demonstrations build a small GPT, differentiate it by hand-written reverse-mode automatic differentiation and train it in plain JavaScript while you watch, so every number on screen is computed rather than stored. Everything runs offline. With JavaScript switched off each page becomes one long scrolling document with the notes shown.
