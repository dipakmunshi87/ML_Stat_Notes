Leapfrog is the embarrassing row

Two lines of arithmetic. No training, no data, no parameters. Over thirty-two periods it holds the amplitude to one part in ten thousand, while the model loses nearly half of it.

That is not a close contest, and it does not become one with a bigger model.

Euler is the other lesson

Explicit Euler is the obvious scheme and it is unusable here: it injects energy every step and explodes by eight orders of magnitude. Cheap, simple and first-order is not the problem. Having the wrong structure is.

Euler is included because it fails in the opposite direction, which makes it clear the issue is structural rather than a matter of the error being too big.

The surprise

The rounding is helping it

Compare the last two rows of that table. The model's own fitted rule, iterated in full precision, loses 90% of its amplitude. The model itself — running the same rule but rounding to the nearest bin at every step, as it must — loses 44%.

The tokenisation, which looks like the crude part of the setup, is arresting the decay.

The reason is not mysterious. Rounding is not a small random error sprinkled on top of the dynamics; it is a map that pulls the state back onto the lattice every step. When the underlying drift per step is smaller than half a bin width, the rounding can cancel it entirely — the state is pushed back to where it already was.

So the lattice acts as a crude projection back onto the constraint, which is precisely the trick used to stop constrained integrators drifting. Here it happens by accident, and only partly.

Why this had to be measured separately

If you only ran the model, you would credit it with the 51% rather than the 92%, and conclude that a finer vocabulary would make things better. It would make them worse, by removing the accidental help — while also making the one-step accuracy look better.

Two effects pointing in opposite directions, hidden inside one number. That is why the exact rule is run through the same lattice as a control.

The general warning

Discretisation is part of the dynamics, not a layer of noise on top of it. This is familiar from lattice field theory and from any fixed-point implementation of a filter, and it applies unchanged to a language model over a quantised state.

This is the least intuitive result in the set and the one most worth stating carefully.

The practical form of this

If you are going to use a learned model as a time-stepper, the quantity to plot is not the one-step error. It is a conserved quantity, over the longest run you care about. Nothing shorter will show you the failure, and the failure is the thing that decides whether the model is usable.
