Physics × Machine Learning — finding the unexpected

Anomaly detection
Finding the needle, and the haystack you didn’t expect

An anomaly is a datum that does not fit the pattern of the rest. Detecting them serves two ends that run through all of physics: discovery — the rare object, the new resonance, the transient no one modelled — and quality control — the glitch, the bad detector, the corrupted night. This deck is a pedagogical tour of the methods, the mathematics, the software, and a wide set of examples from astrophysics, cosmology and physics at large.

Three flavours of anomaly

Point — a single odd datum. Contextual — normal in general but odd in its context (a hot day… in winter). Collective — a group that is jointly anomalous (a burst, a streak) though each point looks fine.

How much do you know?

Supervised (labelled anomalies — rare). Semi-supervised / one-class (train on ‘normal’ only). Unsupervised (no labels — the usual case in discovery science).

The core idea

Build a model of ‘normal’, score how far each datum departs from it, and threshold. Every method differs only in how it models normality and measures departure.

The hard part

Anomalies are rare, diverse and unlabelled, so you cannot simply train a classifier. Evaluation, thresholds and the ‘look-elsewhere’ effect all need care — covered here.

One idea, made precise

Let the ‘normal’ data be drawn from a density p(x). An anomaly is a point in a region where p is small — unlikely under the model of normality. The natural anomaly score is therefore the negative log-density, or a surrogate for it:

s(x)  =  − log p(x)    →    flag x as anomalous if   s(x) > τ large score = low probability under normality; τ is a threshold set by a target false-positive rate

Everything downstream is a way to estimate p (or a monotone proxy for it) without ever fully knowing it: fit a parametric density, measure distance to neighbours, count how easily a point is isolated, or learn to reconstruct normal data and watch what fails. The methods differ only in that estimate.

Score, threshold, contamination

Given scores, a threshold turns them into decisions. Below, a live stream of data flows past a threshold (dashed): points whose score crosses it are flagged in red as they arrive — the streaming version of “score, then cut.” Nudge the threshold and watch the false alarms and missed spikes trade off.

If a fraction ν of the data are expected to be anomalous (the contamination), set τ at the (1−ν) quantile of the scores; if not, set τ from the score distribution’s tail (see “Evaluation & thresholds”). The two errors trade off: lower τ catches more real anomalies (higher recall) at the cost of more false alarms.

Two regimes worth separating

Outlier detection: the training set already contains the anomalies (unsupervised); the model must be robust to them. Novelty detection: train on clean ‘normal’ data, then flag anything unlike it (semi-supervised / one-class). Drift / out-of-distribution: the whole distribution shifts — a related but distinct problem with its own tools.

Takeaway. “Anomalous” = “improbable under the model of normal.” Choosing a detector is choosing how to estimate that model — and every choice bakes in an assumption about what ‘normal’ looks like.

Classical statistics: distance from a fitted distribution

1. z-score and robust variants

The oldest score: how many standard deviations from the mean, s(x)=|x−μ|/σ. Because μ and σ are themselves inflated by outliers, use robust estimates — the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD): s(x)=|x−med|/(1.4826 MAD). Grubbs’ test formalises “is the most extreme point an outlier?” for Gaussian data.

2. Mahalanobis distance — the multivariate Gaussian score

For x∼N(μ,Σ), the negative log-density is (up to constants) a quadratic form. That form is the anomaly score:

DM2(x) = (x−μ)T Σ−1 (x−μ)  =  −2 log p(x) + const and under the Gaussian, DM2 ∼ χ2d — so a χ2 p-value gives a calibrated threshold

It is exact when the data really are one Gaussian blob — and misleading when they are not (see the next page’s figure).

3. Density estimates — KDE

Drop the Gaussian assumption and estimate p directly with a kernel density estimate, p̂(x)=(1/n)Σ Kh(x−xi); the score is −log p̂(x). Flexible in low dimensions, but degrades as dimension grows (the curse of dimensionality).

4. Extreme Value Theory — principled tails

Anomalies live in the tail, and the tail has its own universal law. Above a high threshold u, exceedances follow the Generalized Pareto Distribution:

P(X−u ≤ y  |  X>u) = 1 − (1 + ξy/β)−1/ξ fit (ξ,β) to the observed exceedances; extrapolate to get a threshold for any target anomaly rate q

This is the rigorous way to set τ on a stream of scores without assuming Gaussianity (the SPOT / DSPOT algorithms). It answers “how large is ‘too large’?” from the data’s own tail shape.

Geometry and trees: model-free notions of ‘normal’

k-NN distance

Score a point by its distance to its k-th nearest neighbour (or the mean of the k). Points in sparse regions score high. Simple, strong, and parameter-light — but O(n2) naively and blind to varying density.

Local Outlier Factor (LOF)

Fixes varying density by comparing a point’s local reachability density to that of its neighbours:

LOF(x) = meank lrd(xi) / lrd(x)

LOF > 1 means x is in a sparser patch than its neighbours — a local outlier even inside otherwise dense data.

Isolation Forest

Anomalies are easy to isolate: random axis splits separate them in few cuts. With expected path length E[h(x)] and its normaliser c(n)=2H(n−1)−2(n−1)/n,

s(x) = 2− E[h(x)] / c(n)

s→1 = anomalous, s≈0.5 = normal. Fast, scalable, few assumptions — a default workhorse.

One-class SVM & SVDD

Learn a boundary enclosing the ‘normal’ support in feature space:

min ½∥w∥2 + (1/νn)Σξi − ρ

The parameter ν bounds the outlier fraction. SVDD is the equivalent “smallest enclosing sphere” view; Deep SVDD makes the map neural.

Reconstruction on a linear manifold — PCA

If normal data live near a low-dimensional linear subspace, fit it with PCA (top-k directions, projector Pk) and score by the residual off that subspace:

s(x) = ∥ x − Pk x ∥2 large residual = the point does not lie on the learned manifold. Autoencoders (next) make this nonlinear.

Gaussian/Mahalanobis, k-nearest-neighbour distance, Local Outlier Factor, and a kernel density estimate on identical data. The methods disagree precisely where the assumption of ‘normal’ differs.

The Gaussian / Mahalanobis score can only draw one ellipse, so it misreads the V-shape: it swallows the empty middle and misses structure. The k-NN distance, LOF and the kernel density estimate trace the two arms and isolate the scattered points — because they model density locally rather than assuming a single blob.

Pedagogical point. There is no universally best detector — only the one whose built-in idea of ‘normal’ matches your data. Gaussian for a blob; distance- and density-based methods for structured or multi-scale data; tree-based (Isolation Forest) and boundary (one-class SVM) methods at scale; deep methods (next) for images, spectra and time series.

When ‘normal’ is an image, a spectrum, or a light curve

1. Autoencoders — reconstruction error

Train an encoder–decoder to compress and rebuild normal data. Anomalies, unlike anything seen in training, rebuild poorly:

s(x) = ∥ x − g(f(x)) ∥2 encoder f, decoder g trained on normal data; large reconstruction error = anomaly. The nonlinear PCA.

2. Density models — VAEs and normalizing flows

Learn the full density pθ(x) and score by −log pθ(x). Normalizing flows give the likelihood exactly via the change-of-variables formula:

log pθ(x) = log pz(fθ(x)) + log |det ∂fθ/∂x| an invertible f maps data to a simple base density; the Jacobian term makes the likelihood tractable

Deep SVDD / one-class deep nets

Learn a neural map that pulls normal data into a small sphere; distance to its centre is the score. The deep version of one-class SVM.

Generative & diffusion

GAN-based (AnoGAN) and diffusion-based detectors score how hard it is for a generator trained on normal data to produce x — strong for images.

Time series

Forecast the next value from the past (ARIMA, LSTM, transformer) and score the forecast residual; or reconstruct windows. Collective anomalies (bursts, regime changes) show up here.

Self-supervised & OOD

Train a pretext task on normal data; degraded performance flags anomalies. Out-of-distribution detection scores whether new data even come from the training distribution.

3. Likelihood-ratio & simulation-based

The most powerful test between “background only” and “background + something” is the likelihood ratio (Neyman–Pearson). When the likelihood is implicit — only a simulator exists — a classifier trained to separate data from a reference learns that ratio, which is the engine of modern collider anomaly searches.

Anomaly as a deviation from an expected smooth model

In much of physics ‘normal’ is not a cloud of points but a smooth expectation — a falling spectrum, a quiet baseline, a featureless light curve. An anomaly is then a localized departure from it. Fit the smooth model (often from sidebands, to stay blind to the signal region), take the residuals, and ask whether any region deviates more than noise allows.

A mock falling mass spectrum with a smooth background fit from the sidebands. The residual panel reveals a localized 3.4σ excess — a candidate resonance / anomaly.

This is the bump hunt at the heart of new-physics searches, and structurally the same as flagging a fast radio burst over a smooth noise floor, a stellar flare over a quiet light curve, or a spectral line no template predicts.

The look-elsewhere effect

Search enough independent regions and some will fluctuate high by chance. The local significance at the found location overstates the case; the global significance corrects it by the number of independent trials:

pglobal ≈ Ntrials · plocal the “trials factor” — why a 3σ bump found anywhere in a wide scan is far less compelling than 3σ at a predicted place
Discipline of the field. Physics has hard-won machinery for exactly this — blind analysis, sidebands, and the look-elsewhere correction — that the wider anomaly-detection literature is only now formalising.

Particle physics: discovering the Higgs as a bump hunt

The most famous anomaly-detection result in physics is the Higgs boson. In the two-photon channel (H→γγ), the signal is a small Gaussian bump on a large, smooth, falling background of ordinary di-photon events. You cannot see it in a little data — only as luminosity accumulates does the excess at ~125 GeV rise above the noise. Drag the sliders, or press Collect data, and watch the local significance climb toward the 5σ discovery threshold.

Dark bars: observed di-photon events (Poisson). Blue curve: smooth background fit from the sidebands (the signal window is held out). Gold band: the 120–130 GeV signal window. Significance is the counting excess in the window, Z = S/√B.

The real result

On 4 July 2012, ATLAS and CMS independently announced a new boson at ≈125 GeV, each at 5σ local significance, combining H→γγ and H→ZZ→4ℓ with roughly 5–10 fb−1 of 7–8 TeV data. The combined mass is now 125.11 ± 0.11 GeV. The “5σ gold standard” (a chance probability below ~3×10−7) exists precisely because, across a wide mass scan, the look-elsewhere effect makes 3σ bumps common — so particle physics demands 5σ after the trials correction before claiming discovery.

Same machinery, highest stakes. Fit a smooth background, hold out the signal region, measure the localized excess, and convert it to a significance corrected for the look-elsewhere effect. The Higgs is this deck’s bump-hunt page, made real — and worth a Nobel Prize.

How do you know it works — and where to cut?

Metrics under label scarcity

With a few known anomalies you can score a detector’s ranking. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) measures overall separation; but because anomalies are rare, precision–recall (AUPRC) and precision@k — how many of the top-k flagged are real — better reflect the discovery workflow, where a human vets a short list.

Rank, don’t just classify

In discovery science the score’s order matters more than any single threshold: the goal is to put the interesting objects near the top of a list an expert will actually inspect.

Active / human-in-the-loop

Show an expert the top candidates, learn from their yes/no, re-rank. This “personalised” anomaly detection (e.g. Astronomaly) turns a firehose into a manageable, relevant queue.

Setting the threshold

If contamination ν is known, cut at the (1−ν) score quantile. If not, fit the tail of the score distribution with Extreme Value Theory (previous math) and choose τ for a target false-alarm rate — a data-driven cut that adapts to the score’s own tail. For departure-from-model searches, translate the excess into a significance and apply the look-elsewhere correction.

Reality check. Perfect labels don’t exist for the very things you’re trying to discover. Evaluate on injected/synthetic anomalies and on rank quality, and treat any single threshold as provisional.

Anomaly detection as a discovery engine in the sky

Modern surveys produce millions of alerts a night; the discoveries hide in the tails. Anomaly detection is how they are surfaced.

Time-domain transients

ZTF today and Rubin/LSST tomorrow issue millions of alerts nightly. Broker pipelines and dedicated methods rank the novel — SNAD found dozens of real anomalies in supernova catalogues; live deep detectors flag odd extragalactic transients as they rise.

Rare objects in imaging

Outlier algorithms on galaxy images surface mergers, unusual morphologies and genuine oddities — the “weirdest SDSS galaxies” study, and Astronomaly scaled to millions of galaxies.

Spectroscopic oddities

Outliers in spectra flag rare quasars, exotic stars and mislabelled objects. Famously, Boyajian’s star (“Tabby’s star”) surfaced as an anomalous light curve in Kepler citizen-science data.

Gravitational waves

Detector data are riddled with non-Gaussian glitches; Gravity Spy classifies them by citizen science + ML. Unmodelled burst searches look for coherent excess power with no template — anomaly detection for signals.

Cosmology & the CMB

Large-scale-structure and CMB analyses hunt for anomalies — the CMB “Cold Spot” and low-ℓ alignments — and, just as vital, for systematics: foregrounds, bad pixels and survey artefacts masquerading as signal.

Radio: RFI & FRBs

Radio-frequency interference is anomaly detection as data cleaning (the satellite-RFI problem); fast radio bursts are anomaly detection as discovery — a bright, dispersed spike over a noise floor.

Exoplanets

Single-transit events, anomalous transit shapes and self-lensing binaries are outliers against a smooth or periodic light curve — unsupervised searches in Kepler/TESS surface the unusual.

Gaia & stellar populations

Billions of stars: outlier methods pick out rare spectral types, hypervelocity stars and unusual orbits from the bulk stellar distribution.

Beyond astronomy: new physics, plasmas, and machines

Model-independent searches at colliders

Rather than test one theory, ask “is there anything the Standard Model doesn’t predict?” Weakly-supervised methods (CWoLa hunting) and density-based ones (ANODE) turn a resonance search into learned anomaly detection; the LHC Olympics benchmarked the field, and ATLAS/CMS run such searches.

Fusion & plasma physics

Tokamak disruption prediction is anomaly/precursor detection on plasma diagnostics — catching the run-up to a disruption in time to act is critical for ITER-scale devices.

Accelerator & detector health

Beam instrumentation, RF cavities and cryogenics are monitored for faults; anomaly detection on sensor streams gives early warning of equipment failure and data-quality problems.

Condensed matter & materials

Defect and phase-transition detection in microscopy and diffraction; outliers in high-throughput materials data flag interesting or erroneous samples.

Fluid dynamics & turbulence

Rare extreme events — intermittency, coherent structures, rogue waves — are heavy-tailed anomalies where Extreme Value Theory and detection meet.

Experiment monitoring at scale

Any large experiment — neutrino, dark-matter, cosmic-ray — runs online anomaly detection for data quality, vetoing bad periods and surfacing candidate events.

The unifying view. Whether the goal is a Nobel-worthy discovery or a clean dataset, the question is identical: which data do not look like the rest, and is that departure real? Physics brings the added rigour of significance and the look-elsewhere effect.

Software you can actually use

General-purpose (Python)

scikit-learn — IsolationForest, LocalOutlierFactor, OneClassSVM, EllipticEnvelope. PyOD — 50+ outlier detectors under one API. PyThresh — automatic thresholding. alibi-detect — drift & OOD.

Time series & streaming

Merlion, Darts, ADTK, TODS for time-series anomalies; River for online/streaming; the SPOT/DSPOT EVT thresholders for streams.

Deep & density-based

DeepOD — deep anomaly detection; PyGOD — graph anomalies. Normalizing flows: zuko, nflows. Simulation-based inference & likelihood ratios: sbi. EVT tails: pyextremes, scipy.stats.genpareto.

Astronomy-specific

Astronomaly — personalised active anomaly detection. Alert brokers: ANTARES, Lasair, Fink, ALeRCE. astroML; modAL for active learning; SNAD tools.

High-energy physics

The LHC Olympics 2020 dataset and community solutions (CWoLa hunting, ANODE, and many others) are the reference playground for model-independent collider anomaly detection.

Start here. For a first pass on tabular/feature data, scikit-learn or PyOD with Isolation Forest + LOF; for images/spectra/light curves, an autoencoder or a normalizing flow; for streams, River + an EVT threshold; for astronomy at survey scale, Astronomaly plus a broker.
