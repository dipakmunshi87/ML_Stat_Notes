Gaussian processes
Putting error bars on functions

A Gaussian process (GP) is a probability distribution not over numbers but over whole functions. Give it a few noisy data points and it returns not a single best-fit curve but a full posterior: a mean function and a principled uncertainty band that widens where you have no data and pinches shut where you do. It is nonparametric, Bayesian, and analytic — the predictive equations are just linear algebra — and it is the natural language for interpolation, emulation and uncertainty quantification across physics and astronomy.

The one idea

Any finite set of function values is jointly Gaussian. Fix the mean and the covariance function (the kernel), and conditioning on data is just the conditional of a multivariate Gaussian — done in closed form.

What it delivers

A predictive mean with calibrated error bars, a marginal likelihood that tunes its own smoothness (Occam’s razor built in), and a single framework that contains splines, kriging and ridge regression as special cases.

What’s inside

The intuition; the maths of conditioning; the kernel zoo; regression and hyperparameters; a worked example by hand; computation; connections; and dedicated tours of GPs in physics and in astronomy — all with five live widgets you can drive.

Where it’s used

Simulation emulators, Bayesian optimisation, stellar-activity detrending for exoplanets, AGN and time-domain variability, photometric redshifts, and non-parametric reconstruction of the cosmic expansion history.

A function is just a very long vector

Start with the humble multivariate Gaussian. A vector f = (f1,…,fn) is Gaussian if

f ∼ 𝒩(μ, K),    p(f) ∝ exp( −½(f−μ)T K−1(f−μ) ) μi is the mean of component i; Kij is the covariance between components i and j

Now imagine indexing the components not by an integer i but by a continuous input x: write f(x) instead of fi. The mean vector becomes a mean function m(x), and the covariance matrix becomes a covariance function k(x,x′). A function evaluated on a grid of inputs is then just a Gaussian vector — a very long one, one entry per grid point.

f(x) ∼ 𝒢𝒫( m(x),   k(x,x′) ) a Gaussian process: any finite collection { f(x1),…,f(xn) } is a multivariate Gaussian
The definition. A Gaussian process is a (possibly infinite) collection of random variables, any finite subset of which is jointly Gaussian. It is fully specified by two functions: the mean m(x) (usually taken to be 0) and the kernel k(x,x′), which sets how strongly two points move together.

Everything else — drawing sample functions, fitting data, quantifying uncertainty — follows from two facts about Gaussians: they are closed under marginalisation (ignore some components and the rest are still Gaussian) and under conditioning (fix some components and the rest are Gaussian with a shifted mean and shrunk covariance).

What does a “distribution over functions” look like?

Pick a grid of inputs, build the covariance matrix Kij=k(xi,xj), and draw f ∼ 𝒩(0,K). Each draw is a sample function. The kernel controls what these functions look like — how wiggly (the length scale ℓ) and how tall (the amplitude σ). Below, the samples are generated live by a Cholesky factorisation of K, exactly as a real GP library does it.

Sample functions from a GP prior

Five random functions drawn from 𝒩(0,K). Shorten ℓ and they wiggle faster; raise σ and they get taller. Nothing is fitted — this is the prior belief, before any data.

Notice two things. Every sample passes through a cloud of typical height σ, and neighbouring values are correlated over a distance ℓ. The envelope (the grey band at ±2σ) is the marginal uncertainty at each point — constant here, because we have no data yet.

The kernel is the model

Choosing a GP is choosing a covariance function. It encodes every assumption you make about the unknown function — its smoothness, periodicity, and characteristic scales. A few standard kernels (with r = |x−x′|):

RBF: σ2e−r2/2ℓ2  •  Matérn-3/2: σ2(1+√3 r/ℓ)e−√3 r/ℓ  •  Exponential: σ2e−r/ℓ  •  Periodic: σ2e−2sin2(πr/p)/ℓ2 the squared-exponential (RBF) gives infinitely smooth functions; Matérn is rougher; exponential is the roughest (Markov); periodic repeats with period p

Top: three prior samples for the chosen kernel. Bottom: the kernel itself, k(0,r) — how correlation falls with separation. The smoothness of the samples is dictated by the shape of k near r=0.

What controls smoothness: the Matérn family

The Matérn kernel has a smoothness parameter ν: the sample functions are ⌈ν⌉−1 times differentiable. ν=1/2 is the exponential (continuous but nowhere differentiable — a Markov process); ν=3/2 and 5/2 are the popular “once” and “twice differentiable” choices used when the RBF’s infinite smoothness is unrealistically strong; ν→∞ recovers the RBF. Choosing ν is choosing how rough you believe the truth to be — a real modelling decision, not a technicality.

Kernels are Lego

Sums and products of valid kernels are valid kernels (a sum is an OR of explanations, a product an AND), which is how you build structured models:

  • periodic × RBF → a quasi-periodic signal that repeats but slowly drifts — exactly the stellar-activity kernel.
  • long-RBF + short-RBF → a slow trend plus fast fluctuations, each with its own scale, cleanly separated.
  • RBF + constant + white → signal, an unknown offset, and independent measurement noise — the everyday regression kernel.
  • ARD (automatic relevance determination): give each input dimension its own length scale ℓd; the fitted ℓd reveal which inputs matter (large ℓd ⇒ that feature is irrelevant), a built-in feature selection.

Two more staples: the rational quadratic σ2(1+r2/2αℓ2)−α is an infinite scale-mixture of RBFs (good when several length scales coexist), and the linear kernel σ2x x′ is non-stationary and reproduces Bayesian linear regression. The design of the kernel is where the physics goes in — it is the single most important modelling choice in a GP.

Fitting data is just conditioning a Gaussian

Here is the whole of GP regression. We have training inputs X with noisy outputs y = f(X)+ε, ε∼𝒩(0,σn2), and we want f at test inputs X∗. Because training and test values are jointly Gaussian,

❝ y, f∗ ❞ ∼ 𝒩❝ 0,   [ K(X,X)+σn2I   K(X,X∗) ;   K(X∗,X)   K(X∗,X∗) ] ❞ the joint prior over observed data and unknown test values

Applying the Gaussian conditioning formula gives the posterior over f∗ directly — a mean and a covariance, in closed form:

ƒ̅∗ = K(X∗,X) [K+σn2I]−1y
cov(f∗) = K(X∗,X∗) − K(X∗,X) [K+σn2I]−1K(X,X∗) the predictive mean is a weighted average of the data; the variance is the prior variance minus what the data explain
Read the two lines. The mean is linear in y — a smoothing of the observations by kernel weights. The covariance does not depend on y at all: uncertainty is set purely by where you sampled, shrinking near data and returning to the prior far from it. This is the signature of a GP: honest error bars that know what they don’t know.

The marginal likelihood tunes the model itself

The kernel has hyperparameters — length scale ℓ, amplitude σ, noise σn. We do not fit them by cross-validation; the GP scores them with the marginal likelihood (the evidence), the probability of the data with the function integrated out:

log p(y|θ) = −½yT[Kθ+σn2I]−1y − ½log|Kθ+σn2I| − (n/2)log2π data-fit term  −  complexity penalty  −  constant

The two terms fight. The first rewards fitting the data; the second, the log-determinant, penalises complexity — a short length scale makes K flexible but raises |K| and is charged for it. Maximising the sum automatically finds the smoothness the data actually support: Occam’s razor, quantified.

Occam’s razor live — slide the length scale

A fixed data set. Left: the GP fit at the current ℓ. Right: log marginal likelihood versus ℓ, with your position marked. Too small → overfits and is penalised; too large → underfits; the peak is the evidence-optimal ℓ.

In practice one maximises log p(y|θ) over all hyperparameters by gradient ascent (the gradients are analytic). The evidence can be multimodal — a short-ℓ “explain-it-all-as-noise” solution and a long-ℓ “smooth-signal” solution — which is itself diagnostic.

Three points, one prediction — all the numbers

Take the RBF kernel k(x,x′)=exp(−(x−x′)2/2) (so ℓ=σ=1) with a little noise σn2=0.01. Three observations:

(x1,y1)=(−2,−1),   (x2,y2)=(0, 2),   (x3,y3)=(2,−1)

Build the covariance matrix. With k(xi,xj)=exp(−(xi−xj)2/2): k(−2,0)=e−2≈0.135, k(−2,2)=e−8≈0.0003, k(0,2)≈0.135, so

K+σn2I = [ 1.01   0.135   0.000 ;   0.135   1.01   0.135 ;   0.000   0.135   1.01 ]

Solve (K+σn2I)α=y for the weight vector: α ≈ (−1.30, 2.33, −1.30)T. Now predict at x∗=1. The test-to-train covariances are k∗=(e−4.5,e−0.5,e−0.5)≈(0.011,0.607,0.607), so

ƒ̅(1) = k∗Tα ≈ 0.61,    var(1) = 1 − k∗T(K+σn2I)−1k∗ ≈ 0.35  ⇒  σ(1) ≈ 0.59 a prediction of 0.61 ± 0.59 at x=1 — pulled between the y=2 point at x=0 and the y=−1 point at x=2
That is the entire algorithm. Build K, solve one linear system for α (the “training”), then every prediction is a dot product k∗Tα for the mean and one more solve for the variance. Three one-line calculations that build the intuition

All use the RBF kernel k(x,x′)=σ2e−(x−x′)2/2ℓ2 with ℓ=σ=1 and zero prior mean. With a single observation the predictive formulas collapse to something you can evaluate in your head.

Example 1 — one noiseless point: interpolation

Observe f(0)=1.5 exactly (σn=0). The predictive equations reduce to just the two kernel values k(x,0) and k(0,0)=1:

ƒ̅(x)= [k(x,0)/k(0,0)]×1.5 = 1.5 e−x2/2,    var(x)= k(0,0) − k(x,0)2/k(0,0) = 1 − e−x2 evaluate at a few x: (0)→1.50±0.00, (1)→0.91±0.79, (2)→0.20±0.98, (∞)→0.00±1.00

Read it off: at the data point the mean is exactly 1.5 and the variance is zero — a noiseless GP interpolates. Move away and the prediction decays back to the prior mean 0 over a distance ℓ, while the uncertainty grows back to the prior σ=1. That single line is the whole behaviour of a GP in miniature.

Example 2 — one noisy point: shrinkage (this is ridge regression)

Now allow observation noise σn2=0.25. The denominator picks up the noise:

ƒ̅(x)= [k(x,0)/(k(0,0)+σn2)]×1.5 = 1.2 e−x2/2,    var(x)= 1 − k(x,0)2/1.25 at x=0: mean=1.2 (not 1.5!) and var=1−1/1.25=0.20, so σ(0)=0.45 — the point is no longer hit exactly

Two things changed. The estimate at the data point is shrunk from 1.5 toward the prior mean 0 (to 1.2), because the GP now distrusts a noisy measurement; and the variance there is no longer zero. This shrinkage is exactly the ridge-regression / James–Stein effect — the noise plays the role of the ridge penalty.

Example 3 — two points: correlation carries information

Observe f(0)=1 and f(0.5)=1.2 (noiseless). Since k(0,0.5)=e−0.125=0.882, the covariance and its solution are

K = [ 1   0.882 ;   0.882   1 ],    α = K−1y = (−0.27,  1.44)T

Predict at x∗=1, where k∗=(e−0.5,e−0.125)=(0.607,0.882):

ƒ̅(1)=k∗Tα≈1.11,    σ(1)≈0.30 the two points define a gentle upward slope, and the GP extrapolates it a little way — then reverts to the prior mean beyond ∼ℓ from the data
The pattern in all three. The mean is a kernel-weighted blend of the observations; noiseless data are hit exactly and noisy data are shrunk; the variance depends only on where you sampled, collapsing at data and returning to the prior far away. Everything the widgets do is these formulas with more points.

The cost, and how to beat it

The bottleneck is the term [K+σn2I]−1. In practice one never inverts; one takes the Cholesky factor K+σn2I=LLT and solves triangular systems. That costs O(n3) time and O(n2) memory — fine for n up to a few thousand, prohibitive at n=105+.

α = LT\(L\y),   log|K+σn2I| = 2∑ilog Lii train once (the factorisation), then predictions and the marginal likelihood are cheap

Sparse / inducing points

Represent the whole data set by m ℒ n inducing points u=f(Z) and only ever handle the m×m covariance. FITC (Snelson–Ghahramani 2006) does this with an approximate likelihood; the variational approach (Titsias 2009) instead maximises a rigorous lower bound on the marginal likelihood, choosing Z to lose as little information as possible. Cost drops from O(n3) to O(nm2), and stochastic variational GPs (Hensman 2013) push it to mini-batches and millions of points — the workhorse for large catalogues.

Structured kernels

When the covariance has special structure the solves get cheap. On a regular grid K is Toeplitz/Kronecker and matrix–vector products are O(n log n) by FFT. The celerite family uses semiseparable kernels (sums of damped cosines) whose Cholesky is exactly O(n) in 1-D — the breakthrough that made GP fits to million-epoch light curves and asteroseismic time series routine rather than heroic.

Iterative & GPU

Avoid the factorisation entirely: solve (K+σn2I)α=y by conjugate gradients, needing only fast matrix–vector products, and estimate the log-determinant with stochastic Lanczos quadrature (Hutchinson trace estimator). With kernel-matrix–vector products computed on the fly on a GPU (KeOps, GPyTorch), exact GPs reach 105–106 points without ever forming K. Preconditioning (a partial pivoted Cholesky) makes CG converge in a handful of iterations.

Conditioning & the jitter

When two inputs nearly coincide, or ℓ is large, K becomes numerically singular (its condition number explodes) and the Cholesky fails. A tiny diagonal jitter — add 10−6…10−8 to the diagonal, formally a minute white-noise term — restores positive-definiteness at negligible cost. It is exactly what these in-browser widgets add before every factorisation. Always work with the Cholesky factor, never an explicit inverse.

The training/prediction split. Everything expensive — the O(n3) Cholesky and the O(n2) storage — happens once. Afterwards each posterior mean is an O(n) dot product and each variance an O(n2) solve, and the marginal likelihood and its gradients come almost for free from the same factor, which is why hyperparameter optimisation is cheap.

What GPs contain as special cases

Linear / ridge regression

Bayesian linear regression f(x)=wTφ(x) with a Gaussian prior w∼𝒩(0,Σp) is a GP with kernel k(x,x′)=φ(x)TΣpφ(x′). Its posterior mean is the ridge estimate ƒ̅=Φ(ΦTΦ+λI)−1… = the kernel-ridge solution K(K+λI)−1y, with λ=σn2. The kernel trick then lets φ be infinite-dimensional (RBF = infinitely many Gaussian bumps) while you only ever compute the n×n matrix K. The GP gives the same point estimate and a variance ridge never provides.

Splines & kriging

The cubic smoothing spline that minimises ∑(yi−f(xi))2+λ∫f″(x)2dx is exactly the posterior mean of a GP whose kernel is that of the (twice-)integrated Wiener process; the roughness penalty is the log-prior. Kriging in geostatistics is GP regression under another name (Matheron, 1960s): “ordinary kriging” = GP with unknown constant mean, and the kriging variance map is the GP predictive variance. Radial-basis-function and thin-plate-spline interpolation are the noiseless limits.

The neural-network limit

Neal (1996) proved that a one-hidden-layer network with i.i.d. random weights and infinitely many units converges to a GP by the central limit theorem; the induced kernel is computable in closed form (the arccosine kernel for ReLU). The same holds layer-by-layer for deep nets — the NNGP kernel — and the training dynamics of a wide net under gradient descent are governed by the Neural Tangent Kernel (Jacot 2018), so a wide network is kernel regression. GPs are thus the analytic lens on deep learning’s infinite-width limit.

Random fields & the power spectrum

A GP over 2- or 3-D space is a Gaussian random field — the primordial density field of cosmology. The kernel is the real-space two-point correlation function ξ(r), and by the Wiener–Khinchin theorem its Fourier transform is the power spectrum P(k). Bochner’s theorem makes this exact: a stationary function is a valid covariance iff its Fourier transform is a non-negative measure — the spectral density. So “choose a kernel” and “choose a power spectrum” are the same act, and the RBF kernel is a Gaussian P(k).

One framework, many names. Ridge regression, kernel machines, splines, kriging, random fields and wide neural networks are all the same Gaussian object viewed through different kernels — and, via Bochner, through different power spectra. The linear kernel is Bayesian linear regression

Take the model f(x)=w0+w1x with a Gaussian prior on the weights, w∼𝒩(0,σp2I). Marginalising w, the function values are Gaussian with covariance

k(x,x′)=σp2(1+x x′) the “linear” kernel — a GP whose sample functions are straight lines

Worked example

Fit four points (−1,−0.8), (0,0.2), (1,1.1), (2,1.6). With design matrix X (columns [1,x]) and noise σn2, the posterior over w is Gaussian with

Σpost=(σn−2XTX+σp−2I)−1,   μpost=σn−2ΣpostXTy

which is exactly the ridge estimate with λ=σn2/σp2. For these numbers the posterior mean line is y≈0.12+0.81x. But the GP gives more than a line: the predictive variance at input x is

var(x)=[1,x] Σpost [1,x]T = Σ00+2xΣ01+x2Σ11 quadratic in x — the error band is a funnel, narrowest at the data centroid and widening as you extrapolate

Posterior over lines — click to add data

Each faint line is a sample from the posterior over (w0,w1); blue is the mean (the ridge fit); the shaded band is ±2σ. Watch it funnel outward — the honest uncertainty ordinary least-squares hides behind flat error bars.

The point. Bayesian linear regression is the simplest GP. The kernel trick then swaps the two features [1,x] for infinitely many (an RBF is infinitely many Gaussian bumps), and everything — the ridge mean, the funnel variance — carries straight over.

Smoothing splines and kriging are GPs in disguise

The cubic smoothing spline is the function minimising a fit term plus a roughness penalty:

ƒ̂ = argminf ∑i(yi−f(xi))2 + λ∫ f″(x)2 dx data fit  +  λ × wiggliness

Worked correspondence

Compare with the GP posterior, whose negative log density is ½σn−2∑(yi−fi)2+½fTK−1f. Term by term:

λ∫f″2dx  ↔  fTK−1f   (the roughness penalty is the log-prior)

The roughness penalty ∫f″2 corresponds to a specific kernel (that of the twice-integrated Wiener process); the spline is then the posterior mean of that GP, and λ=σn2. The representer theorem guarantees the minimiser is a sum of kernel functions centred on the data — which is precisely ƒ̅(x)=k∗T(K+λI)−1y. And kriging, the geostatistician’s interpolator (Matheron, 1960s), is this same GP regression; its “estimation-variance map” is the GP predictive variance.

One knob, two limits — slide the smoothing λ

The blue curve is simultaneously the smoothing spline and the GP posterior mean of the same fixed data. As λ→0 it interpolates every point (a rough spline); as λ grows it stiffens toward a straight line. The band is the kriging variance.

The point. “Fit a smoothing spline”, “krige the field” and “take a GP posterior mean” are three names for one computation. The GP viewpoint adds what the other two lack: a full posterior, hence calibrated error bars and a marginal likelihood to choose λ itself.

An infinitely wide neural network is a Gaussian process

Consider a one-hidden-layer network with M units, f(x)=(1/√M)∑jvj h(wjx+bj), with all weights drawn i.i.d. from a zero-mean prior. This is Neal’s celebrated observation (1996).

Worked argument

The output is a sum of M i.i.d. random terms, so by the central limit theorem it becomes Gaussian as M→∞. Its mean is 0 and its covariance is independent of M:

Cov(f(x),f(x′)) = 𝔼w,b[ h(wx+b) h(wx′+b) ] ≡ k(x,x′) for ReLU units this integral is the closed-form arccosine kernel; the network output is a GP sample

So a wide random network draws functions from a GP with a specific kernel — the NNGP kernel. Stacking layers composes the kernel; and the way a wide network trains under gradient descent is governed by a related fixed kernel, the Neural Tangent Kernel (Jacot 2018), so a wide network learning by gradient descent is doing kernel regression.

Watch random networks converge to a GP

Widen the network with the slider: few units give lumpy, non-Gaussian functions; many units give a smooth Gaussian band and a bell-shaped histogram — a GP.

The point. The infinite-width limit turns deep networks into GPs, giving an analytic handle on why over-parametrised nets generalise and linking modern deep learning back to kernel methods.

A kernel is a power spectrum — Bochner’s theorem

A GP over space is a Gaussian random field — the object of the primordial density field in cosmology. For a stationary field the kernel depends only on separation, k(r), and two theorems tie it to the power spectrum:

Bochner: k(r) is a valid covariance ⇔ its Fourier transform S(k)≥0.    Wiener–Khinchin: P(k)=∫ k(r) e−i k r dr the spectral density S(k)=P(k) is non-negative — that is exactly what makes k a legal kernel

Worked pair

The Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian, so the RBF kernel and its spectrum are a matched pair:

k(r)=e−r2/2ℓ2  ↔  P(k) ∝ e−ℓ2k2/2 short correlation ℓ ⇒ broad spectrum ⇒ power at high k ⇒ small-scale structure; long ℓ ⇒ narrow spectrum ⇒ large blobs

Kernel ↔ spectrum, live

Left: a 2-D Gaussian random field (a GP over the plane), flowing as its Fourier modes drift. Right: the power spectrum P(k) it is drawn from. Change the correlation length and watch both respond in lock-step — Bochner’s theorem in motion.

The point. “Choose a kernel” and “choose a power spectrum” are the same act. The correlation function a cosmologist measures is a GP kernel, and its Fourier transform is the P(k) that structure-formation theory predicts — the deepest instance of the GP idea in physics.

Where GPs earn their keep in physics

Emulators / surrogates

A first-principles simulation — lattice QCD, a climate model, a hydro or N-body run — may cost days per evaluation, far too slow for an MCMC that needs 106 of them. Train a GP on a few hundred runs placed by a space-filling Latin-hypercube design and it predicts the output anywhere in parameter space in microseconds, with a calibrated error bar. That variance drives active learning: run the next expensive simulation exactly where the emulator is least certain. This is how the Coyote and Mira–Titan cosmic emulators and countless engineering surrogates are built.

Bayesian optimisation

To optimise a costly black box — a detector geometry, an accelerator setting, a fusion pulse, a hyperparameter — fit a GP to the evaluations so far and pick the next point by maximising an acquisition function: expected improvement EI, upper confidence bound μ(x)+κσ(x), or probability of improvement. Each balances exploiting the current best against exploring where σ(x) is large. A handful of evaluations often suffice where grid search would need thousands.

Latent force models

Put a GP prior on the unknown forcing u(t) of a linear ODE/PDE, ℒf=u. Because a linear operator acting on a GP yields another GP, the observable f is itself a GP whose kernel is the Green’s-function convolution of the forcing kernel. Data on f then infer the hidden input u — mechanistic physics and data-driven inference fused in one Gaussian model (Álvarez, Luengo & Lawrence). The same trick imposes physical constraints (divergence-free fields, conservation laws) by building them into the kernel.

Interpolating potentials / PES

Machine-learned interatomic potentials (GAP, Bartók et al.) write the total energy as a sum of local atomic energies, each a GP regression over a rotation/permutation-invariant descriptor (SOAP) of the neighbourhood, trained on quantum-chemistry (DFT) points. Forces are analytic derivatives of the GP mean, and the predictive variance flags configurations off the training manifold — telling you which new structures to compute ab initio next.

Uncertainty quantification

To propagate an uncertain input distribution p(θ) through an expensive code, push it through a GP emulator: the output uncertainty then has two, separable parts — the spread from p(θ) and the emulator’s own σ(x). Sensitivity indices (Sobol’) and calibration against experiment (the Kennedy–O’Hagan framework, with a GP discrepancy term) are standard in inertial-confinement fusion, materials discovery and detector modelling.

Solving & discovering PDEs

Place a GP prior on the solution of a differential equation and condition on the equation holding at collocation points: the linear-operator trick again makes everything Gaussian, so you solve the PDE and get a posterior variance on the answer (probabilistic numerics; Raissi, Perdikaris & Karniadakis). Run it backwards and the same machinery discovers the operator — a probabilistic cousin of PINNs that returns honest error bars.

The common thread. Physics reaches for a GP whenever evaluations are expensive and honest error bars matter — to emulate, to optimise, to interpolate, to propagate uncertainty, and to say clearly where the model should not be trusted. Emulating an expensive simulation — and choosing the next run

A first-principles code may cost hours per evaluation. Train a GP on a handful of runs and it predicts the output everywhere in microseconds with error bars. The variance is the payoff: it says where to spend the next expensive run — active learning picks the input of maximum predictive uncertainty.

xnext = argmaxx σ(x)   (“most uncertain point”),    σ(x)2 = k(x,x) − k∗T(K+σn2I)−1k∗ evaluate the true code at xnext, add it, refit — the band collapses fastest where it was widest

Optimising a costly black box in a handful of evaluations

To maximise an expensive function — a detector design, an accelerator tune, a fusion pulse — fit a GP to the evaluations so far and pick the next input by maximising an acquisition function that balances exploiting the current best against exploring where the GP is uncertain:

UCB: α(x)=μ(x)+κ σ(x)  •  EI: α(x)=𝔼[max(f(x)−fbest,0)] evaluate where α peaks; large σ drives exploration, large μ drives exploitation

Inferring the hidden force behind a differential equation

Put a GP prior on the unknown forcing u(t) of a linear ODE, ℒf=u. A linear operator acting on a GP gives another GP, so the observable f is itself a GP whose kernel is the forcing kernel convolved twice with the Green’s function G:

ℒf = u,   u ∼ 𝒢𝒫(0,ku)  ⇒  f ∼ 𝒢𝒫(0, G∗ku∗G) data on the output f then infer the latent input u — mechanistic physics fused with data-driven inference

A forcing and its response, live

Top (red): a random latent forcing u(t) drawn from a GP. Bottom (blue): the response x(t) of the ODE x′=−x/τ+u — a smoothed, lagged version of the forcing, and itself a Gaussian process. Change the response time τ.

The point. Latent force models let you keep the physics (the operator ℒ) and be data-driven about what you don’t know (the forcing) — and the same construction imposes hard constraints like divergence-free fields by building them into the kernel.

Machine-learned force fields from quantum chemistry

Potentials like GAP write the total energy as a sum of local atomic energies, each a GP regression over a symmetry-invariant descriptor of the neighbourhood, trained on expensive DFT points. Because the derivative of a GP mean is analytic, the forces come for free, and the predictive variance flags configurations off the training manifold.

E(x) = GP mean,    F(x) = −dE/dx = −(d/dx) k∗T(K+σn2I)−1y the force is another Gaussian process; large σ means “compute a new DFT point here”

A potential-energy surface, learned — click to add DFT points

A double-well potential (red dashed = truth). The GP energy (blue) and its band come from a few points; the green curve is the force −dE/dx, the analytic derivative of the GP. Click to add a quantum-chemistry point and watch both sharpen.

The point. One GP delivers energies, forces and a “trust map” in a single object — which is why active-learned GP potentials now drive large-scale molecular-dynamics simulations at near-DFT accuracy.

Pushing an uncertain input through the model

Given an uncertain input θ∼p(θ), what is the uncertainty on the output? Push the distribution through a GP emulator. To first order the output variance separates into a propagated-input part and the emulator’s own part:

var(y) ≈ [m′(θ̄)]2 σθ2  +  σemu2(θ̄) where the emulator is steep, input uncertainty is amplified; where it is untrained, its own σ dominates

Propagate a distribution through the emulator

The blue curve is the emulator y=m(θ); the red bump on the axis is the input p(θ). Monte-Carlo samples pushed through give the output distribution p(y) on the right. Slide the input mean into a steep region and watch the output spread grow.

The point. Emulator-based uncertainty quantification underlies calibration (Kennedy–O’Hagan, with a GP discrepancy term) and sensitivity analysis in fusion, climate and detector modelling — and it keeps the two sources of error cleanly separate.

A Gaussian process can solve the equation itself

Put a GP prior on the solution f and condition on the equation holding at a set of collocation points. Because a linear differential operator maps a GP to a GP, the pair (f, ℒf) is jointly Gaussian — with cross-covariances that are derivatives of the kernel — so conditioning on ℒf=g and the boundary values yields a posterior that is the solution, with error bars (probabilistic numerics).

f ∼ 𝒢𝒫(0,k),   condition on   f′(xj)=g(xj)  and  f(0)=0 here ℒ=d/dx and g(x)=cos x, whose solution is sin x — recovered below purely by conditioning

Solving f′(x)=cos x, f(0)=0 by GP collocation

The GP is conditioned on the derivative equalling cos x at the green collocation points and on f(0)=0. Its posterior mean (blue) reconstructs the true solution sin x (red dashed); add points and the band collapses onto it. No time-stepping — just conditioning a Gaussian.

The point. Framing a solver as GP inference returns a distribution over solutions — honest uncertainty from discretisation — and, run in reverse, the same machinery discovers the governing operator from data, a probabilistic cousin of PINNs.

Astronomy runs on Gaussian processes — starting with time

Astronomical light curves and radial-velocity (RV) series are noisy, gappy, and contaminated by stellar signals that are not the thing you want. GPs are the standard tool for modelling the nuisance and the signal together, with uncertainty.

Stellar activity & exoplanets

Star spots rotate in and out of view, injecting a coherent-but-drifting signal that mimics or masks a planet in RV and photometry. The standard model is the quasi-periodic kernel k(τ)=A exp[−τ2/2ℓ2 − Γsin2(πτ/Prot)]: the sine term repeats at the rotation period Prot, the exponential lets the pattern evolve over a spot-lifetime ℓ. Fitting it recovers Prot and lets the planet’s Keplerian be inferred underneath — now routine in RV searches for small planets.

Transit detrending

Kepler/TESS/JWST light curves carry instrumental and stellar systematics on the very scale of the transit. Fitting the transit after detrending double-counts and biases the depth; instead the GP correlated-noise model is fit jointly with the transit shape (via exoplanet/PyMC), so the planet radius, its covariances with limb-darkening, and the true error bars all come out together — critical for the shallow transits and transmission spectra JWST now measures.

AGN & the damped random walk

Quasar optical variability is described remarkably well by a GP with an exponential kernel k(τ)=σ2e−|τ|/τd — the damped random walk, a Gaussian Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, equivalently CARMA(1,0). Its amplitude σ and damping timescale τd correlate with black-hole mass and luminosity, so they are physical diagnostics used to classify variables and reject them from transient searches across ZTF and (soon) LSST. Richer CARMA/DHO kernels capture the higher-frequency structure.

celerite & scalable light curves

A general 1-D GP costs O(n3) — hopeless for a million-epoch light curve. celerite (Foreman-Mackey et al. 2017) restricts to kernels that are sums of damped cosines, e−cτ[a cos(dτ)+b sin(dτ)], whose covariance is semiseparable and whose Cholesky is exactly O(n). That single trick made GP modelling of asteroseismic oscillations, granulation, and long survey light curves an everyday tool.

Why a GP and not a parametric model? Because stellar and instrumental noise is correlated in time, and ignoring the correlation gives error bars that are wildly overconfident — a spurious planet at 3σ that is really 1σ. The GP models the covariance directly, so the detection significance is honest. The next four pages take each in turn, with a worked example and a live animation.

The quasi-periodic kernel — modelling star spots

A rotating star carries spots that come and go, producing a signal that repeats at the rotation period but slowly evolves as spots form and decay. No pure sinusoid captures this; the quasi-periodic kernel does — a periodic kernel multiplied by a slow squared-exponential:

k(τ) = A exp[ −τ2/2ℓ2 − Γ sin2(πτ/Prot) ] Prot sets the repeat, ℓ the spot lifetime (how fast it drifts), Γ the within-period wiggliness

A rotating, spotted star — live

A GP sample from the quasi-periodic kernel: it repeats every Prot but the shape drifts over a few rotations (the spot lifetime ℓ). This is the signal a GP subtracts to reveal a planet underneath in radial-velocity data.

The point. Fitting the quasi-periodic GP jointly with a Keplerian recovers the rotation period and the planet, and is now the default treatment of stellar activity in radial-velocity planet searches.

Cleaning a transit out of correlated systematics

A Kepler/TESS/JWST light curve is a shallow transit dip buried in slow instrumental and stellar drifts of comparable size. Detrending then fitting biases the depth; the honest approach models the correlated noise with a GP and the transit together. The idea in miniature: mask the transit, learn the drift with a GP, and subtract it — the clean transit emerges with correct error bars.

flux(t) = transit(t) + drift(t) + noise,    drift ∼ 𝒢𝒫(0,k) the GP absorbs the correlated drift so it cannot be mistaken for a change in transit depth

Raw light curve → GP-detrended

Top: the observed flux — a transit lost inside a red correlated drift. Bottom: after subtracting the GP model of the drift, the clean transit (blue = truth) stands out. Raise the systematic amplitude and the naive eye loses the transit while the GP still finds it.

The point. Because the GP and the transit are fit together, the recovered planet radius and its uncertainty are unbiased — essential for the shallow transits and transmission spectra JWST now measures.

Quasar variability as a Gaussian process

The optical brightness of an accreting quasar wanders stochastically. It is described remarkably well by a GP with an exponential kernel — the damped random walk (DRW), a Gaussian Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process:

k(Δt) = σ2 e−|Δt|/τd  ⇔  dx = −(x/τd) dt + √(2σ2/τd) dW two parameters: the long-term amplitude σ and the damping timescale τd

A quasar light curve, streaming

A DRW light curve generated live as an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, scrolling in time. Short τd gives fast, forgetful flicker; long τd gives slow, correlated swings. The amplitude and timescale are the physical diagnostics fit to survey data.

The point. Because the DRW is a GP, its likelihood is exact and cheap, so σ and τd are measured for millions of quasars — classifying variables and rejecting them from transient searches across ZTF and LSST.

Scalable GPs: the driven damped oscillator

A general GP on a light curve costs O(N3) — hopeless at a million epochs. celerite restricts to kernels that are sums of damped cosines, for which the Cholesky is exactly O(N). The workhorse member is the stochastically-driven damped harmonic oscillator (SHO), whose power spectrum is a tunable peak — ideal for stellar oscillations and granulation:

S(ω) ∝ ω04 / [ (ω2−ω02)2 + ω02ω2/Q2 ] ω0 is the oscillation frequency, Q the quality factor (how sharp/long-lived the mode is)

An oscillation and its power spectrum

Left: a driven damped-oscillator time series (an asteroseismic mode plus noise). Right: its power spectrum — a Lorentzian peak at ω0 whose width is set by Q. High Q gives a long-lived, sharp mode; low Q gives broadband granulation-like noise.

The point. Restricting the kernel to this semiseparable family buys a factor of N2 in speed, turning GP modelling of Kepler/TESS asteroseismology and long survey light curves from impossible into routine.

Non-parametric cosmology and beyond

Reconstructing the expansion history

Rather than assume a dark-energy model, put a GP prior directly on H(z) and condition on supernovae, cosmic chronometers and BAO (GaPP, Seikel et al. 2012). Because a derivative of a GP is another GP, you also get H′(z) and the Om(z) diagnostic with error bands — a model-independent test of ΛCDM and a probe of the Hubble tension. The caveat, worth stating: the result depends on the kernel and its length-scale prior, so robustness to that choice must always be checked.

Photometric redshifts

With spectra for only a sliver of the billions of galaxies imaged, redshift must be inferred from broadband colours. GP regression (e.g. GPz) maps colours→redshift and, crucially, returns a per-object variance that widens in sparse regions of colour space — and a heteroscedastic version models the input-dependent noise, so the redshift PDFs that feed weak-lensing and clustering analyses are properly calibrated.

Emulating Boltzmann codes

CAMB/CLASS take seconds per evaluation; an MCMC needs millions. A GP emulator trained on a few thousand runs predicts the matter power spectrum or CMB Cℓ across parameter space in milliseconds with controlled error, accelerating inference by orders of magnitude. GP emulators underlie the Coyote, EuclidEmulator and Mira–Titan power-spectrum predictions; today’s neural emulators (CosmoPower) are the same idea scaled up, but the GP versions still win when training data are scarce and error bars matter.

Primordial power spectrum

Put a GP prior on the primordial spectrum 𝒫ℛ(k) — its length scale is literally a prior on how much the spectrum may wiggle — and reconstruct it from CMB and large-scale-structure data without assuming the near-scale-invariant power law. It is a principled, uncertainty-aware search for inflationary features (steps, oscillations) that a fixed ns parametrisation would erase by construction.

A Gaussian random field, live — the cosmologist’s GP

A GP over 2-D space is a Gaussian random field: exactly the object of the primordial density field. The kernel sets the correlation length — the size of the blobs. Slide it and watch structure coarsen; the field flows because each Fourier mode drifts in phase.

Same maths, two scales. The kernel that smooths a stellar light curve and the two-point function that describes the cosmic web are the same Gaussian object — a covariance that says how strongly two points move together. The next four pages take the cosmological uses in turn, each with a live animation.

Model-independent expansion history

Instead of assuming a dark-energy model, put a GP prior on H(z) and condition on the data — supernovae, cosmic chronometers, BAO. The reconstruction comes with error bands, and because a derivative of a GP is a GP, you also get H′(z) and the Om(z) diagnostic, a null test of ΛCDM.

H(z) ∼ 𝒢𝒫(HΛCDM, k),    Om(z) ≡ [ (H/H0)2 − 1 ] / [ (1+z)3 − 1 ] Om(z) is constant (=Ωm) for flat ΛCDM — any tilt is new physics

Reconstruct H(z) from data — add measurements

Red points are (noisy) H(z) measurements; blue is the GP reconstruction with its ±2σ band; the dashed line is the true ΛCDM curve. Add data and the band tightens. The reconstruction never assumed a dark-energy model.

The point. GP reconstruction turns a handful of noisy distance and chronometer measurements into a smooth expansion history with honest uncertainty — a data-driven test of dark energy and a fresh angle on the Hubble tension. (The one caveat: results depend on the kernel and its length-scale prior, which must be checked.)

Redshift from colour, with a calibrated error bar

Spectra exist for a sliver of the billions of galaxies imaged, so redshift must be inferred from broadband colours. GP regression maps colour→redshift and — crucially — returns a per-object uncertainty that widens where training galaxies are sparse. That calibrated variance is what downstream lensing and clustering analyses need.

z(colour) ∼ 𝒢𝒫(m,k),    σz(colour) = predictive std — large where training data are thin the error bar per galaxy, not a single global scatter — the point of a GP photo-z

Colour → redshift — note the widening band

Training galaxies (red) are dense at blue colours and sparse at red ones. The GP mean (blue) predicts redshift; its band is tight where galaxies are plentiful and balloons where they are scarce — a per-object uncertainty, exactly what a survey needs.

The point. A GP photo-z (e.g. GPz) delivers not a point estimate but a redshift distribution per galaxy, and a heteroscedastic version even models colour-dependent noise — keeping the redshift PDFs that feed cosmology properly calibrated.

A millisecond stand-in for CAMB/CLASS

A Boltzmann code takes seconds per evaluation; an MCMC needs millions. Train a GP emulator on a few precomputed spectra and it predicts the matter power spectrum (or CMB spectra) across cosmological parameters in milliseconds, with a controlled error — accelerating inference by orders of magnitude.

train on P(k;θ1),…,P(k;θ5)  ⇒  predict P(k;θ∗) ± σ   in milliseconds a GP over the parameter θ at every k — interpolating whole curves, not just numbers

Searching for features without assuming a power law

The standard model says the primordial spectrum 𝒫ℛ(k) is a featureless near-power-law. To test that, put a GP prior on it — its length scale is literally a prior on how much the spectrum may wiggle — and reconstruct from the data. A localised feature (a step or a burst of oscillations) survives if the data demand it.

𝒫ℛ(k) ∼ 𝒢𝒫(power-law, kℓ),    ℓ = how sharp a feature the prior permits too smooth a prior erases real features; too rough fits noise — the length scale is the knob

Reconstruct a spectrum with a hidden feature

The true spectrum (red dashed) is scale-invariant with a bump. Noisy band-power data (red points) constrain the GP reconstruction (blue). Tune the smoothness length scale: too large and the bump is washed out; too small and the reconstruction chases noise.

The point. A GP prior makes “look for features” a principled, uncertainty-aware inference rather than a fishing expedition — the reconstruction shows a feature only where the data insist, with a band that says how confidently.

Where to be careful, and what comes next

The O(n3) wall

Exact GPs do not scale to millions of arbitrary points, and every remedy (inducing points, structured kernels, iterative CG) is an approximation with its own failure modes — too few inducing points quietly inflate the noise estimate and over-smooth, hiding real signal. The discipline: validate the predictive uncertainty out of sample, not just the mean, before trusting a scaled-up GP.

Non-Gaussian likelihoods

Counts, binary labels, and censored or truncated data break the conjugacy that makes GP regression closed-form. Keep a GP on a latent function f and pass it through a link (logistic, Poisson) to the likelihood; inference is then approximate — Laplace, expectation propagation, or variational. This is GP classification, and the log-Gaussian Cox process for point patterns (e.g. modelling a galaxy or photon rate that must stay positive).

Stationarity & heteroscedastic noise

Most kernels assume identical behaviour everywhere, but real signals change length scale across the domain and their noise varies with input. Fixes: input warping (learn a monotonic transform of x so the process is stationary in the warped space), explicitly non-stationary kernels, and heteroscedastic models that put a second GP on log-noise(x). The answer is never better than the kernel, so this is where the modelling care goes.

Deep & multi-output GPs

Deep GPs stack GP layers (the output of one is the input of the next), buying non-stationarity and richer priors at the cost of no-longer-Gaussian posteriors (doubly-stochastic variational inference). Multi-output GPs share strength across correlated channels — the linear model of coregionalisation (LMC/ICM) and convolution processes — e.g. fitting several photometric bands, or flux and its derivative, jointly.

Honest, not magic. A GP gives calibrated uncertainty given its kernel and its Gaussian assumptions. The craft is choosing a covariance that encodes the real physics, handling non-Gaussian data and non-stationarity honestly, and checking that the error bars actually hold up out of sample. The next four pages take each limitation in turn, with a worked example and a live animation.

Beating the O(n3) wall

The exact GP factorises an n×n matrix — O(n3) time, O(n2) memory — hopeless past a few thousand points. The dominant remedy summarises all n data by m ℒ n inducing points, dropping the cost to O(nm2). The trade-off is visible: too few inducing points and the sparse fit over-smooths, quietly hiding real structure.

O(n3)  →  O(nm2),    m inducing points summarise n data the variational choice (Titsias) places the m points to lose as little information as possible

Sparse vs full GP — add inducing points

Grey dashed = the full GP on all 60 points. Blue = a sparse GP built from just m inducing points (red). With too few, the blue curve is too stiff; increase m and it converges to the full fit — at a fraction of the cost.

The point. Sparse and iterative GPs scale to millions of points, but every approximation can under-report uncertainty — so validate the band out of sample, not just the mean.

Non-Gaussian data: a latent GP through a link

Binary labels, counts and censored data break the closed-form conjugacy of regression. Keep a GP on a latent function f, and pass it through a link (the logistic σ for classification) so the probability stays in [0,1]. Inference is then approximate — Laplace, expectation propagation or variational — but the GP’s calibrated uncertainty survives.

P(class=1 | x) = σ( f(x) ),    f ∼ 𝒢𝒫(0,k) the latent GP is squashed to a probability; uncertainty on f becomes uncertainty on the class probability

Classify by clicking — class 1 on top, class 0 below

Green points are class 1 (plotted at 1), red are class 0 (at 0). The blue curve is P(class=1|x) from the squashed latent GP, and the band is its uncertainty — wide where the two classes overlap or data are sparse. Click near the top or bottom to add points.

The point. The same latent-GP-plus-link recipe gives GP classification, Poisson/Cox processes for point patterns (positive rates), and ordinal or survival models — extending GPs far beyond Gaussian noise.

When the noise depends on where you look

Standard kernels assume the same behaviour everywhere, but real data are often heteroscedastic — the measurement scatter grows across the domain (fainter sources, later times). A stationary GP with one global noise level gets the error bars wrong; giving each observation its own noise variance (or a second GP on log-noise) restores an honest, input-dependent band.

y(x) = f(x) + ε(x),   ε(x) ∼ 𝒩(0, σn(x)2) the noise variance is a function of the input — the diagonal of K is no longer constant

Heteroscedastic GP — a band that grows with the noise

The measurement noise increases from left to right. Because each point carries its own variance, the GP predictive band is tight on the quiet (left) side and widens where the data are noisy (right) — exactly as an honest error bar should.

The point. Non-stationarity — in noise or in length scale — is handled by input-dependent kernels, input warping, or stacking (next page). Ignoring it gives over-confident error bars exactly where the data are worst.

Stacking GPs for non-stationarity

A single stationary GP cannot have a length scale that changes across the domain. A deep GP composes them — the output of one GP warps the input of the next, f(x)=g2(g1(x)) — producing non-stationary functions with sharp transitions in some regions and flat stretches in others, all learned from data. The price is that the posterior is no longer Gaussian (doubly-stochastic variational inference).

f(x) = g2( g1(x) ),   g1,g2 ∼ 𝒢𝒫  ⇒  f is non-stationary the first GP warps the input; the second acts on the warped coordinate — variable smoothness for free

A deep-GP sample — watch the smoothness vary

A two-layer composition, animated. Increase the warp strength and the function develops fast-varying regions next to slow ones — the hallmark of non-stationarity a single stationary kernel cannot produce.

The point. Deep GPs buy non-stationarity and richer priors; multi-output GPs (linear coregionalisation, convolution processes) instead share strength across correlated channels — several photometric bands, or a quantity and its derivative, fit jointly. Both extend the GP beyond a single stationary function while keeping its uncertainty.

Software for Gaussian processes

General-purpose

GPyTorch (scalable, GPU, iterative), GPflow (TensorFlow, variational), GPy (the classic), and tinygp (JAX-native, differentiable).

Astronomy time series

celerite2 and george for fast 1-D GPs on light curves and RV data; used throughout exoplanet and time-domain work (often via exoplanet/PyMC).

Cosmology

GaPP for H(z)/w(z) reconstruction; GP-based emulators underlie modern power-spectrum surrogates. JAX-native GPs (tinygp) fit naturally into differentiable cosmology pipelines.

Probabilistic programming

Stan, PyMC and NumPyro all provide GP building blocks, so a GP can sit inside a larger hierarchical Bayesian model with everything sampled jointly.

Getting started. For a first GP, tinygp or GPy is a dozen lines; for large light curves, celerite2; for scale and GPUs, GPyTorch. All of them run exactly the equations on the “Regression” and “Occam” pages.
