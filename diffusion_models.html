Diffusion & Score Models

How an image generator turns pure static into a picture

Diffusion & score-based
models, from scratch

The idea behind Stable Diffusion, DALL·E and the rest — explained with one running toy you can work out on a calculator. Learn the score, walk noise uphill into data, and reverse a noising process step by step.

Diffusion models generate data by gradually removing noise. Start from pure random static and, in many small steps, denoise it into a sample — an image, a molecule, a sound. It sounds like alchemy, but the whole thing rests on one quantity from first-year calculus: the gradient of a log-probability, called the score. This deck builds everything from that, with every formula checked on a running 1-D example, and live 2-D demos where you watch a cloud of noise reorganize itself into a ring of data.

Generative modeling: learn to make new samples

You have a pile of examples — photos, molecules, audio clips — drawn from some unknown distribution pdata(x). You want to generate new samples from that same distribution: new photos that look real, new molecules that are plausible. The catch is that pdata lives in a huge space and you only ever see samples, never a formula.

given samples x₁, x₂, … ~ pdata  →   produce NEW x ~ pdata the central problem of generative modeling — and diffusion is one powerful answer

Older approaches modelled p(x) directly (which needs an intractable normalizing constant), or learned an encoder/decoder (VAEs), or a chain of invertible maps (normalizing flows). Diffusion takes a different route: it never needs the normalizing constant, because it works with the score — and it turns generation into a sequence of easy denoising steps.

Our running toy Instead of a million-pixel image, our pdata is a 1-D distribution with two bumps at −2 and +2 (a mixture of two Gaussians, width 0.6). Small enough to plot, compute, and verify — but it has the one feature that matters: multiple modes a generator must cover.

The one quantity everything is built on

The score: which way is “more probable”?

The score of a distribution is the gradient of its log-density — an arrow at every point saying which way probability increases, and how steeply:

s(x) = ∇x log p(x) points “uphill” toward higher density; zero at peaks; large where the density changes fast

Two reasons the score is the perfect thing to learn:

  • No normalizing constant. Since log p(x) = log p̃(x) − log Z, and Z doesn’t depend on x, the score ∇ log p drops the intractable Z entirely. You can work with unnormalized densities.
  • It tells you how to move. If you can evaluate the score anywhere, you can push a sample toward the data (next pages). Generation becomes “follow the arrows.”
Reframe: instead of asking “what is the probability of x?” (hard, needs Z), ask “which way should I nudge x to make it more probable?” (easy, a gradient). That single change of question is the seed of every diffusion model.

Work it out by hand

For a Gaussian p(x) = 𝒩(x; μ, σ²), take the log and differentiate — the messy constant vanishes:

log density (drop constants)

log p(x) = −(x−μ)² / (2σ²) + const

differentiate in x

s(x) = d/dx log p(x) = −(x−μ) / σ²

score of 𝒩(μ, σ²):   s(x) = −(x − μ)/σ² an arrow pointing straight back to the mean, stronger the farther you are
Plug in numbers — 𝒩(0,1)

μ=0, σ=1, so s(x) = −x. At x=2: s = −2 (push left, toward 0). At x=−1: s = +1 (push right). At x=0 (the peak): s = 0. The arrows always point home to the mean.

For our two-bump toy the score is a weighted blend of “point to −2” and “point to +2,” where the weights are how well each bump explains x (its responsibility). Near +2 it points to +2; at 0, by symmetry, the two pulls cancel and s(0)=0.

See the arrows

Here is the exact score of a 2-D distribution — eight Gaussian blobs arranged in a ring (a toy that’s just complex enough to be interesting). Every arrow is ∇ log p: it points toward the nearest data, and shrinks to nothing right on a blob. This vector field is what a diffusion model learns.

Switch to the noised field: after adding noise the blobs blur together and the arrows become smooth and long-range — which, as we’ll see, is exactly what makes them learnable and useful far from the data.

Turning a score into samples

Walk uphill, but keep some randomness

If the score points toward high probability, why not just follow it? Pure gradient ascent would collapse every sample onto the nearest peak. Langevin dynamics adds a pinch of fresh noise at each step, so instead of collapsing, the samples spread out into the actual distribution:

x ← x + (ε/2) s(x) + √ε z,   z ~ 𝒩(0, 1) a step of size ε/2 uphill, plus Gaussian noise of size √ε; repeat many times

Run this long enough and the collection of samples converges to p(x) itself — a remarkable fact from statistical physics. You sampled from a distribution knowing only its score, never its normalizing constant.

One step by hand — toy score, ε=0.1

Say x = 3.0 and the score there is s(3) = −1.0 (it points left, toward the +2 bump). Take a noise draw z = 0.5.

x ← 3.0 + (0.1/2)(−1.0) + √0.1·(0.5) = 3.0 − 0.05 + 0.316·0.5 = 3.0 − 0.05 + 0.158 = 3.11.

The uphill nudge pulls toward the data; the noise keeps it exploring. Over many steps it settles into the two bumps.

Sampling = following the score with noise. This is the engine. The rest of the deck is about one problem with it — the score is unreliable far from the data — and the elegant fix that becomes the full diffusion model.

Why plain score-matching struggles

Langevin sampling needs a good score everywhere you might walk. But a learned score is only trustworthy where there was training data — and real data sits on a thin sliver (a “manifold”) of a vast empty space. Three failures follow:

Empty regions

Almost everywhere has near-zero density and no training examples, so the estimated score there is garbage — and that’s exactly where a fresh noise sample starts.

Low-density valleys

Between modes (near x=0 for our toy) data is scarce, so the score is inaccurate and Langevin mixes between modes badly — wrong proportions.

Manifold gradients

Off the data manifold, the true score points sharply back and is hard to learn from finite samples.

The crux A sample starts as pure noise, far from all data, precisely where the score is worst. Follow a bad score and you wander into nonsense. We need a score that is reliable even far from the data.
The fix, in one line: deliberately blur the data with noise so it fills the empty space, learn the score of the blurred data (which is smooth and defined everywhere), then undo the blur gradually. That is diffusion.

Many noise levels, from a whisper to a roar

Take the data and add Gaussian noise of scale σ. The blurred distribution pσ spreads out to fill the gaps — its score is now smooth and defined everywhere, including far from the original data. But heavy blur destroys detail. The insight (Song & Ermon, 2019) is to use a whole ladder of noise levels at once:

  • Large noise → the blurred data covers all of space, so the score guides samples from anywhere toward the rough shape.
  • Small noise → barely blurred, so the score restores fine detail near the data.
learn sσ(x) = ∇ log pσ(x) for many σ, from σmax (roar) down to σmin (whisper) then sample: follow the large-noise score first, then progressively smaller-noise scores

You saw this on the score-field page: the noised field was smooth and long-range; the data field was sharp and local. Generation walks from the smooth one to the sharp one. Making that ladder continuous gives the diffusion process.

The trick that makes it trainable The score of noised data has a closed-form learning target — you know exactly what noise you added, so you know which way to denoise. That’s the next few pages, and it’s why diffusion training is stable where other generative models fight.

Diffusion, formally

Gradually turn data into noise

Define a forward process: start with a real sample x₀ and add a little Gaussian noise at each of many steps t = 1…T, shrinking the signal slightly each time. After enough steps, all structure is gone and xT is pure Gaussian noise.

xt = √(1−βt) xt−1 + √βt εt,   εt ~ 𝒩(0,1) βt is a small “noise rate” at step t; the √(1−β) keeps the variance from blowing up

This is a fixed, known process with no learning — it just destroys data on a schedule. The whole model is learning to run it backwards. And there is a beautiful shortcut: you never need to simulate all T steps to noise a sample.

Two names you’ll meet This discrete recipe is DDPM (Ho et al., 2020). Its continuous-time limit is a stochastic differential equation (Song et al., 2021), the “variance-preserving SDE.” Same process, two languages; we’ll use the friendly discrete one.

Jump to any noise level in one shot

Because each step just scales and adds Gaussian noise, composing T of them is still one Gaussian. Define α̅t = ∏s≤t(1−βs) (the surviving signal fraction). Then noising all the way from x₀ to step t collapses to a single line:

xt = √α̅t x₀ + √(1−α̅t) ε,   ε ~ 𝒩(0,1) signal kept: √α̅ · noise added: √(1−α̅) — the two coefficients always satisfy (√α̅)²+(√(1−α̅))²=1
Work it — a calculator-friendly checkpoint

Pick a step where α̅t = 0.36, so √α̅ = 0.6 and √(1−α̅) = √0.64 = 0.8. Take a data point x₀ = 3.0 and a noise draw ε = 0.5.

xt = 0.6·3.0 + 0.8·0.5 = 1.8 + 0.4 = 2.2. One multiply-add — no chain of 400 steps needed. ✓

Why this matters for training. To make a training example at any noise level, pick a random t, draw one ε, and compute xt directly. You also know ε — the exact noise you added — which is the label the network will predict. That’s the entire training loop, coming up.

Watch the data melt into a Gaussian

The two-bump density, noised to increasing levels. As α̅ falls from ~1 (data) toward 0 (pure noise), the peaks slide toward the center, widen, and merge into a single standard Gaussian. Drag the slider through the forward process.

Notice the score simplify. At high noise the two-bump structure is gone and the score becomes nearly linear — just “point to the center,” like a single Gaussian. Easy to learn, defined everywhere. Generation exploits exactly this: start where the score is easy, follow it back to where the score is detailed.

The knob: how fast to add noise

The noise schedule β1…βT sets how quickly signal is destroyed. A common choice is a small linear ramp; what matters is the resulting α̅t, which slides from ~1 (all signal) to ~0 (all noise). Here is a real schedule (T=400, β from 0.0001 to 0.02):

α̅t (signal kept)βt (noise rate, ×20)

Too fast

Big β destroys structure in a few steps; the reverse model has to make huge, error-prone jumps.

Too slow

Tiny β needs thousands of steps to reach noise — accurate but slow to sample. Modern schedules (cosine) balance this.

Whatever the schedule, only α̅t enters the magic identity — so the schedule is really a choice of how the signal fraction √α̅ decays from 1 to 0.

What the neural network actually learns

Guess the noise that was added

Here is the whole training loop, and it is astonishingly simple. Repeat:

# one training step x0 = a real data sample t = a random step in 1..T eps = draw Gaussian noise x_t = sqrt(abar_t)*x0 + sqrt(1-abar_t)*eps # the magic identity loss = || eps_network(x_t, t) - eps ||^2 # predict the noise you added

The network εθ(xt, t) is handed a noisy sample and the noise level, and must guess the noise that was mixed in. That’s a plain regression — mean-squared error against a known target — which is why diffusion training is so stable compared to the adversarial tug-of-war of GANs.

L(θ) = 𝔼x₀, t, ε  ‖ εθ(xt, t) − ε ‖² average over data, noise levels, and noise draws — nothing adversarial, just denoising
Why guessing noise = learning the score Knowing the noise ε that produced xt tells you exactly which way to denoise — and that direction is the score, up to a scale. The next page makes the link an equation.

Predicting noise IS estimating the score

The marginal at step t is xt = √α̅ x₀ + √(1−α̅) ε, a Gaussian centered at √α̅ x₀. Its score (from the Gaussian rule on page 4) is minus the standardized deviation — which is exactly minus the noise, rescaled:

s(xt) = ∇ log pt(xt) = − ε / √(1−α̅t) so the noise-predicting network εθ gives the score for free: sθ = −εθ/√(1−α̅)
Check it — the page-11 checkpoint

There α̅=0.36, so √(1−α̅)=0.8, and we added ε=0.5. Then the score is s = −0.5/0.8 = −0.625.

Negative → push xt to the left, back toward the (scaled) data mean. The bigger the noise you added, the stronger the pull back. ✓

Three names, one object. “Predict the noise” (DDPM), “estimate the score” (score-based), and “denoise” are the same task wearing different clothes. Once you have εθ you have the score, and once you have the score you can reverse the process.

Undoing the noise, one step at a time

From xt back to xt−1

Generation runs the forward process backwards. Given a noisy xt, the network predicts the noise, which gives an estimate of the clean sample x₀, from which we take a small step toward slightly-less-noisy xt−1. The clean-sample estimate is just the magic identity solved for x₀:

x̂₀ = ( xt − √(1−α̅t) εθ ) / √α̅t “subtract the predicted noise, then rescale” — a guess of the original data from the noisy sample
Denoise by hand

From page 11, xt=2.2, α̅=0.36. Suppose the network predicts the noise perfectly, εθ=0.5.

x̂₀ = (2.2 − 0.8·0.5)/0.6 = (2.2 − 0.4)/0.6 = 1.8/0.6 = 3.0 — exactly the original data point. ✓

The full DDPM reverse step blends this x̂₀ with xt (the posterior mean of q(xt−1|xt, x̂₀)) and adds a touch of noise for the stochastic version. In practice: predict noise → estimate clean → step back → repeat T times.

Noise becomes data (1-D)

Start with 6000 points of pure Gaussian noise and run the reverse process using our toy’s exact score. Step through: the single noise blob (violet) splits and sharpens into the two data peaks (teal). This is generation — sampling from pdata starting from nothing but static.

This is the payoff. The forward process (data→noise) is fixed and dumb. The reverse process (noise→data) is the model, and it’s just: repeatedly ask “which way is the data?” (the score) and take a step. Do it hundreds of times and static turns into samples.

Deterministic sampling, far fewer steps

The stochastic reverse process (DDPM) adds noise at every step and needs hundreds of them. Song et al. noticed there is also a deterministic path that hits the same marginals — the probability-flow ODE, whose discrete cousin is DDIM. No noise is injected during reverse; the same score defines a smooth trajectory from a noise point to a data point.

dx = −½ β(t) [ x + s(x) ] dt  (reverse-time probability-flow ODE) an ordinary differential equation driven by the score — solve it and noise flows to data

Why it’s useful

Deterministic → the same seed always gives the same sample, you can interpolate smoothly between samples, and clever ODE solvers reach good samples in 10–50 steps instead of 1000.

The trade

Stochastic (DDPM) sampling can be a touch more diverse and forgiving of score errors; deterministic (DDIM/ODE) is faster and reproducible. Most fast samplers today are ODE-based.

Our 1-D and 2-D reverse widgets both use this deterministic DDIM-style update — that’s why they march cleanly from noise to data.

SDE, ODE, and discrete DDPM

The same diffusion model can be described three equivalent ways. Knowing they’re the same thing dissolves most of the confusion in the literature.

ViewForwardReverse (generation)Flavor
Discrete DDPMadd noise on a β-schedule, T stepsancestral sampling, T stochastic stepsconcrete, easy to code
Score SDEa stochastic differential equationthe reverse-time SDE (needs the score)continuous, unifying
Probability-flow ODEsame marginals, no randomnessa deterministic ODE (DDIM)fast, reproducible

All three share one learned object — the score (equivalently the noise-predictor). The forward process is fixed; the reverse process plugs the score into whichever solver you like. Continuous-time theory (Song et al., 2021) showed the discrete DDPM and the score-matching approaches were the same model all along.

One thing to learn, many ways to sample. Train the noise/score network once; then choose stochastic or deterministic, many steps or few, depending on whether you want diversity or speed.

Making it do what you ask

Steering generation with a condition

Unconditional diffusion makes a sample; you usually want a sample matching a prompt (“a cat in a hat”) or class. The score makes conditioning clean: to sample from p(x | c), use the conditional score, which by Bayes splits into the unconditional score plus a term pointing toward the condition:

∇ log p(x | c) = ∇ log p(x) + ∇ log p(c | x) “go toward data” + “go toward matching the prompt”

Classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2021) trains one network to predict noise both with and without the condition, then at sampling time extrapolates between them to amplify the condition’s pull:

ε̂ = εuncond + w ( εcond − εuncond ) the guidance weight w dials prompt-adherence up (sharper, more literal) or down (more varied)
Guidance is a score you add. Every controllable diffusion model — text-to-image, inpainting, super-resolution — is this same move: add a term to the score that pulls samples toward satisfying your constraint.

How diffusion relates to everything else

ModelRelation to diffusion
Energy-based modelsBoth work with unnormalized densities via the score; diffusion sidesteps EBM’s hard sampling by using the noise ladder.
VAEsA diffusion model is (formally) a deep hierarchical VAE with a fixed noising encoder and a learned denoising decoder — the DDPM loss is a variational bound.
Normalizing flowsThe probability-flow ODE is a continuous normalizing flow; diffusion gives you exact likelihoods through it, if you want them.
GANsSame goal (generate samples) but no adversary: diffusion trades GANs’ one-step speed for stable training and full mode coverage.
Flow matching / rectified flowThe modern generalization: learn a velocity field that transports noise to data along straight-ish paths — often faster, same spirit, and reduces to diffusion as a special case.
Diffusion sits at a crossroads. It borrows the score from energy models, a variational bound from VAEs, an ODE from flows — and its noise-ladder training is what made all of that finally work at scale. Flow matching is where much of the field is heading next, but it’s the same core idea: learn a field that moves noise to data.

Strengths and honest weaknesses

Why diffusion won

  • Stable training — plain regression, no adversary to balance.
  • Mode coverage — the noise ladder reaches every mode; little of GANs’ “mode collapse.”
  • Controllable — guidance and conditioning slot in as score terms.
  • Scales — works from tiny toys to billion-parameter image and video models.

The costs

  • Slow sampling — many network passes per sample (mitigated by ODE solvers, distillation).
  • Compute-hungry to train.
  • Not exact by default — likelihoods need the ODE; samples can still hallucinate.
  • Latent tricks needed for high-res (do diffusion in a compressed space — “latent diffusion”).
The one-sentence why By breaking “generate a sample” into hundreds of tiny, well-posed denoising problems — each a stable regression with a known target — diffusion replaces one impossibly hard task with many easy ones. That decomposition is the whole trick.

Where score-based models show up

Images & video

Stable Diffusion, DALL·E, Imagen, Sora — text-to-image and text-to-video, inpainting, super-resolution, editing.

Audio

Speech synthesis and vocoders (WaveGrad, DiffWave), music generation.

Science

Molecule and protein generation, materials design, and inverse problems (MRI/CT reconstruction) where the score is a learned prior.

3-D & robotics

Shape and scene generation; diffusion policies that generate action sequences for control.

Cosmology & physics

Emulating fields (dark-matter, turbulence), generating simulations, and as priors for likelihood-free inference of physical parameters.

Data & tabular

Synthetic data, imputation, and anomaly detection via the learned density/score.

The pattern: anywhere you need a prior over complex data — to generate it, complete it, or invert a measurement of it — a diffusion model’s learned score is now a default tool, well beyond pretty pictures.

The whole pipeline in six calculator steps

Everything, on our checkpoint α̅=0.36 (√α̅=0.6, √(1−α̅)=0.8), data point x₀=3, noise ε=0.5.

1 · Score of a Gaussian

s(x) = −(x−μ)/σ². For 𝒩(0,1) at x=2: s = −2. Points to the mean.

2 · Langevin step

x ← x + (ε/2)s(x) + √ε z. Follow the score, add noise, repeat → samples.

3 · Forward noising (one shot)

xt = √α̅ x₀ + √(1−α̅) ε = 0.6·3 + 0.8·0.5 = 2.2.

4 · Training target

network sees xt=2.2 and t; must output ε≈0.5. Loss = (ε̂−ε)².

5 · Score from noise

s = −ε/√(1−α̅) = −0.5/0.8 = −0.625.

6 · Denoise (recover data)

x̂₀ = (xt − √(1−α̅)ε̂)/√α̅ = (2.2 − 0.4)/0.6 = 3.0. Back to the start. ✓

Langevin dynamicssampling by following the score plus injected noise.


Forward / diffusion processthe fixed procedure that gradually adds noise, turning data into pure Gaussian noise.
