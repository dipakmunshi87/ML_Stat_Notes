ABC & SBI

Bayesian inference when you can simulate but can’t write down a likelihood

ABC & simulation-based
inference, from scratch

How to do Bayesian inference when your model is a simulator — a program you can run forward, but whose likelihood you cannot evaluate. From rejection ABC you can do with a pencil, up to the modern neural methods (NPE, NLE, NRE).

Huge parts of science are like this: you have a computer model that generates data (a climate model, an epidemic model, a galaxy-formation model, a queue, a financial market), and you want to know which parameters are consistent with what you actually observed. The trouble is that writing down “the probability of this data given these parameters” — the likelihood — is hopeless. Simulation-based inference (SBI), also called likelihood-free inference, gets the Bayesian answer using only the ability to run the simulator.

Meet the mystery dial — we will never let it go

The mystery dial

Somewhere there is a dial set to an unknown number θ. Before we look, we believe it is probably near zero but could be off by a few units — a prior θ ~ 𝒩(0, 3²). We can’t read the dial directly. Instead we press a button and the machine spits out a noisy reading:

θ ~ 𝒩(0, τ²), τ=3   — the prior
x = θ + 𝒩(0, σ²), σ=1   — the simulator (one press of the button) we pressed the button once and saw  xobs = 2.0

Our question is the Bayesian one: given that single reading of 2.0, what do we now believe about θ? That belief is the posterior distribution, and it is what every single method in this deck is trying to compute.

Two things make this the perfect teaching example. First, it is trivial to simulate: pick a θ, add a bit of noise, done. Second, because everything is Gaussian, the posterior has a known exact formula — so we can grade every approximate method against the truth.

Why pretend we can’t just do the math? For this toy we can. But we will only ever let each method run the simulator — press the button for chosen θ and look at the output — never peek at the Gaussian formula. That is exactly the situation in real science, where the simulator is a million lines of code and no formula exists. The dial just lets us check the answers.
Keep this in your head: a dial θ we can’t see, a button that returns x = θ + noise, one observed press xobs=2. Everything that follows is “what is θ?”

The target every method chases

Bayes’ rule says the posterior is the prior times the likelihood, renormalised:

p(θ | xobs) ∝ p(xobs | θ) × p(θ) posterior ∝ likelihood × prior

For our Gaussian dial both pieces are Gaussian, and the product of Gaussians is Gaussian. The precisions (one-over-variance) add, and the mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean (0) and the reading (xobs):

Posterior precision

1/s⁎² = 1/τ² + 1/σ² = 1/9 + 1/1 = 1.111  →  s⁎² = 0.9,   s⁎ = 0.949

Posterior mean

μ⁎ = s⁎² × (0/τ² + xobs/σ²) = 0.9 × 2.0 = 1.800

p(θ | xobs=2) = 𝒩(1.800,  0.949²) this is the exact answer — the bullseye for every method in this deck

Notice the reading was 2.0 but the posterior mean is only 1.8: the prior (centred at 0) pulls the estimate back toward zero. That shrinkage is Bayesian inference doing its job. Now — pretending we never saw this formula — let’s reconstruct this same curve using nothing but the simulator.

Prior × likelihood = posterior

Before we abandon the formula, build intuition for it. Drag the observed reading and the noise level; watch the prior (grey) and the likelihood (amber) combine into the posterior (violet). The posterior is always narrower than either, and sits between the prior’s centre (0) and the reading.

prior p(θ)likelihood p(xobs|θ)posterior
The takeaway: a small σ (trustworthy button) makes the likelihood sharp and the posterior follows the reading; a big σ makes it vague and the prior wins. Every method below is an algorithm for building this violet curve — some by brute force, some by learning.

The likelihood-free setup

A simulator is any program that, given parameters θ, produces synthetic data x — usually by drawing internal random numbers along the way. Our dial is a one-liner, but the idea covers enormous models:

Examples of simulators

  • an epidemic model turning (R₀, incubation) into a case curve
  • a climate model turning forcings into temperature series
  • a particle-physics event generator
  • a galaxy-formation or gravitational-lensing model
  • a queueing or agent-based economic model

Why the likelihood is intractable

The likelihood p(x|θ) means “integrate over every internal random draw that could have produced this exact x.” For anything beyond a toy that integral has no formula and astronomically many paths. You can run the model, but you cannot score a dataset with it.

p(x | θ) = ∫ p(x, z | θ) dz   —— the intractable integral over all latent draws z SBI never touches this integral; it only ever calls x = simulator(θ)
The deal SBI offers: give up on evaluating the likelihood. Keep only the power to sample from it (run the simulator). Remarkably, that is enough to recover the posterior. The rest of this deck is how.

Approximate Bayesian Computation

Simulate, compare, keep the close ones

Here is the whole idea of ABC in one breath: you can’t score data, but you can generate it, so generate lots and keep the parameters whose fake data looks like your real data.

# Rejection ABC repeat many times: θ* ← draw from the prior p(θ) # a candidate dial setting x* ← simulate(θ*) # press the button for it if distance(x*, x_obs) < ε: # did it land near our reading? keep θ* # if so, it’s a posterior sample

The kept θ values are (approximately) draws from the posterior. Why? A candidate is kept in proportion to how often it reproduces the observation — and “how often θ reproduces xobs” is exactly the likelihood. Weighting the prior by the likelihood is Bayes’ rule. The tolerance ε is the one knob: ε→0 gives the exact posterior but accepts almost nothing.

On our dial — draw θ* from 𝒩(0,3²), add noise to get x*, and keep θ* only if |x* − 2.0| < ε. No Gaussian formula used anywhere; just the button and a ruler.
Approximate Bayesian Computation earns its name from ε: with ε=0 it would be exact but never accept; with ε>0 it accepts a nearby band, trading a little bias for a lot of speed.

Ten button-presses on paper

Let’s literally do it. Below are 10 draws from the prior, each simulated once, with tolerance ε = 1.0 around xobs=2. Accept when the reading is within 1.0 of 2.0 (i.e. between 1.0 and 3.0). This is the entire algorithm, executed by hand:

Rejection ABC • ε=1.0 • keep if 1.0 ≤ x* ≤ 3.0
#θ* (prior draw)x* = θ*+noise|x*−2|verdict

The accepted θ values already cluster around 1–2, near the true posterior mean of 1.8. With only ten draws the estimate is rough; the magic is that doing this hundreds of thousands of times, with a smaller ε, reconstructs the exact posterior curve — which is the next page.

The cost, already visible With ε=1 we keep maybe a third of draws. Halve ε and you keep far fewer. In high dimensions almost nothing lands close, and rejection ABC grinds to a halt — the flaw every later method is trying to fix.

Big ε → many accepted but too wide (biased toward the prior); small ε → fewer accepted but the bars snap onto the truth.

tolerance ε:
The bias–variance face-off in one slider. Large ε: low variance (tons of samples) but biased (too wide). Small ε: unbiased but high variance (few samples, noisy). Choosing ε is the practice of ABC.

You rarely compare raw data — you compare summaries

Our dial outputs a single number, so “distance” is easy. Real data is a whole time series, image, or catalogue. Comparing those directly is hopeless: two runs will never match sample-for-sample, so nothing is ever within ε. The fix is to compare summary statistics S(x) instead of raw x:

keep θ*  if  ‖ S(x*) − S(xobs) ‖ < ε e.g. the mean, variance, a few autocorrelations, peak counts, quantiles …

Good summaries are the heart of practical ABC. An epidemic model might be summarised by peak height, peak timing, and total cases; a time series by its first few autocorrelations. The dance is:

  • Too few summaries → you throw away information the data contained; the posterior is too wide.
  • Too many summaries → the “curse of dimensionality” strikes: matching all of them within ε becomes as hard as matching raw data.
On our dial the reading is its own summary, S(x)=x. That is a luxury. Half the art of applied ABC is choosing S; the neural methods later will learn S automatically.

When is a summary “lossless”?

A summary S(x) is sufficient if it captures everything the data says about θ — formally, if p(θ | x) = p(θ | S(x)). Use a sufficient summary and ABC targets the true posterior (as ε→0). Use an insufficient one and even a perfect ε=0 run gives only the posterior-given-summary, which is wider.

Our dial, worked out

The reading x itself is sufficient for θ (it’s a one-observation Gaussian). If instead we summarised by S(x)=sign(x) — only whether the reading was positive — we’d keep far too many settings and the posterior would barely narrow. Same algorithm, much worse answer, purely because the summary threw away information.

The catch Outside toy exponential-family models, low-dimensional sufficient statistics usually don’t exist. So applied ABC settles for “nearly sufficient” hand-crafted summaries — and this compromise is precisely what motivated learning summaries with neural nets.
Remember the trade: summaries make comparison possible in high dimensions, but every non-sufficient summary silently widens your posterior. Sufficiency is the ideal you approximate.

“Close enough”, made precise

The hard cutoff “keep if distance < ε” is really a kernel: a box of width ε that weights each simulation 1 (inside) or 0 (outside). We can soften it — e.g. a Gaussian kernel that down-weights smoothly with distance:

w(θ*) = Kε( S(x*) − S(xobs) ),   Kε(u) = exp(− u² / 2ε²) box kernel = hard accept/reject; Gaussian kernel = weighted samples

This reframes ABC as a kernel density estimate of the posterior: each accepted (or weighted) θ is a grain of sand, and ε is the bandwidth. It also explains the bias exactly: the ABC posterior is the true posterior convolved (blurred) by the kernel of width ε. Shrinking ε sharpens the picture; setting ε=0 recovers the truth but accepts nothing.

Why the bars were too wide at big ε

On the previous widget, ε=3 gave a fat histogram. That fatness is the true posterior (width 0.95) blurred by a box of half-width 3 — you were literally seeing the convolution. At ε=0.25 the blur is negligible and the bars matched the curve.

Mental model: ABC = kernel density estimation in parameter space, with ε as the bandwidth and the simulator as your only way to draw samples.

The honest equation for ABC

Putting the last pages together, plain rejection/kernel ABC does not sample the exact posterior. It samples this:

pABC(θ | xobs) ∝ p(θ) ∫ Kε( S(x) − S(xobs) )  p(x | θ)  dx the prior, weighted by how often θ produces data whose summary lands near the observed summary

Two approximations are baked in, and it’s worth naming them so you know what you’re buying:

  1. The ε blur. Nonzero tolerance widens the posterior (the convolution above). Controlled by ε→0.
  2. The summary loss. If S isn’t sufficient, you get p(θ|S(xobs)), not p(θ|xobs) — wider, and no amount of shrinking ε fixes it.

As ε→0 and S sufficient, pABC→p, the true posterior. Every ABC improvement (SMC, regression adjustment) attacks approximation 1; every neural method attacks approximation 2 by learning S or the density directly.

Two dials, two failure modes. Bias from ε, bias from bad summaries. Keep them separate in your head — they’re fixed by different tools.

Making ABC efficient • method 1 of 3

Don’t restart from the prior every time

Rejection ABC’s waste is obvious: it keeps drawing from the broad prior, so almost every draw lands far from xobs and is thrown away. ABC-MCMC (Marjoram et al., 2003) fixes this by taking a guided walk: once you’ve found a θ that works, propose small steps nearby, where good candidates cluster.

# ABC-MCMC (Metropolis with an ABC accept test) θ ← some start repeat: θ' ← θ + small random step # propose nearby x' ← simulate(θ') if |x' − x_obs| < ε and rand() < prior(θ')/prior(θ): θ ← θ' # accept the move record θ

The chain lingers in the high-posterior region instead of blindly sampling the prior, so it stays efficient even when the acceptance band is tight. The price: consecutive samples are correlated (it’s a walk), you must discard an initial burn-in, and it explores one xobs at a time.

Left: the chain’s trace wandering then settling. Right: its histogram (after burn-in) against the exact posterior.

Making ABC efficient • method 2 of 3

Walk the tolerance down in stages

Picking one ε is a dilemma: large wastes accuracy, small wastes compute. ABC-SMC (Sequential Monte Carlo; Sisson, Toni, Beaumont) sidesteps it by using a schedule of shrinking tolerances ε₁ > ε₂ > …. A whole population of particles starts loose and easy, then is nudged and re-weighted to survive ever-tighter rounds — reusing good particles instead of restarting from the prior.

Step through the rounds. Each histogram is a real ABC population at that ε; watch it sharpen from prior-like toward the exact posterior.

Why it wins: most simulations happen at loose, cheap tolerances; only the survivors face the strict final ε. You reach a tiny ε for a fraction of the cost of rejection ABC — the workhorse of applied ABC before the neural era.

Making ABC efficient • method 3 of 3

Keep a loose sample, then correct it

Beaumont, Zhang & Balding (2002) had a clever idea: run ABC at a comfortably large ε (so you accept plenty), then mathematically remove the bias the large ε introduced. Each accepted particle had some mismatch x* − xobs; fit how θ depends on that mismatch and slide every particle to where it would have been at zero mismatch.

θcorrected = θ* − β ( x* − xobs ) β = slope of a local linear fit of θ on the summary; it “transports” each particle to x*=xobs

It is the same trick as a covariate adjustment in statistics. Watch it work on the dial: a deliberately loose ε=1.5 run (raw, too wide and biased) versus the same particles after the linear correction — which snaps onto the exact posterior.

raw ABC (ε=1.5)after regression adjustmentexact posterior
The lesson that carries forward: instead of shrinking ε forever, learn the relationship between simulated summaries and parameters and exploit it. Replace “linear fit” with “neural network” and you have essentially invented neural SBI.

Build a stand-in likelihood from simulations

A different escape from ABC’s ε: instead of accepting/rejecting, estimate the likelihood. Wood (2010) proposed the synthetic likelihood: at each candidate θ, run the simulator m times, and model the summary’s distribution as a Gaussian fitted to those m outputs. Then read that Gaussian’s density at the observed summary — that number is your approximate likelihood.

psyn(xobs|θ) = 𝒩( xobs;  μ̂(θ),  σ̂²(θ) ),   μ̂,σ̂ from m sims at θ turn the black-box simulator into an explicit (Gaussian) likelihood, then do Bayes as usual

Sweep θ across a grid, get a likelihood curve, multiply by the prior, normalise — a posterior, no tolerance anywhere. Here is the real synthetic-likelihood posterior on the dial (m=400 sims per grid point) against the truth:

The assumption Synthetic likelihood assumes the summaries are roughly Gaussian at each θ. When that holds it’s far more efficient than ABC; when summaries are skewed or multi-modal it can mislead. It is the conceptual bridge to the neural methods, which drop the Gaussian assumption and learn the density.

Modern SBI • the big idea

Stop rejecting. Start learning.

Classical ABC throws away almost every simulation — anything outside ε is discarded. That’s profligate when simulations are expensive. The modern insight: every simulation is a labelled training example (θ, x), so train a neural network on all of them and never reject anything.

Run the simulator N times with θ drawn from the prior, collect pairs {(θ₋, x₋)}, and use them to learn one of three targets. That choice of target names the three modern methods:

NPE

learn the posterior directly, q(θ|x). Feed it xobs, out comes the posterior.

NLE

learn the likelihood, q(x|θ). Then do Bayes/MCMC with it, reusing any prior.

NRE

learn the ratio p(x|θ)/p(x) via a classifier. Multiply by the prior for the posterior.

All three use the same simulations; they differ only in what function they fit. And crucially, most are amortized: after a one-time training cost, answering a new dataset is instant — no new simulations, no re-running MCMC.

The reframing: SBI becomes a supervised-learning problem. “Simulate a training set, then fit.” ABC’s accept/reject is replaced by a regression or a classifier that uses every sample.

What does “learn a distribution” even mean?

The neural methods all need to represent a probability distribution and fit it to data. For a first-timer, here is the idea without the machinery.

Fitting a fixed Gaussian is just estimating a mean and a variance. A conditional density like q(θ|x) is a Gaussian (or richer shape) whose mean and width are functions of x — a little network reads x and outputs those numbers. Training tunes the network to make the observed pairs as likely as possible (maximum likelihood):

maximise  ∑₋ log q(θ₋ | x₋)   over the network’s weights “make the network assign high probability to the parameter that actually produced each simulation”

Normalizing flows, in one paragraph

Real posteriors aren’t always Gaussian — they can be skewed, banana-shaped, multi-modal. A normalizing flow builds a flexible density by taking a simple one (a standard Gaussian) and passing it through a stack of smooth, invertible transformations, each reshaping it a little. Because every step is invertible with a tractable stretch factor (Jacobian), you can still compute exact probabilities. Think of it as kneading a plain lump of dough into any shape while always knowing how much each region was stretched.

On our dial the true posterior is Gaussian, so a plain conditional Gaussian is already a perfect flow with zero layers — which is why our NPE below is just a linear fit. Keep the flow idea in your pocket for the day the posterior is a banana.

Neural Posterior Estimation

Aim the network straight at the answer

NPE learns q(θ|x)≈p(θ|x) directly. Train a conditional density on the simulated pairs; then evaluate it at xobs and you’re done — the network outputs the posterior. No MCMC, no tolerance.

For our dial, the true posterior is Gaussian with a mean that is linear in x, so the “network” that maximises ∑ log q(θ₋|x₋) is exactly a least-squares line plus a residual spread. That’s genuine amortized NPE, just with the simplest possible density:

The optimal slope came out near 0.9, which is exactly the Bayesian shrinkage factor τ²/(τ²+σ²)=9/10 from page 3. The network rediscovered Bayes’ rule from simulations alone, never seeing the formula.

Why practitioners love NPE: the output is the posterior itself, ready to sample, and it’s amortized. With a normalizing flow instead of a line, the same recipe handles arbitrarily shaped posteriors.

Train once, answer any dataset instantly

This is the superpower that sets neural SBI apart from ABC. ABC-MCMC must re-run from scratch for every new observation. Amortized NPE learns a single function of x, so a brand-new reading gets its posterior by one forward pass — no new simulations at all.

Slide the observed reading. The violet posterior updates instantly from the one pre-trained map; the grey scatter is the training set and the line is the learned mean. Nothing is being re-simulated — you’re just evaluating the amortized network.

Amortization changes the economics. Pay a big upfront simulation+training cost once; then serve millions of datasets essentially free. For experiments that repeat (every new patient, every new sky survey tile, every new event), this is transformative — and impossible with per-dataset ABC-MCMC.

Neural Likelihood Estimation

Emulate the simulator, then do Bayes

NLE learns the other conditional: q(x|θ)≈p(x|θ) — a fast surrogate for the simulator’s output distribution. Once you have it, plug in xobs to get a likelihood function of θ, multiply by the prior, and sample with ordinary MCMC.

learn q(x|θ) from sims  →  p(θ|xobs) ∝ q(xobs|θ) p(θ) on the dial: fit mean(x|θ) & spread, then evaluate the density at xobs=2 across a θ grid

When NLE shines

You want to swap priors later, or combine independent observations (multiply likelihoods), or you trust a likelihood surrogate more than a posterior one. The likelihood is often simpler/smoother than the posterior.

The cost

You still need MCMC at the end to turn the learned likelihood into samples — so it’s only partly amortized: the likelihood is reusable, but each xobs needs a fresh MCMC run.

Neural Ratio Estimation

A classifier in disguise

The most surprising of the three. Train a classifier to distinguish two kinds of pairs:

  • Joint pairs (θ, x) where x really was simulated from that θ  (label 1),
  • Marginal pairs (θ, x) where θ and x are shuffled apart  (label 0).

A beautiful fact: the Bayes-optimal classifier’s output secretly encodes the likelihood-to-evidence ratio. Recover it from the logit:

r(θ,x) = p(x|θ) / p(x) = d(θ,x) / (1 − d(θ,x)),   logit d = log r then posterior p(θ|xobs) ∝ r(θ,xobs) × p(θ)

So “how well can you tell real pairs from fake pairs?” is the likelihood ratio. Multiply by the prior for the posterior. On the dial we fit logistic regression on quadratic features of (θ,x) — here is the resulting posterior against the truth:

Why it’s loved: classification is the most robust, best-understood task in ML, and you never model a full density — only a scalar ratio. It also plugs directly into hypothesis testing (the ratio is the test statistic).

Focus the simulations where they matter

Amortized methods train on parameters from the prior, spending simulations everywhere — even in regions xobs rules out. If you only care about one observation and simulations are precious, be greedy: use the current posterior estimate to choose the next parameters to simulate, then retrain. Iterate.

# Sequential (S) variant — SNPE / SNLE / SNRE proposal ← prior repeat a few rounds: simulate N pairs with θ ~ proposal # aim sims near x_obs train the estimator on all pairs so far proposal ← current posterior estimate # zoom in for next round

Each of the three has a sequential cousin: SNPE, SNLE, SNRE. They reach a given accuracy with far fewer simulations — vital when one simulation costs hours of supercomputer time.

The trade you’re making Sequential methods sacrifice amortization: by focusing on one xobs, the trained network is no longer valid for arbitrary datasets. And drawing θ from a proposal that isn’t the prior requires a correction (importance weights, or a modified loss) — the subtle bookkeeping that SNPE-A/B/C papers are about.

Rule of thumb: many datasets → amortized (train once, serve all). One precious dataset, expensive simulator → sequential (spend every simulation near the answer).

The cost picture

Amortized

Big one-time cost: many prior simulations + training. Then any number of datasets answered instantly.

Break-even: worth it when you’ll analyse many observations, or need real-time inference, or want to run SBC calibration (which needs thousands of posteriors — free once amortized).

Sequential

Lower total simulations for a single target, spent adaptively. But re-run the whole procedure for the next dataset.

Break-even: worth it when the simulator is the bottleneck and you have one (or few) observations to explain.

Calibration • the step people skip and regret

A posterior can be confidently wrong

Every method above returns a posterior. None of them guarantees it’s correct. A too-small ε, an insufficient summary, an under-trained network — each can produce a posterior that is too narrow (overconfident), too wide, or biased. On a real problem you have no truth curve to check against. So how do you know?

The answer exploits a self-consistency the Bayesian framework must obey. If your inference is correct, then a parameter drawn from the prior should look, statistically, like a typical draw from the posterior you compute for its data. Turn that into a test you can run with only the simulator.

draw θ⁎ ~ prior  →  x⁎ = simulate(θ⁎)  →  posterior for x⁎  →  where does θ⁎ rank among posterior draws? repeat thousands of times and look at the distribution of ranks
The promise: if the posteriors are calibrated, those ranks are uniform — a flat histogram. Any systematic shape is a fingerprint of a specific failure. This is Simulation-Based Calibration, and it needs nothing but the simulator.

SBC is a necessary check, not a sufficient one: passing it means the average-over-the-prior calibration is fine, which can hide problems localised to a particular xobs. Complement it with coverage tests (do 90% credible intervals contain the truth 90% of the time?) and, for amortized methods, local coverage at the specific xobs you care about.

Culture point In modern SBI, reporting a posterior without a calibration check is considered incomplete. It is the field’s answer to “neural nets can be confidently wrong.” Budget simulations for it — amortized methods make it nearly free.

Let the network find the statistics

Recall ABC’s Achilles heel: you must hand-pick summary statistics S(x), and a bad choice silently widens the posterior. Neural SBI can learn S. An embedding network sits in front of the density/ratio estimator, maps raw high-dimensional data (a time series, an image) down to a handful of learned features, and is trained jointly with the rest — so the features are optimised for inference, not guessed.

raw data x  →  embedding net  →  learned summary Sφ(x)  →  NPE / NLE / NRE the summary and the inference are trained together, end to end

This closes the loop opened on the sufficiency page. Where classical ABC needed a domain expert to nominate “peak height, peak time, total count,” a convolutional or recurrent embedding can distil near-sufficient summaries straight from raw output — often better than the hand-crafted ones, and with no manual tuning.

On our dial there is nothing to learn — x is already a scalar sufficient summary. But this is the reason neural SBI scaled to images and long time series where ABC stalled: it removed the human from the summary-design loop.
The quiet revolution: automatic, learned, near-sufficient summaries. This, more than the density estimator itself, is why SBI moved from toy problems to real high-dimensional science.

How everything connects

Step back and see the single family tree. Every method here is a way of doing Bayes with a simulator; they branch on what they approximate and how.

Bayes: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior │ can you evaluate the likelihood? ╱ ╲ yes no → only a simulator (MCMC, VI) │ ┌───────┴───────┐ match sims learn a function │ │ ┌─────┴────┐ ┌───────┼──────┐ rejection SMC/MCMC posterior likelihood ratio + reg. + synth-lik NPE NLE NRE (ABC family) (neural SBI)

And the deep links across the branches:

  • ABC → NPE: regression-adjusted ABC is a linear NPE; swap the linear fit for a flow and you have full NPE.
  • Synthetic likelihood → NLE: both learn p(x|θ); NLE just drops the Gaussian assumption for a flow.
  • NRE ↔ likelihood-ratio tests: the learned ratio is exactly the frequentist test statistic — SBI meets classical hypothesis testing.
  • All → SBC: one calibration test validates any of them, because all target the same posterior.

“This is just that, but…”

The fastest way to hold a field in your head is a set of “X is Y but Z” sentences. Here is the whole deck as those:

This……is that, but…
Rejection ABCis importance sampling from the prior, but with a hard distance filter instead of a likelihood weight.
ABC with a Gaussian kernelis kernel density estimation of the posterior, but with ε as the bandwidth.
ABC-MCMCis ordinary Metropolis MCMC, but the likelihood ratio is replaced by an accept-if-close test.
ABC-SMCis simulated annealing / tempering, but the temperature is the tolerance ε.
Regression-adjusted ABCis linear NPE, but with a straight line instead of a neural network.
Synthetic likelihoodis NLE, but assuming the summaries are Gaussian rather than learning their density.
NPEis supervised regression from x to θ, but predicting a whole distribution, not a point.
NLEis building a fast emulator of the simulator, but a probabilistic one you can do Bayes with.
NREis a binary classifier (real vs shuffled pairs), but its logit is secretly the log-likelihood-ratio.
Sequential (S-) variantsare the amortized methods, but with simulations aimed adaptively at one dataset.
SBCis a goodness-of-fit test, but for the entire inference procedure rather than a single fit.

Are ABC and SBI the same thing?

A question worth settling cleanly, because the terms get muddled. No — ABC is a member of the SBI family, not a synonym.

Simulation-Based Inference (SBI)

The umbrella: any method that does statistical inference using a simulator you can run but whose likelihood you can’t evaluate. Also called likelihood-free inference. Includes ABC, synthetic likelihood, and the neural methods.

Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC)

The original, sampling-based branch of SBI: simulate, compare via summaries/tolerance, accept the close ones. The C is “Computation,” not “Calculation.”

ABC  ⊂  SBI  (= likelihood-free inference) ABC is one technique under the SBI umbrella, alongside synthetic likelihood and neural NPE/NLE/NRE

Historically, “likelihood-free inference” and ABC were used almost interchangeably, because ABC was the method. The term “SBI” rose with the neural methods (roughly post-2016) precisely to name the broader family that ABC no longer covered alone. So an older paper saying “likelihood-free” usually means ABC; a recent one saying “SBI” usually means the neural toolbox — but both live under the same roof.

One-liner: all ABC is SBI; not all SBI is ABC. If someone says “we used SBI,” ask which method — it could be a pencil-and-paper rejection sampler or a normalizing flow with an embedding net.

Where people go wrong

Classical ABC

  • ε too large and never checked → posterior secretly = prior.
  • Insufficient summaries → irreducible over-wide posterior, no ε can save it.
  • Unscaled summaries → one large-magnitude statistic dominates the distance. Standardise!
  • Reporting the raw ε sample without regression adjustment → avoidable bias.

Neural SBI

  • Too few simulations → over-fit, overconfident posteriors (U-shaped SBC).
  • Skipping calibration → confidently wrong, and you never know.
  • Sequential proposal correction forgotten → biased posteriors in SNPE.
  • Trusting amortized posteriors far from training data → silent extrapolation failure. Check local coverage.
The universal one Model misspecification. Every method assumes your simulator can actually produce data like the real observation. If it can’t, ABC finds nothing within ε and neural methods extrapolate confidently into nonsense. Always simulate from your fitted posterior and check the fake data resembles the real data (posterior predictive check).

Before you believe any SBI posterior

  1. Posterior predictive check. Simulate from your inferred θ. Does the fake data look like xobs? If not, stop — the model is misspecified.
  2. Convergence in ε / sims. For ABC, does the posterior stop moving as ε shrinks? For neural, as you add simulations? If it’s still drifting, you haven’t converged.
  3. SBC rank histogram. Flat? Or U / dome / sloped? Diagnose and fix per page 29.
  4. Coverage. Do your X% credible intervals contain the truth X% of the time across simulated trials? Check globally and locally at xobs.
  5. Sensitivity to summaries. Does adding/removing a summary change the answer a lot? That flags information loss or noise.
  6. Two methods agree? Cross-check with a second method (e.g. SMC-ABC vs NPE). Disagreement is a red flag worth chasing.
The mindset: in likelihood-free inference you never see the likelihood, so you can’t rely on its usual diagnostics. You buy trust through simulation-based checks — the same simulator that gave you the posterior also audits it.

Recipes you can run with a pencil

Each of these you can literally do on paper for the dial (draws from 𝒩(0,9), noise from 𝒩(0,1), target xobs=2). This is the whole deck compressed to actions.

Rejection ABC

1. Draw θ from prior. 2. Compute x = θ + noise. 3. Keep θ if |x−2| < ε. 4. Repeat; the kept θ’s are your posterior. Smaller ε = sharper but slower.

Regression adjustment

Do rejection ABC with a loose ε. Fit a line θ vs x on the kept pairs (slope β). Replace each θ with θ − β(x−2). Removes the ε bias.

Synthetic likelihood

For each candidate θ on a grid: simulate m times, take the mean & variance, evaluate a Gaussian at x=2. Multiply the grid by the prior, normalise. No ε.

NPE (linear)

Simulate many (θ,x) pairs. Fit a line predicting θ from x (least squares), note residual spread s. Posterior at x=2 is 𝒩(slope·2 + intercept, s²). Reusable for any x.

NRE (by counting)

Make joint pairs (θ,x) and shuffled pairs. Train any classifier. Its odds d/(1−d) at (θ,2) is the likelihood ratio; times the prior gives the posterior.

SBC (the check)

Draw θ⁎ from prior, simulate x⁎, build its posterior, record the rank of θ⁎ among posterior draws. Repeat. Histogram of ranks should be flat.

A decision guide

START: you have a simulator, a prior, and an observation x_obs. Is the data low-dimensional with obvious summaries? YES → is the simulator cheap? YES → rejection ABC (prototype), then SMC-ABC NO → SMC-ABC or sequential neural (SNPE/SNLE) NO (raw time series / images / high-dim) → use a neural method with a learned embedding: many datasets to analyse? → amortized NPE one precious dataset? → SNPE / SNLE / SNRE need a reusable likelihood? → NLE want a test statistic / robustness? → NRE ALWAYS → run SBC + posterior predictive check before believing anything.

If you take one path

For most modern problems: amortized NPE with an embedding net, validated by SBC. It gives instant posteriors, scales to real data, and makes calibration cheap. Reach for the alternatives only when a specific need (reusable likelihood, one costly dataset) points elsewhere.

Software worth knowing

The sbi package (mackelab) implements NPE/NLE/NRE and SBC; pyABC and ABCpy cover the ABC family with SMC. This deck rebuilt the ideas from scratch so the packages feel transparent, not magical.

Where SBI earns its keep

Any field with a trusted forward simulator but no tractable likelihood is a candidate — which is most of computational science.

Physics & astronomy

Cosmological parameters from large-scale structure and lensing; gravitational-wave source properties; particle-physics event inference; exoplanet and stellar models.

Biology & epidemiology

Population genetics (ABC’s birthplace); epidemic parameters (R₀, incubation) from case curves; systems-biology reaction networks; neuroscience circuit models.

Earth & engineering

Climate and hydrology model calibration; seismic source inversion; radar/sensor tracking; reliability and queueing models.

Economics & finance

Agent-based market models; structural macro models; stochastic volatility where the likelihood integral is intractable.

Neuroscience

Fitting biophysical neuron and network models (e.g. Hodgkin–Huxley) to recordings — a flagship success of amortized NPE.

Machine learning itself

Calibrating expensive black-box simulators, likelihood-free model comparison, and Bayesian experimental design.

The common signature: “I can simulate this process forward realistically, but I can’t write down the probability of my data.” Whenever you hear that sentence, SBI is the toolkit.

How the field arrived here

A quick timeline helps place the methods relative to each other.

EraDevelopment
~1984Rubin frames the idea: accept parameters whose simulated data matches the observed — the conceptual seed of ABC.
1997–99Population genetics (Tavaré, Pritchard et al.) turns it into practical rejection ABC with summaries and tolerance.
2002Beaumont, Zhang & Balding: regression adjustment, a big accuracy jump.
2003Marjoram et al.: ABC-MCMC.
2007–09Sisson, Toni et al.: ABC-SMC, the efficient sequential workhorse.
2010Wood: synthetic likelihood — model the summary density instead of rejecting.
2016–19The neural wave: Papamakarios & Murray (NPE/NLE with flows), Hermans et al. (NRE), sequential SNPE-A/B/C. “SBI” becomes the umbrella term.
2018Talts et al.: Simulation-Based Calibration — the universal trust check.
2020Cranmer, Brehmer & Louppe: “The frontier of simulation-based inference” unifies the field in one review.
The arc: from “reject what doesn’t match” (1984–2010) to “learn a function from every simulation” (2016–), with calibration maturing alongside so the neural posteriors could be trusted.

Plain-language glossary




Simulatora program that turns parameters θ into synthetic data x, using internal randomness.


Likelihood p(x|θ)the probability of data given parameters. In SBI you can sample it (run the sim) but not evaluate it.


Posterior p(θ|x)your updated belief about θ after seeing x. The goal of every method here.


Prior p(θ)your belief about θ before seeing data.


Tolerance εABC’s accept radius: keep a simulation if it lands within ε of the observation.


Summary statistic S(x)a low-dimensional feature of the data used for comparison instead of raw x.


Sufficient statistica summary that loses no information about θ; using it, ABC targets the true posterior.


Amortizedtrained once, then answers any new dataset instantly with no new simulations.


Normalizing flowa flexible learned distribution built by warping a simple one through invertible maps.


Embedding networka net that learns summary statistics from raw data, trained jointly with the estimator.


NPE / NLE / NREneural methods learning the posterior / likelihood / likelihood-ratio respectively.


SBCSimulation-Based Calibration: a rank test that checks whether posteriors are trustworthy.


Posterior predictive checksimulate from the inferred θ and verify the fake data resembles the real data.
