The No-U-Turn Sampler

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo works beautifully once you know how long to follow each trajectory. NUTS works that out for itself: it runs the dynamics forwards and backwards, doubling the length each round, and stops the moment the path starts to double back on itself.

NUTS is the default sampler in Stan, PyMC and NumPyro, so in practice "running HMC" almost always means running NUTS. Its two adaptations, of path length and of step size, are what make it usable without expert tuning.

The problem

How long should the trajectory be?

One HMC iteration draws a momentum p∼N(0,M)\boldsymbol p\sim\mathcal N(0,M)p∼N(0,M) and follows Hamilton's equations for LLL leapfrog steps of size ε\varepsilonε. The step size is set by stability, ε<2ℓ\varepsilon<2\ellε<2ℓ with ℓ\ellℓ the narrowest width. The path length LεL\varepsilonLε is a free choice, and it decides everything:

  • Too short: the sampler is a random walk again, with gradients wasted.
  • Too long: the trajectory curls back on itself; the extra gradient evaluations buy nothing.
  • Exactly resonant: for a Gaussian of width σ\sigmaσ, Lε≈πσL\varepsilon\approx\pi\sigmaLε≈πσ maps θ↦2μ−θ\theta\mapsto2\mu-\thetaθ↦2μ−θ every time. The chain then flips between two points with 100% acceptance — perfect diagnostics, no information.

And the right value is different in every direction and at every point of a curved posterior.

Seen in the test problems

Fixed-length HMC on the one-dimensional target hit exactly this resonance, with 100% acceptance and a chain alternating between two values. Jittering LLL removes it; NUTS removes the choice altogether.

Physics picture

A particle in a harmonic well returns to its starting point after one period. Sampling it once per period tells you nothing; sampling it after about half a period moves you as far as the dynamics can.

The "optimal" path length for a Gaussian is a quarter to a half period. NUTS effectively discovers that locally, at every iteration, without knowing the period.

The criterion

Stop when the ends start approaching

The separation vector is Δ=θ+−θ−\boldsymbol\Delta=\boldsymbol\theta^+-\boldsymbol\theta^-Δ=θ+−θ−, and it is still growing as long as both ends move apart:

that is, when extending the path further would start bringing the ends back together. At that point the trajectory has covered as much of the distribution as this direction of travel allows.

Building the trajectory
  1. Start with the current point as a trajectory of length 1.
  2. Choose a direction of time at random, forwards or backwards.
  3. Double the trajectory in that direction: run 2j2^j2j more leapfrog steps.
  4. Stop if the U-turn test fires anywhere in the new part, or across the whole trajectory.

Doubling keeps the set of visited points balanced, which is what allows the sampler to remain exact.

Choosing the next state

The original paper draws a slice level u∼U(0,e−H0)u\sim\mathcal U(0,e^{-H_0})u∼U(0,e−H0​) and samples uniformly among the points with e−H>ue^{-H}>ue−H>u. Modern implementations use multinomial sampling with weights e−He^{-H}e−H, which is a little more efficient. Either choice preserves detailed balance.

The subtlety is that you must not simply take the last point: that would break reversibility. Doubling plus a balanced selection rule is what keeps the algorithm exact.

Keeping it exact

Why doubling, and not just "stop when it turns"

A sampler is valid if the probability of proposing BBB from AAA matches, in the right sense, the probability of proposing AAA from BBB. A naive rule — integrate forwards until the U-turn and take the last point — fails this: from the new point, the trajectory that would be built is not the same set.

NUTS fixes it with two devices:

  • Random time direction at each doubling, so the trajectory grows both ways.
  • Sub-tree checks: the U-turn test is applied to every balanced sub-tree, not just the whole trajectory. This makes the stopping rule a property of the set of points, not of the order in which they were built.

With those, every point in the final trajectory would have built the same trajectory, and selecting among them with weights e−He^{-H}e−H leaves π\piπ invariant.

Cost

A tree of depth jjj costs 2j−12^j-12j−1 gradient evaluations, and an iteration doubles until the U-turn or a maximum depth (10 by default in Stan: 1023 steps). On the ridge test NUTS averaged about 30 gradients per iteration, against about 90 for the fixed-length HMC, for a similar answer.

Physics picture

Think of it as integrating the orbit in both directions from the current phase-space point until the excursion stops growing — roughly half an orbit — then picking a new point from that arc with Boltzmann weights.

Betancourt's review is the clearest account of why the naive version is wrong and how the balanced trees repair it.

Adaptation and diagnostics

What warm-up actually does

Step size ε

Tuned by dual averaging during warm-up to hit a target acceptance statistic, 0.8 by default. Higher targets mean smaller steps, deeper trees and more robustness in curved geometry.

Mass matrix M

Estimated from warm-up samples, usually diagonal (M−1≈M^{-1}\approxM−1≈ the posterior variances). This is preconditioning: it rescales each direction so the dynamics sees a rounder distribution.

Warm-up windows

Stan interleaves the two: short windows to find ε, longer ones to estimate MMM, then a final ε tuning. Warm-up samples are discarded because the chain is not yet π\piπ-invariant while adapting.

The two warnings you will meet

Divergent transitions. The energy error ∣ΔH∣|\Delta H|∣ΔH∣ blows up, usually where the posterior narrows sharply (a funnel). The sampler cannot enter that region properly, so the affected area is under-sampled. Fix by raising the acceptance target, or better, by reparameterising (non-centred form).

Maximum tree depth reached. Not a correctness problem but an efficiency one: the trajectory was cut off before the U-turn, usually because the posterior is badly scaled in some direction. Fix with a better mass matrix or reparameterisation.

Divergences matter: a handful in a long run is often harmless, but a systematic pattern means part of the posterior is not being explored. Look at where they occur in parameter space.

The instructive failure

On the four-mode target NUTS crossed a barrier once, by luck, and then spent 63% of its samples in a mode whose true weight is 0.20. A single lucky crossing produced a more confidently wrong answer than never crossing at all: the chain looks well-mixed between two modes and reports tight error bars.

On the ridge, by contrast, it matched fixed-length HMC while needing no tuning.

The lesson for practice: NUTS should be your default for smooth unimodal posteriors, and should always be checked against dispersed starts or a tempered or nested run when multimodality is possible.

Summary

When to reach for it

Good when…

  • the posterior is smooth, continuous and mostly unimodal;
  • gradients are available through automatic differentiation;
  • the dimension is moderate to large, tens to thousands;
  • you want a sampler that tunes itself and warns you when it struggles.

Bad when…

  • modes are separated by regions of very low probability;
  • parameters are discrete, or the density has kinks or hard edges;
  • the geometry is funnel-shaped and cannot be reparameterised;
  • you need the evidence ZZZ: use nested sampling.

Background: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Contrast: random-walk Metropolis, which is blind to gradients and therefore, on the four-mode problem, less confidently wrong.

In the satellite-RFI analysis NUTS is used through NumPyro; the same warnings apply there.
