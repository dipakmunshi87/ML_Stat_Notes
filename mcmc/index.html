The problem

Inference gives a probability density over parameters, known only up to a constant:

π(θ)=L(θ) p(θ)Z  ≡  e−U(θ)Z,Z=∫L p  dθ  (usually unknown).\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{\mathcal L(\boldsymbol\theta)\,p(\boldsymbol\theta)}{Z} \;\equiv\;\frac{e^{-U(\boldsymbol\theta)}}{Z},\qquad Z=\int \mathcal L\,p\;d\boldsymbol\theta\ \ \text{(usually unknown)} .π(θ)=ZL(θ)p(θ)​≡Ze−U(θ)​,Z=∫Lpdθ  (usually unknown).

Everything we want is an average over it — a mean, an error bar, a marginal, a credible region:

Eπ[f]=∫f(θ) π(θ) dθ  ≈  1N∑n=1Nf(θ(n)),θ(n)∼π.\mathbb E_\pi[f]=\int f(\boldsymbol\theta)\,\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta \;\approx\;\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}f\big(\boldsymbol\theta^{(n)}\big),\qquad \boldsymbol\theta^{(n)}\sim\pi .Eπ​[f]=∫f(θ)π(θ)dθ≈N1​n=1∑N​f(θ(n)),θ(n)∼π.

In more than about five dimensions, grids are hopeless: ten points per axis means 10d10^d10d evaluations. So we generate a sequence of parameter values whose histogram converges to π\piπ. That is Monte Carlo.

Physics picture

π∝e−U\pi\propto e^{-U}π∝e−U is a Boltzmann distribution at temperature kBT=1k_BT=1kB​T=1, with U=−log⁡πU=-\log\piU=−logπ playing the potential energy. Sampling a posterior and sampling the equilibrium states of a system in a heat bath are the same computational problem, and the algorithms came from that side first.

The one thing that makes it possible

Every method here uses only ratios of the density, π(θ′)/π(θ)\pi(\boldsymbol\theta')/\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)π(θ′)/π(θ), or levels of it. The unknown constant ZZZ cancels — except in nested sampling, which is built to compute it.

What a Markov chain gives you

A Markov chain is a rule T(θ→θ′)T(\boldsymbol\theta\to\boldsymbol\theta')T(θ→θ′) for taking one step at random, using only where you are now. It is useful if π\piπ is stationary under it, meaning that a sample from π\piπ stays a sample from π\piπ:

∫π(θ) T(θ→θ′) dθ=π(θ′).\int \pi(\boldsymbol\theta)\,T(\boldsymbol\theta\to\boldsymbol\theta')\,d\boldsymbol\theta =\pi(\boldsymbol\theta') .∫π(θ)T(θ→θ′)dθ=π(θ′).

The usual way to guarantee that is detailed balance, the microscopic reversibility condition

π(θ) T(θ→θ′)=π(θ′) T(θ′→θ),\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)\,T(\boldsymbol\theta\to\boldsymbol\theta') =\pi(\boldsymbol\theta')\,T(\boldsymbol\theta'\to\boldsymbol\theta),π(θ)T(θ→θ′)=π(θ′)T(θ′→θ),

together with the chain being able to reach everywhere. Then time averages converge to expectations. What the theory does not say is how long that takes — and the whole practical art of MCMC lives in that gap.

Cost

Compare samplers by error per density or gradient evaluation, never per iteration: one HMC step can cost a hundred Metropolis steps and buy far more, or on the wrong problem, nothing.

Diagnostics

Multiple chains from dispersed starts, R^\hat RR^, effective sample size and divergence warnings are necessary but never sufficient: no statistic can see a mode the chain never visited.

The five methods

Each deck stands alone; this is the order they build on each other.

1

Random-walk Metropolis →

Propose a small random step, accept it with probability min⁡(1,π′/π)\min(1,\pi'/\pi)min(1,π′/π). The baseline everything else is measured against.

Needs: the density only  ·  Cost: O(d2)\mathcal O(d^2)O(d2)  ·  Watch for: step-size tuning, the 0.234 rule
2

Gibbs sampling →

Update one coordinate at a time from its exact conditional. Nothing is ever rejected, but every move is parallel to an axis.

Needs: conditionals you can sample  ·  Cost: no tuning  ·  Watch for: strong correlations
3

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo →

Give the parameters a momentum and follow Hamilton's equations, so the sampler glides along the distribution instead of diffusing across it.

Needs: gradients  ·  Cost: O(d5/4)\mathcal O(d^{5/4})O(d5/4)  ·  Watch for: separated modes, resonance
4

The No-U-Turn Sampler →

HMC that chooses its own trajectory length by doubling until the path turns back. The default in Stan, PyMC and NumPyro.

Needs: gradients  ·  Cost: self-tuning  ·  Watch for: divergences, tree depth
5

Nested sampling →

Not a Markov chain: squeeze an ensemble of live points upwards in likelihood, computing the evidence ZZZ and getting posterior samples as a by-product.

Needs: constrained prior draws  ·  Gives: ZZZ with an error bar  ·  Watch for: dimension

Three test problems, with exact answers

All five methods were run on the same three targets. Every reference value is known in closed form, so the tables report error rather than a convergence diagnostic, and the budget is matched in density or gradient evaluations.

1 · One dimension

θ∼N(0,1)\theta\sim\mathcal N(0,1)θ∼N(0,1) with one measurement s=θ+ns=\theta+ns=θ+n, n∼N(0,0.52)n\sim\mathcal N(0,0.5^2)n∼N(0,0.52), s=2.0s=2.0s=2.0. Exact posterior N(1.600,0.4472)\mathcal N(1.600,0.447^2)N(1.600,0.4472). Nothing is hard here; it exists to show that the ranking depends on which axis you plot against.

2 · A 20:1 ridge

One tight measurement of θ1+θ2\theta_1+\theta_2θ1​+θ2​ leaves the difference at prior width: widths 1.000 and 0.0499. A random walk needs (L/ℓ)2=400(L/\ell)^2=400(L/ℓ)2=400 steps per independent sample. This is the case HMC was built for.

3 · Four modes

Four Gaussians at the corners of a square, weights 0.40/0.25/0.20/0.15, 8.9 standard deviations apart. The winners of problem 2 fail here, and report converged, confidently wrong answers.

The one-line summary

No method wins twice. Gradients are decisive on a correlated, smooth posterior and actively harmful when the modes are separated, because they point back into the mode you are already in.

Choosing a sampler

A practical order

  1. Smooth, unimodal, gradients available → NUTS.
  2. Conditionally conjugate or with discrete latent variables → Gibbs, or Gibbs with other updates inside it.
  3. No gradients, modest dimension, quick answer needed → random-walk Metropolis.
  4. Evidence needed, or multimodality suspected → nested sampling, or parallel tempering.
  5. Understanding why any of it works → HMC, which is where the physics is clearest.

Three habits

  1. Test on a problem whose answer you know before trusting one whose answer you do not.
  2. Compare per evaluation, not per iteration.
  3. Run several chains from dispersed starts; if they disagree, believe the disagreement.

And remember that a beautifully mixed chain is evidence about the region it explored, and no evidence at all about the region it did not.
