This deck is self-contained: every equation is explained from Hamiltonian mechanics, and the results come from a study in which all three target distributions have closed-form answers, so errors are measured, not guessed from convergence diagnostics.

The problem

We want samples, not a formula

Bayesian inference gives a posterior density over parameters θ∈Rd\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathbb R^dθ∈Rd, known only up to a constant:

π(θ)  ∝  L(data∣θ)⏟likelihood  p(θ)⏟prior  ≡  e−U(θ),U=−log⁡π.\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)\;\propto\;\underbrace{\mathcal L(\text{data}\mid\boldsymbol\theta)}_{\text{likelihood}}\;\underbrace{p(\boldsymbol\theta)}_{\text{prior}}\;\equiv\;e^{-U(\boldsymbol\theta)},\qquad U=-\log\pi .π(θ)∝likelihoodL(data∣θ)​​priorp(θ)​​≡e−U(θ),U=−logπ.

Every answer we want is an average over it: means, error bars, correlations, marginals.

Eπ[f]=∫f(θ) π(θ) dθ  ≈  1N∑n=1Nf(θ(n)),θ(n)∼π.\mathbb E_\pi[f]=\int f(\boldsymbol\theta)\,\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta\;\approx\;\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N f(\boldsymbol\theta^{(n)}),\qquad \boldsymbol\theta^{(n)}\sim\pi .Eπ​[f]=∫f(θ)π(θ)dθ≈N1​n=1∑N​f(θ(n)),θ(n)∼π.

In d≳5d\gtrsim 5d≳5 dimensions grids are hopeless: 101010 points per axis means 10d10^d10d evaluations. So we build a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is π\piπ (Markov chain Monte Carlo, MCMC).

Physics picture

π∝e−U\pi\propto e^{-U}π∝e−U is a Boltzmann distribution at temperature kBT=1k_BT=1kB​T=1, with UUU playing the potential energy. Sampling a posterior is the same job as sampling the equilibrium states of a system in a heat bath.

Statistics vocabulary

Target = the distribution π\piπ. Chain = the sequence of samples. Effective sample size = how many independent samples the correlated chain is worth.

Only need

U(θ)U(\boldsymbol\theta)U(θ) and, for HMC, its gradient ∇U\nabla U∇U. The normalising constant is never needed.

Point out that the normalisation (the evidence) is exactly what we cannot compute, and that MCMC never needs it: only ratios π(θ′)/π(θ)=e−(U′−U)\pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta)=e^{-(U'-U)}π(θ′)/π(θ)=e−(U′−U) appear.

The baseline

A random walk is diffusion, and diffusion is slow

Random-walk Metropolis

  1. Propose θ′=θ+σstep z\boldsymbol\theta'=\boldsymbol\theta+\sigma_{\rm step}\,\boldsymbol zθ′=θ+σstep​z, with z∼N(0,I)\boldsymbol z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)z∼N(0,I).
  2. Accept with probability min⁡ ⁣(1,  e−[U(θ′)−U(θ)])\min\!\big(1,\;e^{-[U(\boldsymbol\theta')-U(\boldsymbol\theta)]}\big)min(1,e−[U(θ′)−U(θ)]), otherwise stay put.

This satisfies detailed balance, π(θ)T(θ→θ′)=π(θ′)T(θ′→θ)\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)T(\boldsymbol\theta\to\boldsymbol\theta')=\pi(\boldsymbol\theta')T(\boldsymbol\theta'\to\boldsymbol\theta)π(θ)T(θ→θ′)=π(θ′)T(θ′→θ), so π\piπ is stationary.

The cost

The step must be as small as the narrowest direction ℓ\ellℓ, or almost every proposal is rejected. Crossing the widest direction LLL is then a random walk of nnn steps, travelling only ℓn\ell\sqrt nℓn​:

ℓn∼L⟹n∼(Lℓ)2 steps per independent sample.\ell\sqrt n\sim L\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n\sim\Big(\frac{L}{\ell}\Big)^{2}\ \text{steps per independent sample.}ℓn​∼L⟹n∼(ℓL​)2 steps per independent sample.

θ∼N(0,I2)\boldsymbol\theta\sim\mathcal N(0,I_2)θ∼N(0,I2​), and one precise measurement of the sum y=θ1+θ2+ny=\theta_1+\theta_2+ny=θ1​+θ2​+n, n∼N(0,1/200)n\sim\mathcal N(0,1/200)n∼N(0,1/200). The difference stays at prior width.

Physics picture

Overdamped Brownian motion: displacement grows as t\sqrt tt​, not ttt. What we want is ballistic motion, distance ∝t\propto t∝t. That requires momentum.

The (L/ℓ)2(L/\ell)^2(L/ℓ)2 argument is the whole motivation for HMC. In high dimension the same thing happens without any visible "ridge": the step size must shrink like d−1/2d^{-1/2}d−1/2 to keep acceptance up.

The key idea

Give the parameters a momentum

Introduce an auxiliary momentum p∈Rd\boldsymbol p\in\mathbb R^dp∈Rd, one component per parameter, and a Hamiltonian:

H(θ,p)=U(θ)⏟potential+12 p ⁣⊤M−1p⏟kinetic,U=−log⁡π.H(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol p)=\underbrace{U(\boldsymbol\theta)}_{\text{potential}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\,\boldsymbol p^{\!\top}M^{-1}\boldsymbol p}_{\text{kinetic}},\qquad U=-\log\pi .H(θ,p)=potentialU(θ)​​+kinetic21​p⊤M−1p​​,U=−logπ.

MMM is a mass matrix we are free to choose (often M=IM=IM=I). Now sample the joint "canonical" distribution:

π(θ,p)  ∝  e−H=e−U(θ)⏟π(θ)  e−12p ⁣⊤M−1p⏟N(0, M).\pi(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol p)\;\propto\;e^{-H}=\underbrace{e^{-U(\boldsymbol\theta)}}_{\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)}\;\underbrace{e^{-\frac12\boldsymbol p^{\!\top}M^{-1}\boldsymbol p}}_{\mathcal N(0,\,M)} .π(θ,p)∝e−H=π(θ)e−U(θ)​​N(0,M)e−21​p⊤M−1p​​.

It factorises: θ\boldsymbol\thetaθ and p\boldsymbol pp are independent, and the θ\boldsymbol\thetaθ-marginal is exactly our target. So we sample (θ,p)(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol p)(θ,p) and simply throw p\boldsymbol pp away.

Physics picture

A particle of mass MMM in potential UUU, in contact with a heat bath at T=1T=1T=1. Maxwell–Boltzmann momenta, Boltzmann positions, independent of each other, exactly as in the canonical ensemble.

Why bother?

Because the joint distribution lets us move with Hamiltonian dynamics, which follows the landscape over long distances without diffusing, and (almost) never gets rejected.

History

Invented in lattice QCD as Hybrid Monte Carlo (Duane, Kennedy, Pendleton & Roweth, 1987). Brought to statistics by Radford Neal in the 1990s.

Emphasise that the momentum is fictitious: it has no meaning in the physical problem being inferred. The "particle" lives in parameter space, and the "potential" is minus the log posterior: valleys are where the posterior is high.

Hamilton's equations

Three properties of mechanics that MCMC needs

dθdt=∂H∂p=M−1p,dpdt=−∂H∂θ=−∇U(θ)=∇log⁡π(θ).\frac{d\boldsymbol\theta}{dt}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol p}=M^{-1}\boldsymbol p,\qquad \frac{d\boldsymbol p}{dt}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}=-\nabla U(\boldsymbol\theta)=\nabla\log\pi(\boldsymbol\theta).dtdθ​=∂p∂H​=M−1p,dtdp​=−∂θ∂H​=−∇U(θ)=∇logπ(θ).

The force on the particle is the gradient of the log posterior: it is pushed towards high probability, while its momentum carries it on.

① Energy conservation

dHdt=0\dfrac{dH}{dt}=0dtdH​=0. The joint density e−He^{-H}e−H is unchanged along a trajectory, so a Metropolis test would accept every move.

② Volume preservation

Liouville's theorem: phase-space volume is conserved, so the map has Jacobian 111. The acceptance rule needs no Jacobian correction.

③ Time reversibility

Run for time TTT, flip p→−p\boldsymbol p\to-\boldsymbol pp→−p, run again: you are back where you started. That gives detailed balance.

The catch

We cannot solve these equations exactly for a real posterior. A numerical integrator breaks ① slightly. The question is whether it can keep ② and ③ exactly. The leapfrog integrator does.

This is the conceptual heart. Energy conservation means high acceptance; volume preservation and reversibility together mean the proposal is symmetric in the right sense, so the simple Metropolis ratio is correct.

Leapfrog: the integrator that respects the geometry

One step of size ε\varepsilonε: half kick, full drift, half kick.

p1/2=p−ε2∇U(θ)θ′=θ+ε M−1p1/2p′=p1/2−ε2∇U(θ′)\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol p_{1/2}&=\boldsymbol p-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla U(\boldsymbol\theta)\\ \boldsymbol\theta'&=\boldsymbol\theta+\varepsilon\,M^{-1}\boldsymbol p_{1/2}\\ \boldsymbol p'&=\boldsymbol p_{1/2}-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla U(\boldsymbol\theta') \end{aligned}p1/2​θ′p′​=p−2ε​∇U(θ)=θ+εM−1p1/2​=p1/2​−2ε​∇U(θ′)​
  • Each sub-step is a shear in phase space, so volume is conserved exactly.
  • The pattern is symmetric, so it is exactly reversible.
  • It is symplectic: it conserves a nearby "shadow" Hamiltonian, so the energy error stays bounded, ∣ΔH∣=O(ε2)|\Delta H|=\mathcal O(\varepsilon^2)∣ΔH∣=O(ε2), instead of drifting.
  • Stability: for a harmonic well of width σ\sigmaσ, leapfrog is stable only if ε<2σ\varepsilon<2\sigmaε<2σ.

Harmonic oscillator U=θ2/2U=\theta^2/2U=θ2/2 (so σ=1\sigma=1σ=1), 60 steps from (θ,p)=(1,0)(\theta,p)=(1,0)(θ,p)=(1,0). Dashed dark blue: the exact orbit. Red: plain Euler spirals outwards, gaining energy every step. Green: leapfrog stays on a slightly squashed ellipse. Push ε past 2 to see leapfrog go unstable.

Try ε = 0.3, then 1.5 (leapfrog still bounded, just a distorted ellipse), then 2.1 (it blows up). The Euler method is not symplectic: it multiplies phase-space area by 1+ε21+\varepsilon^21+ε2 each step.

The algorithm

One HMC iteration in four moves

  1. Refresh the momentum: draw p∼N(0,M)\boldsymbol p\sim\mathcal N(0,M)p∼N(0,M). This is an exact Gibbs step for p\boldsymbol pp, and the only source of randomness in the dynamics.
  2. Simulate: take LLL leapfrog steps of size ε\varepsilonε from (θ,p)(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol p)(θ,p) to (θ∗,p∗)(\boldsymbol\theta^*,\boldsymbol p^*)(θ∗,p∗).
  3. Flip: set p∗→−p∗\boldsymbol p^*\to-\boldsymbol p^*p∗→−p∗. This makes the proposal its own inverse (irrelevant in practice, since p\boldsymbol pp is redrawn next).
  4. Correct: accept with probability α=min⁡ ⁣(1,  e−[H(θ∗,p∗)−H(θ,p)]),\alpha=\min\!\Big(1,\;e^{-\left[H(\boldsymbol\theta^*,\boldsymbol p^*)-H(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol p)\right]}\Big),α=min(1,e−[H(θ∗,p∗)−H(θ,p)]), otherwise keep θ\boldsymbol\thetaθ.
Why it is exact

Leapfrog is volume-preserving and reversible, so this is a valid Metropolis step on the joint (θ,p)(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol p)(θ,p). The integration error is not an approximation of the result: it only lowers the acceptance rate.

Physics picture

Molecular dynamics with an occasional thermostat. Between momentum refreshes the energy is (nearly) constant; each refresh re-thermalises the kinetic energy and moves the particle to a new energy shell. This is Andersen's thermostat, with a full refresh each time.

Cost

One iteration costs LLL gradient evaluations. The fair comparison between samplers is error per gradient evaluation, not per iteration.

Two sources of efficiency: long trajectories (large LεL\varepsilonLε) move far, and near-conservation of HHH keeps acceptance near 1 even for proposals far away. A random walk cannot have both.

Tuning

Three knobs: ε, L and M

Step size ε

Set by the narrowest direction: stability needs ε<2ℓ\varepsilon<2\ellε<2ℓ. Too large: ΔH\Delta HΔH grows and proposals are rejected. The optimal acceptance rate is about 0.65 (random walk: 0.234).

Path length Lε

Should reach across the widest direction: Lε∼LwidthL\varepsilon\sim L_{\rm width}Lε∼Lwidth​, so L∼Lwidth/ℓL\sim L_{\rm width}/\ellL∼Lwidth​/ℓ steps. That is linear in the aspect ratio, against quadratic for the random walk.

Mass matrix M

Choosing M−1≈Cov⁡π(θ)M^{-1}\approx\operatorname{Cov}_\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)M−1≈Covπ​(θ) makes the posterior look round to the dynamics: it is preconditioning. A good MMM removes the ridge problem entirely.

The reason: HMC's energy error per trajectory scales as d ε4d\,\varepsilon^4dε4, so ε\varepsilonε need only shrink as d−1/4d^{-1/4}d−1/4, while a random walk's step must shrink as d−1/2d^{-1/2}d−1/2.

In practice (Stan, NumPyro) ε is tuned automatically during warm-up by "dual averaging" to hit a target acceptance of about 0.8, and a diagonal or dense MMM is estimated from the warm-up samples.

A trap worth knowing

Perfect acceptance, zero progress

For a Gaussian target N(μ,σ2)\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)N(μ,σ2) the dynamics is a harmonic oscillator of period 2πσ2\pi\sigma2πσ. The exact solution is

θ(t)−μ=(θ0−μ)cos⁡tσ+σp0sin⁡tσ.\theta(t)-\mu=(\theta_0-\mu)\cos\frac{t}{\sigma}+\sigma p_0\sin\frac{t}{\sigma}.θ(t)−μ=(θ0​−μ)cosσt​+σp0​sinσt​.

If the path length happens to be half a period, Lε≈πσL\varepsilon\approx\pi\sigmaLε≈πσ, every trajectory maps

θ  ⟼  2μ−θ(whatever p0 is).\theta\;\longmapsto\;2\mu-\theta\quad(\text{whatever }p_0\text{ is}).θ⟼2μ−θ(whatever p0​ is).

The chain flips between two points forever. Energy is conserved, so the acceptance rate is 100%, and every diagnostic that looks at acceptance says all is well.

What actually happened in the study

HMC on the one-dimensional target first ran with a fixed number of leapfrog steps that put the path near half the oscillation period. The chain did exactly this, with 100% acceptance throughout.

The fix

Jitter the number of steps (or ε) randomly from iteration to iteration. No single path length can then resonate. NUTS avoids the problem by choosing the length adaptively.

Physics picture

Stroboscopic sampling of a periodic orbit: if you look at a pendulum once per half-swing, it appears to jump between two points and you learn nothing about its motion.

This is a good example of a failure that no convergence statistic based on a single chain will detect; only knowing the answer (or running chains with different settings) reveals it.

Automatic path length

NUTS: stop when the particle turns back

The No-U-Turn Sampler (Hoffman & Gelman, 2014) removes LLL as a tuning parameter.

  1. Integrate forwards or backwards in time, doubling the trajectory each round: 1, 2, 4, 8, … steps.
  2. Stop as soon as the two ends start moving towards each other, the U-turn: (θ+−θ−)⋅p−<0or(θ+−θ−)⋅p+<0.(\boldsymbol\theta^+-\boldsymbol\theta^-)\cdot\boldsymbol p^-<0\quad\text{or}\quad(\boldsymbol\theta^+-\boldsymbol\theta^-)\cdot\boldsymbol p^+<0 .(θ+−θ−)⋅p−<0or(θ+−θ−)⋅p+<0.
  3. Pick the next state from the points on the trajectory, in a way that keeps detailed balance (slice sampling in the original paper).
In practice

NUTS is the default in Stan, PyMC and NumPyro (used in the satellite-RFI analysis). Step size ε and the mass matrix MMM are tuned during warm-up.

Physics picture

Run the particle for roughly half an orbit, as far as it can go before the dynamics starts to undo itself. That is the longest useful trajectory, and the U-turn criterion finds it without knowing the orbit's period.

Implementation note

The slice level u∼U(0,e−H)u\sim\mathcal U(0,e^{-H})u∼U(0,e−H) underflows in floating point for large HHH. It must be carried in logs: log⁡u=−H+log⁡U(0,1)\log u=-H+\log\mathcal U(0,1)logu=−H+logU(0,1).

The cost of NUTS per iteration varies: it is the trajectory length it chose. In the ridge study it averaged about 30 gradient evaluations per iteration, against about 90 for our fixed-length HMC.

At a matched budget, HMC's error in the posterior mean is about an order of magnitude below the other two. This is the case the method was built for.

Gibbs sampling is left out on purpose: its axis-aligned moves would crawl at exactly the random walk's rate.

The random walk still gets the right answer eventually. The point is the rate: its error falls as the square root of the number of independent samples, and it only gets one every ~400 steps.

Why

Between the modes the gradient points back towards the nearest mode. A particle with typical kinetic energy (∼d/2\sim d/2∼d/2) cannot climb a barrier of many units of UUU. HMC never left its first mode and reported a converged, 60%-wrong answer. NUTS crossed once, by luck, and spent 63% of its samples in a mode whose true weight is 0.20.

Gibbs wins only because this mixture is axis-aligned, which makes its conditionals exact: it is the best case for Gibbs, not a general result. Nested sampling found all four modes without being told the structure.

Lessons

No method wins twice

Use HMC / NUTS when…

  • the posterior is smooth and connected (one main mode);
  • it is strongly correlated, or high-dimensional (d≳10d\gtrsim10d≳10);
  • gradients are cheap, e.g. automatic differentiation in JAX or PyTorch.

Be careful when…

  • there are separated modes: add tempering, many dispersed chains, or use nested sampling;
  • the posterior has sharp edges or discrete parameters (no gradient);
  • the curvature varies wildly (funnels): ε cannot suit both ends.
Three habits
  1. Compare samplers per gradient evaluation, never per iteration.
  2. Test on a problem with a known answer first. Diagnostics cannot see a mode you never visited.
  3. Run several chains from dispersed starts. If they disagree, believe the disagreement.

The single most important message: good-looking chains are not evidence of a correct answer when the posterior may be multimodal.
