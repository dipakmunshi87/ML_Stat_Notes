Random-walk Metropolis

Propose a small random move, accept it if the density goes up, and accept it with a fair chance if it goes down. Twelve lines of code, exact in the limit, and the yardstick every other sampler is measured against.

This deck is the foundation for the other four. Everything later is an attempt to avoid the random walk's diffusive behaviour, either with gradients (HMC, NUTS), with exact conditionals (Gibbs), or by changing the problem into a sequence of constrained ones (nested sampling).

The algorithm

Propose, then accept or reject

We want samples from π(θ)∝e−U(θ)\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)\propto e^{-U(\boldsymbol\theta)}π(θ)∝e−U(θ), known only up to its normalisation. Starting from θ\boldsymbol\thetaθ, repeat:

  1. Propose θ′=θ+σ z\boldsymbol\theta'=\boldsymbol\theta+\sigma\,\boldsymbol zθ′=θ+σz, with z∼N(0,I)\boldsymbol z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)z∼N(0,I).
  2. Accept with probability α=min⁡ ⁣(1,  π(θ′)π(θ))=min⁡ ⁣(1, e−[U(θ′)−U(θ)]),\alpha=\min\!\Big(1,\;\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol\theta')}{\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)}\Big)=\min\!\big(1,\,e^{-[U(\boldsymbol\theta')-U(\boldsymbol\theta)]}\big),α=min(1,π(θ)π(θ′)​)=min(1,e−[U(θ′)−U(θ)]), otherwise stay where you are and record the same point again.

Uphill moves are always accepted; downhill moves are accepted with a probability that falls off exponentially in how much worse they are.

Why no normalisation is needed

Only the ratio π(θ′)/π(θ)\pi(\boldsymbol\theta')/\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)π(θ′)/π(θ) appears, so the unknown constant Z=∫e−UZ=\int e^{-U}Z=∫e−U cancels. This is what makes MCMC possible at all.

Physics picture

This is the Metropolis Monte Carlo of statistical mechanics, at temperature kBT=1k_BT=1kB​T=1: the same rule used to sample spin configurations of the Ising model. It came from Los Alamos in 1953.

A common mistake

A rejected move is not a wasted iteration to be skipped: the current point must be recorded again. Dropping repeats biases the answer towards the tails.

Hastings (1970) generalises this to asymmetric proposals q(θ′∣θ)q(\theta'\mid\theta)q(θ′∣θ), where the ratio gains the factor q(θ∣θ′)/q(θ′∣θ)q(\theta\mid\theta')/q(\theta'\mid\theta)q(θ∣θ′)/q(θ′∣θ). With a symmetric Gaussian proposal that factor is 1.

Why it is exact

Detailed balance in three lines

Write the transition density as T(θ→θ′)=q(θ′∣θ) α(θ,θ′)T(\boldsymbol\theta\to\boldsymbol\theta')=q(\boldsymbol\theta'\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\,\alpha(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\theta')T(θ→θ′)=q(θ′∣θ)α(θ,θ′) for θ′≠θ\boldsymbol\theta'\ne\boldsymbol\thetaθ′=θ, with a symmetric proposal qqq. Then

which is symmetric under θ↔θ′\boldsymbol\theta\leftrightarrow\boldsymbol\theta'θ↔θ′. Hence π(θ)T(θ→θ′)=π(θ′)T(θ′→θ)\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)T(\boldsymbol\theta\to\boldsymbol\theta')=\pi(\boldsymbol\theta')T(\boldsymbol\theta'\to\boldsymbol\theta)π(θ)T(θ→θ′)=π(θ′)T(θ′→θ): detailed balance. Integrating over θ\boldsymbol\thetaθ gives ∫π(θ)T(θ→θ′) dθ=π(θ′)\int\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)T(\boldsymbol\theta\to\boldsymbol\theta')\,d\boldsymbol\theta=\pi(\boldsymbol\theta')∫π(θ)T(θ→θ′)dθ=π(θ′), so π\piπ is a stationary distribution of the chain.

Physics picture

Detailed balance is microscopic reversibility: in equilibrium, the flow of probability from state θ\boldsymbol\thetaθ to θ′\boldsymbol\theta'θ′ is exactly balanced by the reverse flow. It is sufficient for equilibrium, though not necessary.

What is still needed

Stationarity alone is not enough. The chain must also be able to reach anywhere with positive probability (irreducibility) and not cycle deterministically (aperiodicity). Then the distribution converges to π\piπ from any start, and time averages converge to expectations.

Note what this guarantee does not say: nothing about how long convergence takes. All the practical difficulty of MCMC lives in that gap.

Measuring the cost

Correlated samples: the effective sample size

Consecutive states of a chain are correlated, so NNN samples are worth fewer than NNN independent ones. With autocorrelations rkr_krk​ of the quantity of interest,

Error bars scale as 1/Neff1/\sqrt{N_{\rm eff}}1/Neff​​, so the honest measure of a sampler is effective samples per unit of computer time, not samples per second.

Statistics vocabulary

Burn-in: the early part of the chain, before it has forgotten where it started, which is discarded. Thinning: keeping every kkk-th sample. It saves memory but always loses information; it is not a cure for correlation.

Diagnostics

Run several chains from dispersed starts and compare their spread with the spread within each (R^\hat RR^, Gelman & Rubin). R^≈1\hat R\approx1R^≈1 is necessary, not sufficient: it cannot see a mode no chain ever visited.

The interactive slide reports the crude version of this, (1−r)/(1+r)(1-r)/(1+r)(1−r)/(1+r), using only the lag-1 correlation. Proper estimates sum many lags, with a cut-off, as in the Geyer or Sokal estimators.

Tuning and scaling

Why it struggles in high dimension

Two competing effects set the step size σ\sigmaσ:

  • Too small: almost every move is accepted, but each is tiny; the chain diffuses slowly.
  • Too large: proposals land where the density is low and are rejected; the chain sticks.

For a ddd-dimensional Gaussian target the optimum is

σ∝d−1/2,acceptance rate  →  0.234,\sigma\propto d^{-1/2},\qquad \text{acceptance rate}\;\to\;\boxed{0.234},σ∝d−1/2,acceptance rate→0.234​,

(Roberts, Gelman & Gilks 1997). Each independent sample then costs O(d)\mathcal O(d)O(d) iterations, and each iteration costs O(d)\mathcal O(d)O(d) work: O(d2)\mathcal O(d^2)O(d2) in total, against O(d5/4)\mathcal O(d^{5/4})O(d5/4) for HMC.

Anisotropy is worse than dimension

If the target's widest and narrowest directions differ by a factor L/ℓL/\ellL/ℓ, the step must match ℓ\ellℓ while the chain has to cross LLL: that costs (L/ℓ)2(L/\ell)^2(L/ℓ)2 steps per independent sample. For the 20:1 ridge in the test problems, 400.

What to do about it
  • Precondition: propose with a covariance matched to the posterior's, if you can estimate it.
  • Adapt σ\sigmaσ during warm-up towards the 0.234 target (then stop adapting, or use a vanishing schedule, to keep the chain valid).
  • Reparameterise so the posterior is closer to round.
  • Use gradients: MALA, HMC, NUTS.

0.234 is an asymptotic result for product-form targets, but it is a good rule of thumb from about d=5d=5d=5 upwards. For one or two dimensions the optimum is nearer 0.4–0.5.

The family tree

Relatives worth knowing

Metropolis–Hastings

Any proposal q(θ′∣θ)q(\boldsymbol\theta'\mid\boldsymbol\theta)q(θ′∣θ), with acceptance min⁡(1,π(θ′)q(θ∣θ′)π(θ)q(θ′∣θ))\min\big(1,\frac{\pi(\boldsymbol\theta')q(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\boldsymbol\theta')}{\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)q(\boldsymbol\theta'\mid\boldsymbol\theta)}\big)min(1,π(θ)q(θ′∣θ)π(θ′)q(θ∣θ′)​). Every sampler in this collection is a special case or a composition of such steps.

MALA

Metropolis-adjusted Langevin: propose θ′=θ+ε22∇log⁡π+εz\boldsymbol\theta'=\boldsymbol\theta+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}\nabla\log\pi+\varepsilon\boldsymbol zθ′=θ+2ε2​∇logπ+εz, then accept. One gradient per step; O(d4/3)\mathcal O(d^{4/3})O(d4/3); it is HMC with a single leapfrog step.

Slice sampling

Draw a height u∼U(0,π(θ))u\sim\mathcal U(0,\pi(\boldsymbol\theta))u∼U(0,π(θ)), then a new point uniformly from the slice {θ:π(θ)>u}\{\boldsymbol\theta:\pi(\boldsymbol\theta)>u\}{θ:π(θ)>u}. No step size to tune, and it is the engine inside PolyChord-style nested sampling.

Parallel tempering

Run chains at several "temperatures" π1/T\pi^{1/T}π1/T and swap between them. The hot chains cross barriers, the cold one samples the target: the standard cure for the multimodality that defeats HMC.

Slice sampling and tempering are the two ideas most worth adding to a random-walk toolkit; both keep the "no gradients needed" property.

The surprise

On the problem with four separated modes the humble random walk beat both HMC and NUTS: its proposals are blind to the gradient, so occasionally one lands across a barrier. Gradients, by contrast, point firmly back into the mode you are already in.

Cost per iteration: 1 density evaluation, against ~30 for HMC and ~12 for NUTS on this target.

The lesson is not that the random walk is good, but that "state of the art" is problem-dependent. On the ridge it needed ninety times more iterations than HMC for a worse answer.

Summary

When to reach for it

Good when…

  • the dimension is modest (say d≲10d\lesssim10d≲10);
  • gradients are unavailable, expensive, or the model has discrete parameters;
  • you need something implemented and checked in an afternoon;
  • the posterior may be multimodal and the modes are not too far apart.

Bad when…

  • the posterior is strongly correlated and you cannot precondition;
  • the dimension is large: the O(d2)\mathcal O(d^2)O(d2) cost bites;
  • you need the evidence ZZZ (use nested sampling);
  • each density evaluation is expensive, so wasted rejections hurt.

Next: Gibbs sampling, which never rejects anything, or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, which uses gradients to move much further per step.

A good default workflow: start with a random walk to get a feel for the posterior, then move to NUTS for production runs, and keep a tempered or nested run as a check on multimodality.
