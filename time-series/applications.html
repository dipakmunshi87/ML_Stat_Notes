Astronomy · 1 and 2

Stars as resonators, and planets as dips

① Asteroseismology

Data: a star's brightness, sampled every 1–30 minutes for months or years (Kepler, TESS, PLATO).

Physics: convection stochastically excites and damps the star's acoustic modes. A damped, randomly driven oscillator has a Lorentzian line in the power spectrum, whose width is the damping rate and whose area is the excitation power — exactly the AR(2) picture of page 3.

Measured: the frequency of maximum power νmax⁡\nu_{\max}νmax​ and the large separation Δν\Delta\nuΔν give the star's mass and radius; individual mode frequencies probe the interior; the granulation background is fitted as a sum of Lorentzians (Harvey profiles).

Technique: power spectrum of a long, nearly uniform series; fitting Lorentzians plus background by maximum likelihood with the correct χ22\chi^2_2χ22​ statistics.

② Exoplanet transits and stellar noise

Data: the same photometry, now searching for a dip of a few hundred parts per million.

Problem: the star itself varies — spots, granulation, oscillations — producing correlated noise on the same time scale as the transit. Treating it as white noise biases the fitted depth and badly underestimates its error.

Technique: fit the transit shape and a Gaussian process noise model simultaneously, with a kernel chosen for the stellar variability. Fast kernels make the likelihood O(N)\mathcal O(N)O(N) instead of O(N3)\mathcal O(N^3)O(N3), which is what makes this routine for million-point light curves.

Payoff: planet radii and their honest uncertainties — and the same machinery handles the radial-velocity case, where stellar activity mimics a planet.

Both examples show the same lesson from a different side: in one the "noise" is the signal (granulation, oscillations), in the other it is a nuisance that must still be modelled rather than ignored.

Astronomy · 3

Accreting compact objects: timing without images

Data: X-ray photon arrival times from a black-hole or neutron-star binary, binned into a light curve at millisecond resolution.

Physics: the inner accretion flow cannot be imaged, so its structure is inferred from the statistics of its variability. Broad Lorentzian components mark characteristic time scales; narrow ones are quasi-periodic oscillations; the high-frequency cut-off tracks the inner radius.

Techniques, all from the earlier pages:

  • power spectra with Poisson noise subtracted (the white floor is 2/count rate2/\text{count rate}2/count rate in the standard normalisation);
  • cross-spectra between energy bands, giving the coherence and the phase lag as functions of frequency — the light-travel delay between the corona and the reflecting disc;
  • the rms–flux relation: variability amplitude proportional to flux, the signature of a multiplicative rather than additive process.
Why the statistics matter here

A single periodogram ordinate is exponentially distributed, so a peak three times the local mean happens by chance in about 5% of bins. With 10410^4104 bins searched, dozens of such peaks appear in pure noise. Claims of QPOs stand or fall on the trials factor and on the red-noise continuum used as the null — see the errors page.

Physics picture

This is response-function measurement: the corona's variability drives the disc, and the cross-spectrum is the transfer function. Reverberation mapping of active galaxies is the same idea at longer wavelengths and longer time scales.

Stingray implements most of this: power spectra, cross spectra, coherence, lags and bispectra, with the correct statistics.

Instruments and the Earth · 4, 5 and 6

Drifts, ambient noise and clocks

④ CMB detector drifts

Data: time-ordered detector samples as the telescope scans the sky.

Problem: the detectors have strong 1/f1/f1/f drift, so a naive map has stripes along the scan direction.

Technique: model the drift as an offset per short baseline and solve for the map and the offsets together (destriping), or apply the full noise-weighted map-making equation with N−1N^{-1}N−1 built from the measured noise spectrum. Both are least squares with a correlated noise covariance — a time-series problem that determines the final cosmology.

⑤ Ambient-noise seismology

Data: continuous ground motion at two stations, dominated by oceanic microseisms: pure noise, no earthquakes needed.

Physics: the cross-correlation of a diffuse random field between two receivers converges to the Green's function between them. Noise becomes a seismic source.

Technique: long cross-correlations, stacked over weeks; group-velocity dispersion measured from the result; repeated to monitor velocity changes of a few parts in 10410^4104 before eruptions or after earthquakes.

⑥ Atomic clocks

Data: fractional frequency of an oscillator against a reference.

Problem: for 1/f1/f1/f and random-walk noise the ordinary variance diverges as the record lengthens, so the usual standard deviation is meaningless.

Technique: the Allan variance, the variance of differences of adjacent averages over time τ\tauτ. Its slope identifies the noise type — τ−1\tau^{-1}τ−1 white, flat flicker, τ+1\tau^{+1}τ+1 random walk — and its minimum gives the optimal averaging time.

Laboratory and plasma · 7 and 8

Noise as spectroscopy

⑦ 1/f and telegraph noise in devices

Data: the fluctuating output of a SQUID, a transistor, or the decay of a superconducting qubit repeated many times.

Physics: individual two-level fluctuators produce random telegraph noise — a two-state switching signal whose spectrum is a Lorentzian with corner at the switching rate. A broad distribution of rates, P(γ)∝1/γP(\gamma)\propto1/\gammaP(γ)∝1/γ, sums to a 1/f1/f1/f spectrum. Measuring the spectrum therefore measures the distribution of microscopic defects.

Technique: long records, Welch spectra over many decades in frequency, hidden-Markov fits for individual fluctuators, and noise spectroscopy by dynamical decoupling — a pulse sequence acts as a narrow filter in frequency, so the qubit's decay measures S(f)S(f)S(f) point by point.

⑧ Turbulence in the solar wind

Data: magnetic-field and velocity components measured in situ by a spacecraft, at up to hundreds of samples per second.

Physics: a spectrum with an inertial range close to f−5/3f^{-5/3}f−5/3, a steeper dissipation range beyond an ion-scale break, and intermittency — rare, large jumps that make the statistics non-Gaussian.

Technique: power spectra with careful windowing, structure functions Sp(ℓ)=⟨∣δv(ℓ)∣p⟩S_p(\ell)=\langle|\delta v(\ell)|^p\rangleSp​(ℓ)=⟨∣δv(ℓ)∣p⟩ and their scaling exponents, wavelet analysis for intermittency, and Taylor's hypothesis to convert time lags into spatial scales. Higher-order structure functions are the standard diagnostic of departures from Gaussianity.

Both examples are cases where the spectrum is a measurement of microphysics: defect distributions in one, the turbulent cascade in the other.
