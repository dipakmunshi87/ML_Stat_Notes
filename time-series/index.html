What a time series is

A time series is a sequence of measurements x(t1),x(t2),…,x(tN)x(t_1),x(t_2),\dots,x(t_N)x(t1​),x(t2​),…,x(tN​), usually equally spaced by Δt\Delta tΔt, regarded as one realisation of a random process. The defining feature is that the order carries information: shuffling independent measurements loses nothing, while shuffling a time series destroys the very structure you wanted to measure.

Most series are usefully split into three parts,

x(t)=m(t)⏟trend+s(t)⏟periodic+η(t)⏟stochastic,x(t)=\underbrace{m(t)}_{\text{trend}}+\underbrace{s(t)}_{\text{periodic}}+\underbrace{\eta(t)}_{\text{stochastic}},x(t)=trendm(t)​​+periodics(t)​​+stochasticη(t)​​,

and almost all the technique is about describing the third part honestly: how strongly a value is related to the one before, how that dependence decays, and what it does to your error bars.

γ(τ)=Cov⁡(x(t),x(t+τ)),S(f)=∫γ(τ)e−2πifτdτ,Var⁡(x)=∫S(f) df.\gamma(\tau)=\operatorname{Cov}\big(x(t),x(t+\tau)\big),\qquad S(f)=\int\gamma(\tau)e^{-2\pi i f\tau}d\tau,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(x)=\int S(f)\,df .γ(τ)=Cov(x(t),x(t+τ)),S(f)=∫γ(τ)e−2πifτdτ,Var(x)=∫S(f)df.

Those two functions — the autocorrelation and the power spectrum — are the same information in two forms, and between them they describe every stationary series.

Physics picture

γ(τ)\gamma(\tau)γ(τ) is a two-point correlation function and S(f)S(f)S(f) a power spectrum; stationarity is equilibrium; ergodicity is Boltzmann's hypothesis; white noise is a Langevin force; an AR(1) process is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. The subject is largely statistical mechanics with different labels.

The one number that matters most

The correlation time τc\tau_cτc​. It sets how many independent measurements your record really contains, Neff≈T/(2τc)N_{\rm eff}\approx T/(2\tau_c)Neff​≈T/(2τc​). Using NNN instead of NeffN_{\rm eff}Neff​ in an error bar is the most common mistake in the field, and it always makes results look better than they are.

Error bars: the three facts to remember

Correlations inflate errors

Var⁡(xˉ)=σ2N τ,τ=1+2∑k≥1ρ(k).\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\,\tau,\qquad \tau=1+2\sum_{k\ge1}\rho(k).Var(xˉ)=Nσ2​τ,τ=1+2k≥1∑​ρ(k).

For AR(1) with φ=0.95\varphi=0.95φ=0.95 the honest error bar is six times the naive one.

A raw spectrum has 100% error

Each periodogram ordinate is exponentially distributed about the truth, whatever the record length. Only averaging KKK segments or tapers reduces it, as 1/K1/\sqrt K1/K​, and it costs resolution.

Count your trials

Searching MMM frequencies, the largest peak exceeds zzz times the mean with probability ≈Me−z\approx Me^{-z}≈Me−z. A "3σ3\sigma3σ" peak among 10410^4104 bins is ordinary. And judge it against red noise, not white.

Five habits worth having

Do

  1. Plot the series first, and the residuals last.
  2. Measure the background spectrum, and test significance against it, not against white noise.
  3. Quote effective sample sizes.
  4. Say what your cadence and record length can and cannot probe.
  5. Simulate from the fitted model and check the simulation looks like the data.

Don't

  1. Interpolate over gaps and then quote a significance.
  2. Treat a fitted φ\varphiφ as physical without saying what Δt\Delta tΔt was.
  3. Claim a period without accounting for the number of frequencies searched.
  4. Smooth the data and then use NNN in the error bars.
  5. Difference a series out of habit: it amplifies high-frequency noise.
