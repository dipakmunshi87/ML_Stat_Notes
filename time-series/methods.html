Estimating from data

Three routes from data to parameters

① Moments

Estimate the autocovariance directly:

γ^(k)=1N∑t=1N−k(xt−xˉ)(xt+k−xˉ).\hat\gamma(k)=\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^{N-k}(x_t-\bar x)(x_{t+k}-\bar x).γ^​(k)=N1​t=1∑N−k​(xt​−xˉ)(xt+k​−xˉ).

Dividing by NNN rather than N−kN-kN−k is biased low but has much smaller variance and guarantees a positive spectrum — the usual choice. For an AR(ppp) model, the Yule–Walker equations then give the coefficients from a small linear system:

γ(k)=∑j=1pφj γ(k−j),k=1..p.\gamma(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\varphi_j\,\gamma(k-j),\quad k=1..p .γ(k)=j=1∑p​φj​γ(k−j),k=1..p.

② Likelihood

For Gaussian data the likelihood is

with Σtt′=γ(t−t′)\Sigma_{tt'}=\gamma(t-t')Σtt′​=γ(t−t′). Naively O(N3)\mathcal O(N^3)O(N3); in practice it is computed in O(N)\mathcal O(N)O(N) by running a Kalman filter and summing the one-step prediction errors, or approximated in the frequency domain by the Whittle likelihood.

③ Regression

An AR model is just linear regression of xtx_txt​ on its own lags, so ordinary least squares works and gives almost the same answer as ①. It is the quickest route, and the easiest to get wrong if the series has a trend or a unit root.

The Kalman filter in one line

Predict the hidden state and its covariance forward with the dynamics, then correct with the measurement, weighted by how much you trust each: z^←z^+K(y−Hz^)\hat{\boldsymbol z}\leftarrow\hat{\boldsymbol z}+K(y-H\hat{\boldsymbol z})z^←z^+K(y−Hz^) with the Kalman gain KKK balancing the two covariances. It is exact recursive Bayesian inference for linear Gaussian models, and the likelihood falls out of the innovations as a by-product.

Whittle's approximation treats Fourier coefficients as independent, which is exact only in the limit of a long record; it is fast and widely used for 1/f-type models.

The periodogram, and how to stop it lying

With the amplitude at zero, look at how noisy a raw periodogram is: each point has 100% fractional error however long the record, because more data buys more frequencies, not better ones. Welch averaging of K segments cuts that error as 1/√K.

Spectra done properly

Four rules for spectral estimation

  1. Remove the mean and any trend first. A constant offset puts a huge spike at f=0f=0f=0 whose leakage contaminates everything.
  2. Taper (Hann, Hamming, or DPSS for the best control). Without a taper, a strong line leaks power across the whole band with sidelobes falling only as 1/f21/f^21/f2.
  3. Average: split into KKK overlapping segments and average their periodograms (Welch), or use multitaper. The fractional error falls as 1/K1/\sqrt K1/K​ while the resolution worsens by KKK.
  4. Know the statistics: a raw periodogram ordinate is χ22\chi^2_2χ22​-distributed about the true spectrum, so it is exponential — 100% fractional error, and occasional values three times the mean that mean nothing at all.
Uneven sampling

Gaps and irregular cadence break the FFT. Options, in increasing order of rigour:

  • Lomb–Scargle: least-squares fitting of sinusoids at each trial frequency; the standard tool in astronomy. Its window function produces alias peaks — always plot it.
  • Gaussian process or state-space fit: handles gaps natively and gives proper uncertainties.
  • Interpolate and FFT: quick, but it invents correlations and biases the spectrum. Use with care, and never for significance tests.
Checking a claimed period

Compare against the red-noise background, account for the number of frequencies searched, and split the record in two: a real coherent signal keeps its phase; a red-noise peak usually does not.

The 1-day and 1-year aliases in ground-based astronomy are so standard that any claimed period near them deserves special scepticism.

Filtering

Whitening, matched filtering and residuals

If the noise has spectrum S(f)S(f)S(f) and you are looking for a known shape h(t)h(t)h(t), the optimal linear statistic is the matched filter:

ρ=⟨d,h⟩⟨h,h⟩,⟨a,b⟩=4 Re∫0∞a~(f) b~∗(f)S(f) df.\rho=\frac{\langle d,h\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle h,h\rangle}},\qquad \langle a,b\rangle=4\,\mathrm{Re}\int_0^\infty\frac{\tilde a(f)\,\tilde b^*(f)}{S(f)}\,df .ρ=⟨h,h⟩​⟨d,h⟩​,⟨a,b⟩=4Re∫0∞​S(f)a~(f)b~∗(f)​df.

Each frequency is weighted by how quiet the noise is there: the data are whitened, then correlated with the template. This is the Wiener filter, and it is what gravitational-wave searches, radar and pulsar searches all do.

The same idea in reverse is the standard test of a fitted model: pass the data through the inverse of the fitted noise model and check that what comes out is white.

residual ε^t=xt−x^t∣t−1⇒should have ρ(k)≈0,  ∣ρ(k)∣≲2/N.\text{residual}\ \hat\varepsilon_t=x_t-\hat x_{t\mid t-1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{should have }\rho(k)\approx0,\ \ |\rho(k)|\lesssim 2/\sqrt N .residual ε^t​=xt​−x^t∣t−1​⇒should have ρ(k)≈0,  ∣ρ(k)∣≲2/N​.
Common filters
  • Moving average / Savitzky–Golay: smooth, keep peak shapes; simple but with sidelobes.
  • Butterworth (IIR): flat pass-band; apply forwards and backwards to avoid phase shift.
  • Notch: kill a known interference line; wide enough to catch drift, narrow enough to keep signal.
  • Differencing: removes trends and unit roots, amplifies high-frequency noise.
Filtering changes your statistics

Any smoothing introduces correlations, so the effective number of independent points drops. Fitting a model to smoothed data and then using NNN in the error bars is a common and serious mistake.

Tests worth running

Ljung–Box on the residuals (are any lags correlated?), Kolmogorov–Smirnov on the whitened amplitudes (Gaussian?), and a comparison of the residual spectrum with white noise.

The matched filter is optimal only if the template and the noise spectrum are both right. In practice, most of the work in a detection pipeline goes into estimating S(f)S(f)S(f) and into vetoing the non-Gaussian transients that break the assumption.

In code

The same steps, in practice

A minimal, honest workflow

x = x - x.mean() # remove offset
x = detrend(x) # remove trend if physical
f, S = welch(x, fs, nperseg=N//8) # spectrum, averaged
phi = yule_walker(x, order=1) # or statsmodels ARIMA
r = residuals(model, x) # one-step prediction errors
acorr(r) # must look white
ljung_box(r, lags=20) # and pass the test

In Python: numpy, scipy.signal (welch, butter, filtfilt), statsmodels.tsa (ARIMA, Yule–Walker, Ljung–Box), astropy.timeseries (Lomb–Scargle), celerite2 or george (fast Gaussian processes), filterpy or a dozen lines of numpy (Kalman).

Five habits
  1. Plot the series before anything else, then plot the residuals after everything else.
  2. Simulate data from your fitted model and check it looks like the real thing.
  3. Quote effective sample sizes, not raw NNN.
  4. Test significance against the measured background, not against white noise.
  5. Keep the physics: if you know the system relaxes, fit a relaxation time, not an opaque high-order model.
Simulate, then fit

The cheapest way to know whether a pipeline works is to inject a known signal into real noise and see whether it comes out with the right amplitude and error bar. Every detection pipeline in physics does this, and every analysis benefits from it.
