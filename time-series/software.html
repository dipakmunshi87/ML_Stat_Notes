Reading

Textbooks and the papers behind the methods

Books

  • D. B. Percival, A. T. Walden, Spectral Analysis for Physical Applications, CUP (1993). The reference for tapers, multitaper and the statistics of spectral estimates.
  • G. E. P. Box, G. M. Jenkins, G. C. Reinsel, Time Series Analysis: Forecasting and Control. The origin of the ARIMA machinery.
  • C. Chatfield, H. Xing, The Analysis of Time Series: An Introduction with R. The gentlest complete introduction.
  • R. H. Shumway, D. S. Stoffer, Time Series Analysis and Its Applications. Strong on state-space models and the Kalman filter.
  • C. E. Rasmussen, C. K. I. Williams, Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning, MIT Press (2006); free online.
  • Ž. Ivezić, A. Connolly, J. VanderPlas, A. Gray, Statistics, Data Mining and Machine Learning in Astronomy, Princeton (2020). Chapter 10 is time series, with code.
  • W. J. Riley, Handbook of Frequency Stability Analysis, NIST SP 1065 (2008); free from NIST.

Papers worth having read

  • P. D. Welch, The use of FFT for the estimation of power spectra, IEEE Trans. AU 15, 70 (1967).
  • D. J. Thomson, Spectrum estimation and harmonic analysis, Proc. IEEE 70, 1055 (1982) — multitaper.
  • N. R. Lomb (1976) and J. D. Scargle (1982), and J. VanderPlas, Understanding the Lomb–Scargle periodogram, ApJS 236, 16 (2018); arXiv:1703.09824.
  • M. E. Mann, J. Lees, Robust estimation of background noise and signal detection in climatic time series, Climatic Change 33, 409 (1996) — red-noise significance.
  • S. Vaughan, A simple test for periodic signals in red noise, A&A 431, 391 (2005); and the Bayesian version, MNRAS 402, 307 (2010).
  • J. Theiler et al., Testing for nonlinearity in time series: the method of surrogate data, Physica D 58, 77 (1992).
  • D. Foreman-Mackey et al., Fast and scalable Gaussian process modeling with applications to astronomical time series, AJ 154, 220 (2017); arXiv:1703.09710.
  • P. Uttley et al., X-ray reverberation and the origin of variability, A&A Rev. 22, 72 (2014) — cross-spectra, coherence and lags in practice.
  • D. W. Allan, Statistics of atomic frequency standards, Proc. IEEE 54, 221 (1966).

If you read only two: Percival & Walden for spectra, and VanderPlas for Lomb–Scargle and the practicalities of uneven sampling.

Getting started

A one-hour self-test

The quickest way to trust a pipeline is to build it against a case you can compute by hand. In an hour, with scipy and statsmodels:

  1. Simulate an AR(1) series with a known φ\varphiφ and variance.
  2. Recover φ\varphiφ by Yule–Walker and by maximum likelihood; check both against the truth and against each other.
  3. Estimate the spectrum with scipy.signal.welch and overlay the analytic Lorentzian: they should agree within the 1/K1/\sqrt K1/K​ scatter.
  4. Check the error bar: repeat 500 times and compare the spread of the recovered φ\varphiφ with the uncertainty the fit reported.
  5. Break it: add a gap, a trend, or an outlier, and see which step notices.
Why step 4 matters most

It is the only step that tests whether your uncertainties are right, and uncertainty is where time-series analysis usually goes wrong. Everything else tests the central value, which is rarely the problem.

And then

Replace the simulated series with your own data, keep the same tests as a regression suite, and you have a pipeline whose failures announce themselves.

This is the same "test against a known answer" principle as the companion MCMC set, where every target has a closed-form solution.
