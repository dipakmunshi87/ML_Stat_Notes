Example 1 · gravitational waves

Matched filtering: a chirp under coloured noise

An interferometer's strain data are a time series with a strongly frequency-dependent noise spectrum S(f)S(f)S(f): seismic noise at low frequency, thermal noise in the middle, shot noise at high frequency. A compact-binary inspiral produces a known waveform, a chirp rising in frequency and amplitude:

h(t)∝(tc−t)−1/4cos⁡[Φ(t)],fgw(t)∝(tc−t)−3/8,h(t)\propto (t_c-t)^{-1/4}\cos\big[\Phi(t)\big],\qquad f_{\rm gw}(t)\propto (t_c-t)^{-3/8},h(t)∝(tc​−t)−1/4cos[Φ(t)],fgw​(t)∝(tc​−t)−3/8,

with the rate set by the chirp mass. The optimal statistic weights each frequency by how quiet the detector is there:

ρ2=4∫0∞∣d~(f)h~∗(f)∣S(f) df/ ⁣ ⁣∫0∞∣h~(f)∣2S(f) df.\rho^2=4\int_0^\infty\frac{|\tilde d(f)\tilde h^*(f)|}{S(f)}\,df\Big/\!\!\int_0^\infty\frac{|\tilde h(f)|^2}{S(f)}\,df .ρ2=4∫0∞​S(f)∣d~(f)h~∗(f)∣​df/∫0∞​S(f)∣h~(f)∣2​df.

In practice: estimate S(f)S(f)S(f) from data around the event (Welch), whiten, correlate with a bank of templates, and look for peaks coincident between detectors.

Every step is from the earlier pages
  • S(f)S(f)S(f) estimated by averaged periodograms — Part 4;
  • whitening = applying the inverse filter — Part 2;
  • matched filter = Wiener filter;
  • significance assessed against the measured background, including non-Gaussian glitches, not against white noise.
What makes it hard

The noise is neither stationary nor Gaussian in detail: transient glitches mimic short signals, which is why vetoes, coincidence between detectors and time-slide background estimation matter as much as the filter itself.

The template bank is large because the waveform depends on masses and spins; the search is a matched filter repeated for every template, which is why it is computationally heavy rather than conceptually hard.

Example 2 · pulsar timing

When the noise is the signal

A millisecond pulsar is a clock. Subtract the best-fit timing model — spin, spin-down, position, proper motion, binary orbit — and what is left are the timing residuals, a time series sampled every few weeks for decades:

r(t)=white⏟radiometer noise+red⏟spin noise, S∝f−α+dispersion⏟interstellar, ∝ν−2+gravitational waves⏟common to all pulsars.r(t)=\underbrace{\text{white}}_{\text{radiometer noise}} +\underbrace{\text{red}}_{\text{spin noise},\ S\propto f^{-\alpha}} +\underbrace{\text{dispersion}}_{\text{interstellar},\ \propto\nu^{-2}} +\underbrace{\text{gravitational waves}}_{\text{common to all pulsars}} .r(t)=radiometer noisewhite​​+spin noise, S∝f−αred​​+interstellar, ∝ν−2dispersion​​+common to all pulsarsgravitational waves​​.

The gravitational-wave background from supermassive black-hole binaries is itself a red process, with a characteristic strain hc∝f−2/3h_c\propto f^{-2/3}hc​∝f−2/3 and hence a steep residual spectrum S∝f−13/3S\propto f^{-13/3}S∝f−13/3. It is distinguished from each pulsar's own red noise only by being correlated between pulsars in a specific angular pattern (the Hellings–Downs curve).

Why this is a time-series problem
  • Unevenly sampled, with gaps: Gaussian-process or state-space likelihoods, not FFTs.
  • The model has to include the red noise, or its power leaks into the fitted spin parameters.
  • Fitting the timing model itself removes power at the lowest frequencies — a known filter that must be accounted for in the analysis.
  • Significance is assessed against the red-noise background, with the number of trials included.
The lesson

Here the "noise" families of Part 3 are the measurement: the whole result is a statement about the amplitude and slope of a red spectrum, and about its correlation between pulsars.

This is the clearest case where treating red noise casually changes the answer: assuming white noise inflates the apparent significance enormously.

Example 3 · quasar light curves

An AR(1) fit that measures a physical time scale

Accreting supermassive black holes vary irregularly. Survey light curves are well described by a damped random walk, which is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process of Part 2:

dLdt=−L−⟨L⟩τ+ξ(t)⟺Ln+1=φLn+εn,  φ=e−Δt/τ.\frac{dL}{dt}=-\frac{L-\langle L\rangle}{\tau}+\xi(t) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L_{n+1}=\varphi L_n+\varepsilon_n,\ \ \varphi=e^{-\Delta t/\tau}.dtdL​=−τL−⟨L⟩​+ξ(t)⟺Ln+1​=φLn​+εn​,  φ=e−Δt/τ.

The structure function — the average squared difference between epochs separated by Δ\DeltaΔ — is

SF(Δ)=2σ2(1−e−Δ/τ)  → Δ≪τ   ∝Δ,\mathrm{SF}(\Delta)=2\sigma^2\big(1-e^{-\Delta/\tau}\big) \;\xrightarrow{\ \Delta\ll\tau\ }\;\propto\Delta ,SF(Δ)=2σ2(1−e−Δ/τ) Δ≪τ ​∝Δ,

rising and then flattening at the correlation time. Fitted across thousands of objects, τ\tauτ and σ\sigmaσ correlate with black-hole mass and luminosity, which is how a statistical model becomes astrophysics.

Two practical traps

Cadence. If the survey samples every few days and τ\tauτ is years, φ≈1\varphi\approx1φ≈1 and the data look like a random walk: τ\tauτ is then unconstrained from above. Quote the range your sampling can actually probe.

Length. Estimating a correlation time needs a record several times longer than τ\tauτ. Fits to short light curves are biased low, a bias that was only recognised after it had been published on widely.

Method in three lines

Fit the OU model by Kalman-filter likelihood (fast, handles gaps and heteroskedastic errors), or equivalently as a Gaussian process with an exponential kernel; check the residuals are white; report τ\tauτ with an honest error bar, including the cadence caveat.

The same model, under the name CARMA, generalises to damped oscillations, which is how quasi-periodic behaviour in accreting systems is tested.

Example 4 · radio astronomy

Satellite interference in time–frequency data

A radio interferometer records a complex visibility for every baseline, frequency channel and time sample: a large stack of time series. A transmitting satellite adds a contribution that is structured in all three axes:

Vpq(t,ν)=Vpqsky(t,ν)⏟slowly varying+A(t,ν) e i 2πν τpq(t)⏟satellite+ npq(t,ν),V_{pq}(t,\nu)=\underbrace{V^{\rm sky}_{pq}(t,\nu)}_{\text{slowly varying}} +\underbrace{A(t,\nu)\,e^{\,i\,2\pi\nu\,\tau_{pq}(t)}}_{\text{satellite}}+\ n_{pq}(t,\nu),Vpq​(t,ν)=slowly varyingVpqsky​(t,ν)​​+satelliteA(t,ν)ei2πντpq​(t)​​+ npq​(t,ν),

where the geometric delay τpq(t)\tau_{pq}(t)τpq​(t) follows the satellite's orbit. The interference is narrow-band, moves through the band, and its fringe rate is far higher than the sky's — three handles for separating it.

Flagging asks only whether a sample is contaminated (a threshold on amplitude, on spectral kurtosis, or on a trained classifier). Subtraction models the interference — its trajectory and amplitude — and removes it, keeping the data underneath.

The time-series content
  • Windowing and leakage: a strong narrow-band transmitter spreads across channels unless the window is chosen well (Part 1).
  • Fringe rate is a frequency: separating sky from satellite is a filtering problem in delay–rate space.
  • The orbit gives a parametric model, so the problem becomes a fit of a few physical parameters rather than of thousands of amplitudes.
  • Residual tests: after subtraction the residuals should be consistent with the thermal-noise spectrum, and that is the criterion to report.
Kalman, again

Tracking the satellite through the data is exactly the state-space problem of Part 3: a state (orbit) with known dynamics, observed through a noisy nonlinear measurement.

This connects to the satellite-RFI pipeline: trajectory-based subtraction is the "model it, don't flag it" branch, and its success criterion is a residual spectrum consistent with thermal noise.

The common pattern
  1. Describe the noise honestly, usually by its spectrum.
  2. Write the signal as a model with few parameters.
  3. Weight the data by the noise (whiten), then fit or filter.
  4. Check the residuals are what the model says they should be.
And the common failure

Assuming white noise when the data are red. It inflates significances, biases fitted parameters and produces periodicities that are not there. If you take one habit from these pages, take this one: measure the background spectrum, and test against it.
