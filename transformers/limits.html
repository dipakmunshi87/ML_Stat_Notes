The picture

Every pair, or only the near ones

This is the same choice you already make in an N-body code. Direct summation is exact and quadratic; a cutoff or a tree is cheap and approximate.

The difference is that here you also get to choose the cutoff on physical grounds — and if your system has a correlation length, that choice is a statement you can defend.

Physicists usually find this slide reassuring: the cost problem and its solutions are ones they have met before under other names.

The cost

What is actually done about it

At T=104T=10^4T=104 — a modest galaxy field or a detector event — the attention matrix has 10810^8108 entries: 400 MB in single precision, for one head, one layer, one example. That is why most physics applications modify the architecture.

  • Restrict the neighbourhood. Attend only within a radius, a patch, or the kkk nearest neighbours. Cost becomes linear, and the restriction is a physical statement about the correlation length — often a better model, not just a cheaper one.
  • Change the kernel. Replace the softmax with a factorised kernel and the sum can be reassociated as Q(K ⁣⊤V)Q(K^{\!\top}V)Q(K⊤V), which is linear in TTT. Genuinely cheaper, generally less accurate.
  • State-space models. Mamba and its relatives: linear in length, with a learned recurrence. Strong where the data really is a sequence; they give up permutation equivariance.
  • Pool first. Cluster constituents, attend over clusters. The oldest idea in the list and frequently the best one.
What FlashAttention is not

An exact attention implementation with better memory traffic: it removes the O(T2)O(T^2)O(T2) memory cost by never materialising AAA, but the O(T2)O(T^2)O(T2) arithmetic is unchanged. Treating it as a complexity fix is a common and expensive misunderstanding.

Choose the restriction physically

If your system has a correlation length, the neighbourhood should be a few of them — a statement you can defend. A window chosen to fit in memory is not.

The practical order

Pool, then restrict, then reach for an efficient-attention variant — in that order. The first two are physics; the third is engineering with an accuracy cost.

The 400 MB figure is worth quoting in talks: it makes the constraint concrete far better than the symbol does.

No guarantees

What the architecture does not promise

Conservation laws

Nothing in a transformer conserves energy, mass, momentum, charge or probability. A learned surrogate will drift, and the drift usually grows with rollout length. Either impose the constraint architecturally, project onto it after each step, or measure and report the violation. Ignoring it is not an option, and the violation is often the most sensitive diagnostic you have.

Extrapolation

Set size extrapolates well — page 3 measured no degradation from 5 tokens to 12. Parameter-range extrapolation does not. Outside the training distribution the model returns a confident number with no indication that it is now guessing, and this is the single most common way a physics application fails in the field.

Symmetry

Only permutation symmetry is exact. Rotations, boosts, gauge invariance, parity: absent unless built in. A model that respects them approximately, learned from augmented data, will violate them at the percent level — which may or may not matter, but must be measured rather than assumed.

Interpretability

Attention weights are not explanations. They show where information could flow, not what was used; a head can attend strongly to something it then discards. The reliable test is intervention — ablate the head, perturb the input, and measure the change in the output.

Data appetite

Weak inductive bias is the price of generality. Where labelled data is scarce and the physics is well understood, a parametric fit or a physics-informed model will usually win — and will come with error bars you can defend.

The conservation point is the one that most often turns a promising rollout into an unpublishable one.

Reading the literature

How an improvement can be an artefact

  1. The baseline was not tuned. A new architecture tuned for months against a baseline tuned for an afternoon will win, and the result says nothing.
  2. The comparison is not compute-matched. More parameters, more training, more augmentation: compare at equal compute or the claim is about budget, not architecture.
  3. The ablation was run at the wrong scale. Page 2 removed the residual connection at no cost — in a one-layer model. At 24 layers it is fatal.
  4. The test set leaks. Correlated runs split at random; the same object in train and test under different noise; preprocessing fitted on everything.
  5. The metric is not the physics. RMSE-optimal forecasts are over-smoothed: they win on the score and lose on the extremes the forecast exists for.
  6. Only the best seed is shown. Small models vary a great deal between seeds; one number with no spread is not a measurement.
The questions to ask

How was the baseline tuned, and by whom? Equal compute? How many seeds, and what spread? How was the split made? What does the metric miss?

These five dispose of most disputed results, in either direction.

Applies here too

Every measurement on this site is a small model on a toy task, run on one or two seeds: an honest illustration of a mechanism, not evidence about what works at scale. The distinction is exactly the one this slide is about.

Point 5 is the one most specific to physics: the metric being optimised is rarely the quantity the science depends on.

When not to

Problems where something else is better

Use a convolution

Regular grid, local physics, translation invariance that you want enforced rather than learned. Images, fields on a lattice, anything where a stencil is the natural operator. Cheaper, more sample-efficient, and it encodes the locality you already know is there.

Use a Gaussian process

Few inputs, smooth output, small data, and uncertainty that has to be calibrated. Exact posterior, principled error bars, and interpretable hyperparameters. For a low-dimensional emulator this is usually the right answer and is too often skipped.

Use the physics

If you know the model, fit its parameters. Page 5's arithmetic applies in reverse: a three-parameter fit with a likelihood beats a flexible network on accuracy, uncertainty and defensibility whenever the model is right.

Use a graph network

When the connectivity is genuinely known — a mesh, a molecule, a detector geometry — a fixed graph is a stronger prior than an all-pairs attention that must rediscover it, and it is linear in edges rather than quadratic in nodes.

Use a classical algorithm

Sorting, matching, clustering, integration. A learned approximation to a solved problem is slower, less accurate and unverifiable. Surprisingly often proposed anyway.

Use an equivariant model

When the symmetry group is central and data is limited — molecular energies, gauge fields. Harder to build, considerably more sample-efficient, and the symmetry is exact rather than approximate.

The honest summary of the set: transformers are the right default for unordered, variable-size, heterogeneous data, and the wrong default for everything else.

The balance

None of these is a reason not to use a transformer on set-structured physics data, where the case remains strong. They are the list of things to measure and report alongside the result — and a paper that reports them is more convincing than one that does not, regardless of the headline number.
