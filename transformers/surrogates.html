Part 5 of 8 · emulation

Paying once, using many times

An emulator replaces an expensive calculation with a cheap learned approximation. Whether that is legitimate is an arithmetic question and a checking question. It is almost never a question about the network.

This is the application with the clearest payoff in physics and the one most often done without adequate validation.

The whole argument

Pay once, use many times

If the ratio M/N is not large, no amount of architectural cleverness rescues the idea.

The arithmetic

When emulation pays

Write CCC for the cost of one simulation and ccc for one call to the network. You need MMM evaluations and it takes NNN simulations to train. Emulation is worth it when

N C+M c  <  M C,i.e.  M≳N when c≪C.N\,C+M\,c\;<\;M\,C,\qquad\text{i.e. }\ M\gtrsim N \text{ when } c\ll C .NC+Mc<MC,i.e.  M≳N when c≪C.

So the question is never "is the network accurate enough" on its own. It is whether MMM is big enough to repay NNN.

Where it pays decisively: fitting a model to data. An MCMC or nested-sampling run needs 10510^5105 to 10710^7107 likelihood calls, and a few thousand well-chosen simulations train the emulator. A ratio of a thousand or more.

Where it does not: when you need a handful of high-fidelity runs, when the parameter space is wide and thinly sampled, or when every new study moves the region you would have to cover.

The worked case

CosmoPower (Spurio Mancini et al., MNRAS 511, 1771 (2022)) emulates CMB and matter power spectra in place of a Boltzmann solver: emulation error below 0.4% on the matter power spectrum, a speed-up of order 10410^4104 on the full inference pipeline, and Planck 2018 posteriors recovered to within sampling noise — in seconds.

It is an ordinary dense network, not a transformer — which is the point. Attention earns its place when the input is a set or the output a field, not when it is a handful of parameters.

The number that decides it

M/NM/NM/N. If you cannot say what it is for your problem, you are not ready to build an emulator.

The 0.4% figure is quoted relative to the accuracy the surveys require, which is the right comparison — absolute accuracy alone means little.

Where attention helps

Three shapes of surrogate

Parameters → observable

A handful of inputs, a smooth output curve. Power spectra, cross-sections, equations of state. A dense network or a Gaussian process is usually enough and easier to validate; a transformer adds nothing. Use the simplest thing that works.

Field → field

One state of a system to the next: weather, turbulence, structure formation. Attention supplies the non-local coupling that a convolutional stencil cannot, which is why Pangu-Weather and its relatives are transformers. Cost is controlled by patching the field into tokens.

Set → summary

Many objects in, one number or distribution out: a likelihood from a catalogue, a summary statistic from a particle list, a detector response. This is the case that is naturally a transformer, and the one where the choice needs no justification beyond the data type.

A useful discipline

Fit a linear model first, then a Gaussian process or a small dense network, then something larger. Record the error at each stage. Roughly half the time the simple model is adequate, and knowing that costs an afternoon while not knowing it costs the rest of the project.

The middle row is where most current research effort sits; the third row is where the argument for attention is strongest and least contested.

Where the transition happens

Between 60 and 150 examples the gap collapses. There is a threshold, it is problem-specific, and it is cheap to find empirically — train at several budgets and plot this curve. Doing so costs a fraction of the simulation campaign it protects.

In practice

Run the learning curve on a cheap version of the problem — lower resolution, smaller box, fewer particles — and use the shape to plan the expensive campaign. The threshold moves, but the shape rarely does.

Plotting a learning curve before committing simulation time is the highest-value hour in a surrogate project.

Validation

Before you believe a surrogate

  1. Hold out properly. Not a random split if your simulations are correlated — hold out whole runs, whole parameter regions, whole realisations.
  2. Test at the edges. Error in the interior of the training region says nothing about the boundary, and inference chains spend much of their time near the boundary.
  3. Quantify uncertainty. A deep ensemble of five models gives a usable spread; conformal prediction gives calibrated intervals with a guarantee. A point estimate with no error bar is not a scientific result.
  4. Check conservation. Energy, mass, momentum, positivity, unitarity. A learned model respects none of these unless made to, and violations are a sharp diagnostic.
  5. Propagate the emulator error. It enters the posterior. Show that it is small compared with the statistical error — do not assume it.
  6. Re-validate when the problem moves. New priors, resolution or physics: the old validation does not transfer.
The failure that matters

Surrogates fail silently and confidently off-distribution. There is no equivalent of a solver blowing up or a residual refusing to fall: the network returns a plausible number. Every safeguard above exists because nothing else raises the alarm.

The strongest available check

Keep the simulator. Spend a small fraction of the saved budget re-running it at points the surrogate was used at, chosen adversarially — near the edges, at the posterior peak, where the ensemble disagrees. This turns an assumption into a measurement.

Active learning

If simulations are the bottleneck, choose the next one where the ensemble spread is largest rather than at random — typically a factor of a few fewer simulations for the same accuracy.

Point 5 is the one most often skipped in published emulator work, and the one a referee should always ask about.

The one-sentence version

A surrogate is an interpolator with a very flexible basis: trustworthy inside the region it was trained on and densely sampled, unreliable outside it, and silent about which of the two you are in unless you build the alarm yourself.
