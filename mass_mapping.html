Fast mass mapping
with an honest error bar

Reconstructing the dark-matter map (convergence κ) from a noisy shear field is a classic ill-posed inverse problem. Here we build an MMGAN-style amortized posterior sampler — a conditional GAN trained live on lensing mocks — and, crucially, put its uncertainty through a real calibration test.

This deck reproduces the method of Whitney et al. (2024, MNRAS) at demonstrator scale: a conditional generator turns shear + a Kaiser–Squires map + latent noise into posterior samples of κ; their mean is the reconstruction and their spread is the uncertainty. Everything shown is computed from a network I trained here in JAX — not a mock-up.

Shear in, convergence out

Weak lensing distorts galaxy shapes; averaging shapes gives the shear field γ. Shear and convergence κ (the projected mass) share one potential, so in Fourier space they are related by a simple phase — the Kaiser–Squires (KS) relation:

γ̂(k) = ((k1+i k2)² / |k|²) κ̂(k) invertible in the ideal case — but real shear is noisy, masked, and the inversion is ill-posed

Direct KS inversion of noisy shear amplifies noise and gives no error bar. The reconstruction is genuinely uncertain, so the right object is not a single map but a posterior P(κ | γ) — a distribution over maps consistent with the data. The classical Bayesian samplers (proximal MCMC, KaRMMa) do this rigorously but slowly; the deep-learning idea is to amortize it.

The goal. Not just a map, but a calibrated distribution of maps — fast enough to run on every tile of a Euclid / Rubin survey.

A conditional GAN as a posterior sampler

Train a generator G that maps the shear, a KS reconstruction, and a latent noise vector z to a plausible convergence map. Draw many z and you get many maps — approximate samples from the posterior:

κi = G(γ, κKS, zi),  zi ~ 𝒩(0,I)  ⇒  mean = reconstruction,   std = uncertainty one cheap network, sampled repeatedly — posterior mean and pixelwise error bar in under a second

The training objective has three parts (rcGAN-style, Bendel et al. 2023):

  • A supervised term pulling the sample mean toward the truth → the mean approaches the MMSE estimate.
  • A spread term matching the sample std to the reconstruction error — this is what makes the uncertainty meaningful, and the piece that must be tuned carefully (an unbounded diversity reward blows up).
  • An adversarial (Wasserstein) critic keeping individual samples looking like real convergence maps.
Training lesson from this build. An open-ended “maximise diversity” reward is reward-hackable — the generator inflates every output to infinity. The fix is to target the spread to the calibrated posterior width, not maximise it.

The numbers

On held-out mocks, the GAN posterior mean matches KS on correlation but is markedly cleaner in RMSE — because the posterior mean is an MMSE (denoising) estimate, while KS passes the noise straight through:

methodcorrelation with truth ↑RMSE ↓uncertainty?

The correlation gain over KS is modest — at this noise level KS already captures the linear signal — but two things are new: the RMSE drops by roughly a third, and, for the first time, every pixel comes with an error bar. The value of the method is the uncertainty, not a dramatic point-estimate improvement.

Why RMSE not correlation — correlation is insensitive to the noise amplitude KS leaves in; RMSE sees it. The MMSE mean trades a little small-scale power for a large drop in error — a bias we quantify on the power-spectrum page.

Is the error bar honest?

A fast uncertainty is worthless if it is not calibrated. The test: across held-out mocks, what fraction of true pixels actually fall within kσ of the posterior mean? For a well-calibrated Gaussian posterior that should follow the theoretical curve.

Diagonal-style match = calibrated. Empirical curve below the expected = the intervals are too narrow (over-confident); above = too wide (conservative).
The finding. The GAN posterior is mildly over-confident at the core (empirical coverage below expected for small k) and catches up in the tails. This is the textbook GAN calibration issue — the method is useful but its 1σ error bar is a little too tight. It is exactly what a diffusion posterior, or a conformal wrapper, would tighten up.

Simulation-based calibration (SBC)

A sharper test than coverage: for each pixel, where does the truth rank among the posterior samples? If the samples are drawn from the correct posterior, the truth is equally likely to land at any rank — the rank histogram should be flat. A U-shape means under-dispersed (over-confident); a hump means over-dispersed.

Bars: the distribution of the truth’s rank among the samples. Dashed line: the flat, perfectly-calibrated target.
Reasonably flat, slightly bowed. Consistent with the coverage test — broadly calibrated with mild over-confidence. SBC is the diagnostic every posterior-sampling method should report, and it is cheap: it falls straight out of the samples you already drew.

Power spectrum: two opposite biases

Point estimates are biased in telling ways. Compare the angular power spectrum of the truth, the KS map, and the GAN posterior mean:

Log–log power vs angular wavenumber. KS sits above truth (it passes noise through as excess small-scale power); the GAN mean sits below (the MMSE mean is over-smoothed).
Neither point estimate has the right power. KS over-estimates (noise), the posterior mean under-estimates (smoothing). The honest object is the sample set: individual posterior draws restore the correct power, which is why you do statistics on the samples, not on the mean map.

MMGAN’s edge is unambiguously speed — amortized sampling ~1000× faster than the MCMC class, useful for tiling Euclid / Rubin and as a component in simulation-based-inference pipelines. It buys that speed with a small calibration cost.

Where the frontier can move next

If the goal is beating a GAN on both cost and calibration, several routes look promising:

  • Few-step diffusion (flow matching / consistency models). Diffusion posteriors are the best-calibrated; flow-matching and consistency distillation cut sampling from hundreds of steps to 1–4 — diffusion accuracy at GAN speed. The most promising “better on both” direction.
  • Moment / scoring-rule networks. Regress the posterior mean and variance directly under a proper scoring rule — one forward pass gives a calibrated mean + σ, no sampling at all. Cheapest option when you only need moments.
  • Conformal prediction. A distribution-free wrapper that turns any fast method into one with guaranteed coverage, using a small calibration set — a cheap fix for exactly the over-confidence seen here.
  • Unrolled / physics-aware inversion. The KS operator is known — unroll a few proximal-gradient steps with a learned denoiser prior (plug-and-play), plus rotational equivariance. Fewer parameters, more data-efficient, better-calibrated than a black-box GAN.
So we built three of them. The next four pages implement conformal calibration, a moment network and a few-step flow-matching sampler on this same problem, and run them head-to-head against the GAN — with the real numbers.

A distribution-free fix for over-confidence

The GAN gives an error bar, but the calibration test showed it is a little too tight. Conformal prediction repairs that with a guarantee and almost no cost — it needs no retraining and makes no assumption about the shape of the posterior.

The idea: hold out a calibration set with known truth. For each pixel compute a nonconformity score — how many standard deviations the truth sits from the prediction:

s = |κtrue − μ| / σ μ, σ are the GAN’s posterior mean and pixelwise std

To build an interval with target coverage 1−α, take the empirical (1−α) quantile of those calibration scores, q, and widen every interval to μ ± q σ. By exchangeability, the coverage on new data is then guaranteed to be at least 1−α — whatever the true posterior looks like:

P(κtrue ∈ [μ − qσ, μ + qσ]) ≥ 1 − α a finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantee — the theorem does the work the Gaussian assumption could not
Result on this demo. Split-conformal on a held-out half drove the GAN’s mean calibration error from 0.020 → 0.001 — essentially perfect coverage — at the cost of one quantile computation. It doesn’t improve the map, only the honesty of its error bar; but that was the missing piece.

Calibrated mean and σ in a single pass

If you only need the posterior mean and variance — not full map realizations — you can skip sampling entirely. A moment network outputs both directly, and is made calibrated by the choice of loss: a proper scoring rule. Train the network to emit μ(x) and log σ²(x) under the Gaussian negative log-likelihood:

ℒ = ½ ⟨ e−logσ²(κtrue − μ)² + logσ² ⟩ minimised only when μ is the mean and σ² is the true predictive variance — the loss itself enforces calibration

The two terms trade off: the first punishes a wrong mean (weighted by confidence), the second punishes over-confidence (a large σ is penalised unless the errors justify it). Because the optimum is the true first two moments, the network cannot minimise the loss by being over-confident — unlike the GAN’s open-ended diversity reward.

Result on this demo. The moment net gave the best point estimate of all methods (correlation 0.727, RMSE 0.0206, beating both KS and the GAN) and near-perfect calibration (error 0.000) — from a single forward pass, no sampling, and a cheaper, more stable training run than the GAN. When moments are enough, this is the method to beat.
The trade-off. You get μ and σ, but not full field realizations — so you cannot draw calibrated samples for higher-order statistics (peak counts, the PDF). For those you still need a sampler.

Diffusion-class samples in a handful of steps

To get full posterior samples with better calibration than a GAN, the modern tool is a diffusion / flow model. Flow matching (rectified flow) is the simplest form: learn a velocity field vθ that transports pure noise to data along straight lines. Train it by regression — no adversary, no instability:

xt = (1−t) x0 + t κ,   ℒ = ⟨ ‖ vθ(xt, t, γ, κKS) − (κ − x0) ‖² ⟩ x0 ~ 𝒩(0,I); the target velocity is just the straight line from noise to the true map

Sample by drawing noise and integrating the learned velocity in a few Euler steps — here 6:

x ← x + (1/K) vθ(x, t, γ, κKS),  t = 0, 1/K, … each different noise seed gives a different posterior sample; cost is K network evaluations, not hundreds
An honest result. In this scaled-down run the flow matched the GAN on the point estimate (correlation 0.720, RMSE 0.0208) but came out under-dispersed — the worst calibration of the four (error 0.092). That is not a verdict on flow matching; it is what a 1800-step CPU model with 6 sampling steps and no stochasticity does. A production flow (more training, more steps, a stochastic sampler, or a consistency model) is where the best-calibrated fast posteriors actually come from — this demo just shows it needs more care than the two cheap fixes above.

What this demo is and is not

  • Mocks, not κTNG/COSMOS. The convergence fields are Gaussian random fields with a ΛCDM-like spectrum, not full N-body / hydro simulations. Real κ is more non-Gaussian — which makes the calibration question harder, not easier.
  • Scaled down. 64×64 maps and a small U-Net trained on CPU. The point is the method and the diagnostics, not a production reconstruction.
  • Single cosmology, single noise model. The posterior is implicitly conditioned on the training cosmology and noise — fine for a demo, a real caveat for inference.
  • Mild over-confidence. The calibration tests above are honest about it. A production method would need coverage checks on realistic sims before its error bars are trusted.
The transferable result. A conditional GAN really does give fast posterior samples for mass mapping — and a two-line calibration test tells you exactly how much to trust them. That test, not the pretty maps, is the deliverable.

How it was built, and the sources

This demo — a data engine (GRF κ mocks + KS forward model), Kaiser–Squires & Wiener baselines, and a conditional Wasserstein GAN (small U-Net generator + critic, supervised L1 + targeted-spread reward), all hand-rolled in JAX and trained on CPU. The reconstruction, coverage, rank-histogram and power-spectrum panels are computed from the trained network on held-out mocks.
