Machine learning · statistics notes

Conformal prediction: honest error bars for any model

Take any predictor you already trust — a gradient-boosted tree, a neural net, a linear fit — and wrap it so its predictions come with intervals that are guaranteed to contain the truth a chosen fraction of the time. No Gaussian noise, no “correct” model, no large-sample hand-waving. One held-out set and one quantile.

A note on the name (for physicists). “Conformal” here has nothing to do with angle-preserving conformal maps. It takes the other sense of the Latin conform — to fit in with or agree with: the method scores how well a new point conforms to the data already seen (its nonconformity) and keeps every label under which the point still looks typical. Same Latin root as conformal geometry, an entirely different idea.

The idea, with no math

Forget models and probability for a moment. Your food-delivery app says dinner will arrive in 30 minutes. You know not to trust that to the exact minute — but how much slack should you allow? Here is the trick, and it is the whole method.

Look at your last 20 deliveries and, for each, write down how many minutes the app’s estimate was off. Sort those 20 numbers. Now throw away the single worst day and look at the next-largest miss — say it was 12 minutes. That is your cushion. For tonight you say: “30 minutes, give or take 12” — somewhere between 18 and 42 — and because 90% of your past deliveries fell within that cushion, you will be right about 9 times out of 10.

That is conformal prediction in full. There was no bell curve, no assumption about why the app is wrong, no formula beyond “sort and count”. You simply let the model’s own track record of mistakes set the size of the error bar. Three things are worth noticing, because the rest of this note is just making them precise:

  • The cushion came from past mistakes on data you already knew the answer to — never from the current guess. (That held-out track record is the calibration set.)
  • You picked the 90% mark on purpose; wanting to be right 99 times in 100 would mean a bigger cushion. (That is the level \(1-\alpha\).)
  • It works for any predictor — a weather app, a spreadsheet, a neural network — because you only ever looked at its errors, never inside it.

The one thing we leaned on, quietly, is that tomorrow looks like the past — that yesterday’s deliveries are a fair guide to tonight’s. When that breaks (you moved house; the restaurant changed kitchens), so does the promise. That single assumption has a name — exchangeability — and we return to it at the end.

Why this matters

Most machine-learning models hand you a point: a predicted redshift, a predicted price, a predicted class. Point predictions are confident by construction — they never tell you when the model is out of its depth. In science and in business that silence is dangerous: a photo-z estimate for a faint galaxy and one for a bright galaxy look equally crisp, but only one of them is trustworthy.

The usual fixes come with strings attached. A Bayesian posterior (see the companion Bayesian neural networks note) gives beautiful uncertainty — if your prior and likelihood are right. Bootstrap and Gaussian error bars lean on asymptotics or on the noise actually being Gaussian. When the model is misspecified — and it always is — those intervals quietly lose their meaning.

Conformal prediction asks for almost nothing. Fix a miscoverage level \(\alpha\) (say 10%). Then, under a single mild assumption — that your data are exchangeable — it returns a set \(C(x)\) with a finite-sample, distribution-free guarantee:

\[ \mathbb{P}\big(\, y_{\text{new}} \in C(x_{\text{new}}) \,\big) \;\ge\; 1-\alpha . \]

That probability is exact for any sample size, any data distribution, and any underlying model — even a badly wrong one. The model’s quality controls how wide the intervals are; the coverage is guaranteed regardless. That split of duties — the model decides sharpness, conformal decides honesty — is the whole idea.

The one-line version. Measure how wrong your model was on data it didn’t train on, take the 90th percentile of those errors, and attach ±that to every future prediction. Exchangeability turns this folklore into a theorem.

Jargon & notation

Everything below uses only these terms. Each is defined once, here, before it is used.

Miscoverage \(\alpha\)
The error rate you are willing to tolerate. \(\alpha=0.1\) asks for intervals that miss the truth at most 10% of the time, i.e. 90% coverage.
Coverage
The probability that the prediction set actually contains the true label, \(\mathbb{P}(y\in C(x))\). Marginal coverage averages over all \(x\); conditional coverage would hold for every fixed \(x\) separately (much harder — see the last section).
Calibration set
A chunk of labelled data held out and never used to fit the model. Conformal prediction learns its one magic number from this set.
Nonconformity score \(s_i\)
A number measuring how badly the model fits point \(i\) — “how strange is this example?” For regression the natural choice is the absolute residual \(s_i=|y_i-\hat f(x_i)|\). Any score works; the choice only affects efficiency, never validity.
Conformal quantile \(\hat q\)
A specific empirical quantile of the calibration scores — the threshold that separates “normal” from “too strange”. The level is nudged slightly above \(1-\alpha\); that nudge is what makes the guarantee exact in finite samples.
Exchangeability
The data can be reshuffled without changing their joint distribution — a weaker cousin of “independent and identically distributed”. It is the only assumption conformal prediction needs, and the one that fails under distribution shift.
Prediction set \(C(x)\)
The output: an interval \([\,\hat f(x)-\hat q,\ \hat f(x)+\hat q\,]\) for regression, or a subset of class labels for classification.

Split conformal prediction

In this section

The practical workhorse is split (or inductive) conformal prediction. It fits the model once and costs essentially nothing on top. Here is the entire recipe.

The recipe

  1. Split your labelled data into a training set and a calibration set. The two must not overlap.
  2. Fit the model \(\hat f\) on the training set only, exactly as you normally would. Conformal prediction does not care how — it treats \(\hat f\) as a black box.
  3. Score the calibration set. For each of the \(n\) held-out points compute the nonconformity score \(s_i=|y_i-\hat f(x_i)|\). These are the model’s honest, out-of-sample mistakes.
  4. Take the conformal quantile. Sort the scores and read off \(\hat q\), the \(\big\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\big\rceil / n\) empirical quantile — the \((1-\alpha)\) quantile with a small finite-sample bump.
  5. Predict with a band. For a new input \(x\), report \(\;C(x)=\big[\hat f(x)-\hat q,\ \hat f(x)+\hat q\big]\).

The quantile level deserves a second look, because the little “\(+1\)” is the heart of the finite-sample guarantee:

\[ \hat q \;=\; \text{the } \Big\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\Big\rceil\text{-th smallest of } s_1,\dots,s_n . \]

With \(n=500\) calibration points and \(\alpha=0.1\) this is the \(\lceil 501\times 0.9\rceil = 451\)st smallest residual — just slightly more conservative than the plain 90th percentile. As \(n\) grows the correction vanishes, but for small calibration sets it is exactly what keeps coverage from dipping below \(1-\alpha\).

The whole method in fifteen lines

No special library is needed. This wraps any fitted scikit-learn-style model:

conformal_core.pysplit conformal regression 
"""
Split (inductive) conformal prediction — the whole idea in ~15 lines.
=====================================================================
Wrap ANY fitted regressor so its intervals carry a finite-sample,
distribution-free coverage guarantee: P( y in C(x) ) >= 1 - alpha,
assuming only that the data are exchangeable. No Gaussian noise, no
correct model, no asymptotics.
"""
import numpy as np

def split_conformal_band(model, X_cal, y_cal, X_test, alpha=0.1):
    # 1. nonconformity scores on a held-out CALIBRATION set: how wrong was
    #    the (already fitted) model on points it did not train on?
    resid = np.abs(y_cal - model.predict(X_cal))          # s_i = |y_i - yhat(x_i)|

    # 2. the conformal quantile: the ceil((n+1)(1-alpha))-th smallest score.
    #    The small +1 finite-sample correction is what makes the guarantee exact.
    n = len(resid)
    q_level = np.ceil((n + 1) * (1 - alpha)) / n           # slightly above 1-alpha
    q_hat = np.quantile(resid, min(q_level, 1.0), method="higher")

    # 3. the prediction interval is the point prediction +/- that one number.
    pred = model.predict(X_test)
    return pred - q_hat, pred + q_hat, q_hat               # lower, upper, width

It really does cover

Coverage on a single test set is itself a random quantity — you got one particular calibration set, after all. The guarantee is that it lands at or above \(1-\alpha\) on average, for any model and any distribution. Why it works

The proof is short enough to carry in your head, and it is the reason the method feels like magic the first time.

The exchangeability argument

Put the new test point’s score \(s_{n+1}=|y_{n+1}-\hat f(x_{n+1})|\) alongside the \(n\) calibration scores. Because the model \(\hat f\) was fixed before seeing any of these points, and the points are exchangeable, the \(n+1\) scores are themselves exchangeable: every ordering is equally likely. In particular, the rank of \(s_{n+1}\) among the \(n+1\) scores is uniform on \(\{1,2,\dots,n+1\}\).

\[ \mathbb{P}\!\left(s_{n+1} \le \hat q\right) \;=\; \mathbb{P}\!\left(\text{rank of } s_{n+1} \le \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil\right) \;=\; \frac{\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil}{n+1} \;\ge\; 1-\alpha . \]

And \(s_{n+1}\le\hat q\) is exactly the event \(|y_{n+1}-\hat f(x_{n+1})|\le\hat q\), i.e. \(y_{n+1}\in C(x_{n+1})\). That is the guarantee, finished. No Taylor expansion, no central limit theorem, no assumption on the shape of the noise — only that swapping a calibration point for the test point changes nothing about the distribution.

Where each assumption lives. The model being fixed before calibration is why you must never score points the model trained on. Exchangeability is why i.i.d. data (a special case) are fine — and why time series and shifted test sets are not.

Making intervals adaptive

Vanilla split conformal has one cosmetic flaw: because \(\hat q\) is a single number, every interval has the same width. That is wasteful when the noise varies — too wide in the clean regions, too narrow in the messy ones, even though the average coverage is still correct.

Normalised scores and CQR

Two fixes restore local adaptivity while keeping the exact guarantee. Normalised conformal divides the residual by an estimate of the local scale, \(s_i=|y_i-\hat f(x_i)|/\hat\sigma(x_i)\), so the band breathes with the noise. The cleaner modern choice is conformalised quantile regression (CQR): fit models for the lower and upper quantiles, \(\hat q_{\alpha/2}\) and \(\hat q_{1-\alpha/2}\), then conformalise their shortfall,

\[ s_i \;=\; \max\!\Big(\hat q_{\alpha/2}(x_i)-y_i,\;\; y_i-\hat q_{1-\alpha/2}(x_i)\Big), \qquad C(x)=\big[\hat q_{\alpha/2}(x)-E,\ \hat q_{1-\alpha/2}(x)+E\big], \]

where \(E\) is the conformal quantile of those scores. The quantile regressors supply the shape; conformal supplies the guarantee, correcting whatever the quantile models got wrong.

Classification: sets, not single labels

For classification the output is not an interval but a set of labels. Use a nonconformity score such as \(s_i = 1-\hat p(y_i\mid x_i)\) — one minus the softmax probability the model gave the true class. Calibrate \(\hat q\) exactly as before, then for a new \(x\) include every label whose probability clears the bar:

\[ C(x) \;=\; \big\{\, k : 1-\hat p(k\mid x) \le \hat q \,\big\} \;=\; \big\{\, k : \hat p(k\mid x) \ge 1-\hat q \,\big\}. \]

The guarantee is unchanged: the true class lands in the set at least \(1-\alpha\) of the time. The beauty is that the size of the set now carries information for free — one label where the model is confident, several where it is not.

Conformal vs Bayesian uncertainty

These are the two honest ways to get uncertainty, and they answer different questions. It is worth being precise about the trade, especially next to the Bayesian neural networks note.

Two routes to an honest error bar
 Bayesian posteriorConformal prediction
What it needsA prior and a likelihood you believeOnly exchangeable data
Coverage guaranteeExact if the model is correctly specified; no promise otherwiseExact and finite-sample, for any model — even a wrong one
What you getA full distribution over outcomes (and parameters)A set with a coverage rate — nothing more, nothing less
Conditional on \(x\)?Yes, naturally per-inputMarginal by default; per-input coverage is hard
CostPosterior inference (MCMC, variational, …)One extra held-out set; model fit once
Fails whenPrior/likelihood misspecifiedExchangeability broken (shift, time)

They compose, which is the practical punchline. Take a Bayesian neural network’s predictive mean and variance — a well-calibrated guess at the shape of the uncertainty — and run it through CQR. You keep the Bayesian model’s per-input adaptivity and gain a distribution-free coverage guarantee that survives its misspecification. Best of both: the posterior proposes, conformal certifies.

The catch: when coverage breaks

In this section

Conformal prediction is not free of assumptions — it has exactly one, and it is worth respecting.

Exchangeability is load-bearing

Everything rests on the test point being exchangeable with the calibration set. Break that and the guarantee goes with it. The most common way to break it is distribution shift: the world you deployed into is not the world you calibrated on.

Two caveats, and their fixes

Marginal, not conditional. Standard conformal guarantees coverage averaged over inputs. It can over-cover easy regions and under-cover hard ones while the average stays at 90%. Group-balanced (Mondrian) and locally-adaptive conformal push toward conditional coverage; exact conditional coverage is provably impossible without extra assumptions.

Shift has a patch. When the shift is a known covariate shift, weighted conformal prediction re-weights the calibration scores by the likelihood ratio and restores the guarantee. For time series, variants such as adaptive conformal inference adjust \(\alpha\) online as coverage drifts. The assumption never disappears — but it can often be bought back.

For a cosmologist. The photo-z story in the BNN note is exactly the exchangeability trap: a spectroscopic training sample is bright and clean, the photometric survey runs faint. That is covariate shift, so naive conformal intervals on faint galaxies will under-cover — precisely where honest error bars matter most. Weighted conformal, with weights from the selection function, is the principled fix.

In cosmology and physics

In this section

This is not a toy method for the physical sciences — it addresses a problem that modern surveys and experiments have in abundance: machine-learning predictions at enormous scale that must come with trustworthy error bars, from models that are never exactly right.

Where it lands

The flagship application is photometric redshifts. Surveys like LSST and Euclid will estimate redshifts for billions of galaxies from a handful of broad-band magnitudes. Decisions downstream — weak-lensing tomography, clustering — depend on honest redshift uncertainties, and conformal prediction supplies them with a coverage guarantee no matter which estimator (template-fitting, random forest, neural net) produced the point estimate. Beyond photo-z, conformal methods are being used to calibrate simulation-based-inference posteriors (neural posterior estimators trained on N-body or hydrodynamic simulations are often overconfident), to put guaranteed error bars on cosmological emulators that stand in for expensive Boltzmann and N-body codes, and for anomaly and transient detection with a controlled false-alarm rate.

The same machinery runs across physics: calibrated signal-versus-background confidence sets at the LHC with a controlled false-discovery rate, error bars on ML emulators for lattice QCD and materials simulation, and post-hoc calibration of gravitational-wave and neutrino parameter-estimation posteriors.

This is not speculative. Conformal prediction has already been applied to cosmological redshift estimation in the refereed literature — a Bayesian CNN with conformal intervals for redshifts from galaxy images (ApJ 2024), and split conformal photo-z benchmarked against LSST requirements on COSMOS2020 / GalaxiesML (2026) — and conformal calibration of simulation-based-inference posteriors is an active line of work. See the references.

Worked example: photo-z under selection shift

Here is the trap from the BNN note, made quantitative. The spectroscopic sample used for calibration skews bright; the photometric survey runs faint, where colours are noisier. That is a covariate shift keyed to an observed quantity — brightness — so the calibration set is not exchangeable with the faint galaxies.

The fix uses the structure of the problem: brightness is measured, and it drives the shift, so we condition on it. Group-conditional (Mondrian) conformal calibrates per brightness bin; each bin then carries its own valid guarantee. The faint intervals grow — roughly four times wider here — which is the point: those redshifts genuinely are less certain, and now the error bars say so. When the shifting variable is continuous or unobserved, weighted conformal with density-ratio weights is the continuous analogue of the same idea.

Why a cosmologist should care. “90% of my photo-z intervals contain the true redshift” is a statement you can prove holds for your actual survey — not assume because your likelihood was Gaussian — and it degrades gracefully and detectably when it stops holding. For a field that lives and dies by its error budget, that is exactly the right contract.
