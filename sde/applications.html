In physics

Six you will actually meet

Thermal and electrical noise

Brownian motion, Johnson-Nyquist noise in a resistor, thermal noise in a mirror suspension. All Ornstein-Uhlenbeck or free diffusion, all with amplitude fixed by fluctuation-dissipation. The noise floor of a gravitational-wave detector is this calculation done carefully.

Stellar and plasma dynamics

Two-body relaxation in a star cluster is a Fokker-Planck problem: many small velocity kicks, described by drift and diffusion coefficients in energy and angular momentum. The same formalism governs Coulomb collisions in a plasma.

Accretion and variability

Quasar optical light curves are well described by a damped random walk — an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process — with a characteristic timescale that correlates with black-hole mass. Fitting θ\thetaθ and σ\sigmaσ is fitting an SDE to data.

Chemical kinetics

The chemical Langevin equation: the large-copy-number limit of the discrete master equation, with noise amplitude rate\sqrt{\text{rate}}rate​. Multiplicative, so the Ito-Stratonovich question is live and consequential.

Population and epidemics

Demographic stochasticity gives dN=rN dt+σN dWdN=rN\,dt+\sigma\sqrt N\,dWdN=rNdt+σN​dW; environmental noise gives σN dW\sigma N\,dWσNdW. The two have completely different extinction behaviour, and telling them apart is the scientific question.

Finance

Geometric Brownian motion, Black-Scholes as a backward Kolmogorov equation, and the option price as a Feynman-Kac expectation. The one field that consistently means Ito, and says so.

The chemical and population examples are the ones where the calculus choice changes published conclusions, because the noise is genuinely multiplicative.

Machine learning

Diffusion models are a reverse-time SDE

Take data x0x_0x0​ and destroy it with a forward SDE until nothing is left but noise:

dX=f(X,t) dt+g(t) dW,X0∼pdata,XT≈N(0,I).dX=f(X,t)\,dt+g(t)\,dW,\qquad X_0\sim p_{\rm data},\quad X_T\approx\mathcal{N}(0,I).dX=f(X,t)dt+g(t)dW,X0​∼pdata​,XT​≈N(0,I).

Anderson's theorem (1982) says this process run backwards is also an SDE:

dX=[f(X,t)−g2(t) ∇xlog⁡pt(X)]dt+g(t) dWˉ,dX=\Big[f(X,t)-g^2(t)\,\nabla_x\log p_t(X)\Big]dt+g(t)\,d\bar W,dX=[f(X,t)−g2(t)∇x​logpt​(X)]dt+g(t)dWˉ,

with Wˉ\bar WWˉ a Brownian motion in reversed time. The only unknown is the score ∇xlog⁡pt\nabla_x\log p_t∇x​logpt​ — and page 6 already identified that as the drift of a Langevin sampler.

So a diffusion model is: learn the score, then integrate the reverse SDE from noise. The celebrated variants are choices of fff and ggg — variance-preserving, variance-exploding — and DDIM is the probability-flow ODE sharing the same marginals. Every numerical concern from page 3 reappears.

Why physicists have an advantage here

Nothing above is new mathematics. It is Fokker-Planck, time reversal, and Langevin sampling — subjects already in a statistical-physics course. The machine-learning contribution is that the score can be estimated by regression (denoising score matching), which is genuinely clever and entirely separate from the SDE theory.

Related material

A longer treatment, with derivations of the VP and VE forms, Tweedie's formula, DDIM as a probability-flow ODE and flow matching, is in the companion diffusion-models document.

Anderson's reverse-time result predates the machine-learning application by forty years and is worth citing as such.

Neural SDEs

Learning the drift, and where it pays

If the drift and diffusion are unknown functions, parameterise them by networks and fit:

dX=fθ(X,t) dt+gθ(X,t) dW.dX=f_\theta(X,t)\,dt+g_\theta(X,t)\,dW .dX=fθ​(X,t)dt+gθ​(X,t)dW.

Training needs gradients through a stochastic integration, which is where the difficulty sits. Two routes, with the trade-off familiar from the PINN set:

  • Backpropagate through the solver. Exact for the discretisation used, but memory grows with the number of steps.
  • Stochastic adjoint (Li et al. 2020). Constant memory, but the reverse pass must reconstruct the same Brownian path — solved with a Brownian interval tree that regenerates increments deterministically.

Where it genuinely pays: irregularly sampled time series with real uncertainty, latent-state models where the noise is part of what you want to learn, and generative modelling of paths rather than points. Where it does not: any system whose SDE you already know — fit its parameters instead, as on page 5 of the PINN set.

The honest assessment

Neural SDEs are harder to train than neural ODEs and much harder than fitting a known SDE. The variance of the gradient estimator is the binding constraint, not expressiveness. Reach for one when the functional form is genuinely unknown — and check first that a parametric fit really fails.

The middle ground

Often the best answer is a known SDE with a few learned components — a physical drift plus a learned correction, or a known structure with state-dependent noise learned from data. Fewer parameters, interpretable, and far better behaved.

Tooling

torchsde implements the adjoint and the Brownian tree; diffrax does the same in JAX with a wider choice of solvers. Both on page 8.

The "check a parametric fit really fails first" advice mirrors the PINN set's conclusion, and for the same reason: flexibility is not free.
