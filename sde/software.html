Libraries

What to install

Julia — the best of them

DifferentialEquations.jl has the most complete SDE support anywhere: adaptive stepping, stiff solvers, high strong-order methods, Ito and Stratonovich as explicit choices, jump processes. If SDE numerics is the point of your project, this is worth learning Julia for.

Python — gradients

torchsde — solvers plus the stochastic adjoint and the Brownian interval tree, for neural SDEs in PyTorch.

diffrax — the JAX equivalent, with a wider solver set, and the better choice for new work in JAX.

Python — plain

sdeint — small and readable: Euler-Maruyama, Milstein and a few higher-order schemes, both calculi. For one equation in one or two dimensions, twenty lines of NumPy is often clearer — and you will know which calculus you are in.

Libraries, continued

Around the edges, and one warning

Related tooling

Fokker-Planck: any PDE package — FEniCS, Dedalus — in low dimension. Sampling: NumPyro, BlackJAX and Stan for Langevin-family samplers with the accept step done properly. Fitting to data: celerite2 and Stingray for OU-process time series.

Write it yourself, once

Euler-Maruyama is three lines, Milstein four. Write them, reproduce σ2/2θ\sigma^2/2\thetaσ2/2θ, measure the strong order. An afternoon, and the libraries stop being opaque.

The one thing to check

Whichever library you use, find out which calculus it assumes before trusting a result with multiplicative noise. Some default to Ito, some to Stratonovich, some let you choose and default to one without saying which.

That last point is not hypothetical: the same model coded in two libraries can give different answers for exactly this reason.

Reading

Four books and a handful of papers

For physicists

Gardiner, Stochastic Methods — the standard reference. Fokker-Planck, first-passage times, master equations, everything with physics conventions.

Van Kampen, Stochastic Processes in Physics and Chemistry — opinionated, excellent on when the diffusion approximation is legitimate and when it is not.

Risken, The Fokker-Planck Equation — if you need to actually solve one.

For the numerics

Kloeden & Platen, Numerical Solution of Stochastic Differential Equations — the definitive treatment of schemes and convergence orders. Everything on page 3 is in chapters 9-10.

Higham, SIAM Review 43, 525 (2001) — "An algorithmic introduction": twenty pages, MATLAB code, and the clearest explanation of strong versus weak convergence in print.

For the connections

Oksendal, Stochastic Differential Equations — rigorous but readable; the Feynman-Kac chapter is the best short treatment.

Anderson, Stoch. Proc. Appl. 12, 313 (1982) — reverse-time diffusions, the theorem underneath every diffusion model.

Song et al., ICLR (2021) — score-based generative modelling through SDEs.

If you read one thing

Higham's 2001 review. It is twenty pages, it contains runnable code, and it will make the difference between strong and weak convergence permanent — which is the single most useful distinction on these pages and the one most often not known.

Gardiner and Van Kampen disagree about the diffusion approximation in places; the disagreement is itself instructive.

Before you believe it

A checklist that catches real errors

  1. State the calculus. Ito or Stratonovich, written down. If the noise is additive, say that it does not matter — do not leave it unsaid.
  2. Check the noise amplitude against fluctuation-dissipation. For a thermal system the amplitude is fixed by the damping and the temperature; it is not a free parameter.
  3. Halve the step. If the answer moves, you were not converged. Do this before anything else, every time.
  4. Reproduce something known. The stationary variance σ2/2θ\sigma^2/2\thetaσ2/2θ, the equilibrium distribution, an analytic mean. If a known quantity comes out wrong, stop.
  5. Separate the two errors. Statistical error falls as 1/N1/\sqrt N1/N​; discretisation error as Δtβ\Delta t^\betaΔtβ. Quote both, and balance them rather than driving one far below the other.
  6. Use exact updates where they exist. Linear parts of the drift can be integrated without discretisation error at any step size.
The three that actually bite

Wrong calculus with multiplicative noise — changes the answer, silently.
Noise amplitude inconsistent with damping — equilibrates to the wrong temperature, silently.
Step size too large — looks like a physical drift rather than an instability, and therefore gets interpreted.

All three produce plausible output. None announces itself.

The companion sets

Monte Carlo samplers — Metropolis, Gibbs, HMC, NUTS and nested sampling, the algorithmic side of page 6.
Time-series analysis — fitting these processes to data, including the OU/AR(1) correspondence.
Physics-informed neural networks — the deterministic counterpart, and inverse problems.

Point 4 is the one that has saved the most time in practice: pick a limit where you know the answer and check it before running the case you care about.
