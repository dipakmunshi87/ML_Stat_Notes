Langevin dynamics:
physics and sampling

One equation with two lives. As physics it is a particle in a fluid, kicked and damped. As an algorithm it is how modern samplers explore a probability distribution — and the two readings are the same mathematics.

The sampling reading is why this page matters outside statistical physics: it is the basis of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, of stochastic-gradient Langevin dynamics, and of diffusion models.

The equation

Kicks and damping, in balance

Newton's law for a particle in a fluid, with the fluid split into a systematic drag and a random force:

m x¨=−∇U(x)−γ x˙+2γT  ξ(t).m\,\ddot x=-\nabla U(x)-\gamma\,\dot x+\sqrt{2\gamma T}\;\xi(t).mx¨=−∇U(x)−γx˙+2γT​ξ(t).

The coefficient of the noise is not free. Both terms come from the same molecular collisions, so the requirement that the stationary state be thermal forces the amplitude to be 2γT\sqrt{2\gamma T}2γT​ — the fluctuation-dissipation theorem. Get it wrong and the simulation equilibrates to the wrong temperature, silently.

When inertia is negligible — γ\gammaγ large, or times long compared with m/γm/\gammam/γ — drop mx¨m\ddot xmx¨ and rescale time to get the overdamped equation used everywhere on this page:

dX=−∇U(X) dt+2T dW.dX=-\nabla U(X)\,dt+\sqrt{2T}\,dW .dX=−∇U(X)dt+2T​dW.

Page 4 showed its stationary density is p∞∝e−U/Tp_\infty\propto e^{-U/T}p∞​∝e−U/T. Read forwards, that is thermal equilibrium. Read backwards, it is a recipe: to sample from any density ppp, set U=−log⁡pU=-\log pU=−logp and run this equation.

Where the two limits apply

Underdamped: molecular dynamics with a thermostat, colloids on short timescales, anything where inertia carries the system across a barrier.
Overdamped: protein conformational change, reaction coordinates, and essentially all of Monte Carlo, where there is no physical inertia to respect.

The sampling reading

With U=−log⁡pU=-\log pU=−logp the drift is +∇log⁡p+\nabla\log p+∇logp — the score. The equation becomes

dX=∇log⁡p(X) dt+2 dW,dX=\nabla\log p(X)\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dW,dX=∇logp(X)dt+2​dW,

which climbs the log-density while diffusing. That drift is exactly the quantity a diffusion model learns, which is why page 7 can treat generative modelling as an SDE.

The fluctuation-dissipation constraint is the single most common source of silently wrong Langevin simulations: people tune the noise and the damping independently.

As an algorithm

The bias, and the accept step

Discretise with Euler-Maruyama and you have the unadjusted Langevin algorithm:

Xn+1=Xn+∇log⁡p(Xn) Δt+2Δt  ξn.X_{n+1}=X_n+\nabla\log p(X_n)\,\Delta t+\sqrt{2\Delta t}\;\xi_n .Xn+1​=Xn​+∇logp(Xn​)Δt+2Δt​ξn​.

Cheap, needs only the gradient of the log-density, scales to high dimension — and does not sample ppp. The discrete chain's stationary distribution differs from ppp by O(Δt)O(\Delta t)O(Δt), and the bias does not average away; only smaller steps remove it, which slows mixing.

The fix is a Metropolis accept-reject step: MALA. Propose yyy from the Euler step, accept with probability

α=min⁡ ⁣(1, p(y) q(x∣y)p(x) q(y∣x)),\alpha=\min\!\left(1,\ \frac{p(y)\,q(x\mid y)}{p(x)\,q(y\mid x)}\right),α=min(1, p(x)q(y∣x)p(y)q(x∣y)​),

with qqq the Gaussian proposal implied by the step. Detailed balance is restored exactly and the stationary distribution is ppp for any Δt\Delta tΔt: the step size now controls efficiency, not correctness.

Why this matters

It converts a discretisation error — invisible, and not reduced by running longer — into an acceptance rate, which is visible and tunable. The recommended target is about 57% for MALA, against 23% for random-walk Metropolis and 65% for HMC.

The family

Add momentum: underdamped Langevin, whose adjusted discretisation is Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Replace the gradient with a minibatch estimate: stochastic-gradient Langevin dynamics. One equation, three compromises.

When not to adjust

In very high dimension the acceptance rate collapses. Much of machine learning runs unadjusted and tolerates the bias deliberately — defensible, but it should be a choice.

Rates

Kramers, and the exponential

For a barrier ΔU\Delta UΔU large compared with TTT, the escape rate from a well is

k=∣U′′(xb)∣ U′′(xw)2πγ e−ΔU/T,k=\frac{\sqrt{|U''(x_b)|\,U''(x_w)}}{2\pi\gamma}\,e^{-\Delta U/T},k=2πγ∣U′′(xb​)∣U′′(xw​)​​e−ΔU/T,

with curvatures taken at the barrier top and the well bottom. The prefactor is an attempt frequency; the exponential is everything. A barrier of 10 T10\,T10T gives e−10≈4×10−5e^{-10}\approx4\times10^{-5}e−10≈4×10−5 — four orders of magnitude of waiting, from a factor of ten in energy.

This is why direct simulation of rare events fails. To see a transition you must simulate for many times 1/k1/k1/k, and 1/k1/k1/k grows exponentially in the barrier height. The entire field of enhanced sampling — umbrella sampling, metadynamics, forward-flux, transition-path sampling — exists to get around this one exponential.

The demonstration makes the sensitivity visible: between T=0.4T=0.4T=0.4 and T=0.04T=0.04T=0.04 the barrier goes from under one TTT to six, and the crossing rate falls by roughly e5≈150e^{5}\approx150e5≈150.

Recognising the pattern

Arrhenius kinetics, nucleation, dielectric relaxation, protein folding, magnetic switching, false-vacuum decay. Wherever a system must cross an energy barrier assisted by thermal noise, this is the formula, and the exponential dominates every other consideration.

The derivation in one line

Assume a quasi-stationary probability current over the barrier, evaluate it by steepest descent using the harmonic approximations at the well and the saddle, divide by the population in the well. The prefactor is the ratio of two Gaussian integrals.
