The formula

An expectation that solves a PDE

Consider the backward equation with a potential term,

∂u∂t=σ22∂2u∂x2−V(x) u,u(x,0)=f(x).\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}-V(x)\,u, \qquad u(x,0)=f(x).∂t∂u​=2σ2​∂x2∂2u​−V(x)u,u(x,0)=f(x).

The Feynman-Kac formula says its solution is an average over Brownian paths started at xxx:

Each path contributes its endpoint value f(XT)f(X_T)f(XT​), weighted by the exponential of the accumulated potential along the way. Paths that linger where VVV is large are exponentially suppressed.

The proof is Ito's lemma applied to u(Xt,T−t)exp⁡(−∫0tV)u(X_t,T-t)\exp(-\int_0^tV)u(Xt​,T−t)exp(−∫0t​V): the drift terms cancel precisely because uuu satisfies the PDE, leaving a martingale, whose expectation is therefore constant in time. Evaluate at t=0t=0t=0 and t=Tt=Tt=T and the formula falls out.

Physics picture

This is the path integral, in imaginary time and therefore convergent. Feynman's sum over histories with weight eiS/ℏe^{iS/\hbar}eiS/ℏ oscillates and is not a measure; rotating to imaginary time turns it into e−Se^{-S}e−S, which is one, and the measure is Brownian.

Why anyone would do this

The cost of sampling paths is independent of dimension, while a grid costs ndn^dnd. For d≳4d\gtrsim4d≳4 the PDE is unsolvable and the expectation is routine. This is the whole argument for Monte Carlo methods in quantum chemistry and in finance.

The price

Statistical error falling as 1/N1/\sqrt N1/N​. To gain one decimal place you need a hundred times the paths. Feynman-Kac is not a fast way to solve a one-dimensional PDE — it is the only way to attack a fifty-dimensional one.

The martingale proof is four lines and worth doing once; it makes clear that the exponential weight is exactly what is needed to cancel the −Vu-Vu−Vu term.

This is diffusion Monte Carlo

Add branching — replicate paths with large weight, kill those with small — and you have DMC, the workhorse of quantum chemistry. Importance sampling with a trial wavefunction reduces the variance enormously. The demonstration uses neither, so its error bars are honest but large.

The fermion sign problem

All of this requires a positive weight. Antisymmetric wavefunctions change sign, the "probability" goes negative, and the variance grows exponentially with system size. That obstruction is still open, and it is why quantum Monte Carlo is easy for bosons and hard for electrons.

The distinction between ψ0\psi_0ψ0​ and ∣ψ0∣2|\psi_0|^2∣ψ0​∣2 here catches people out: the weighted endpoint density is linear in the wavefunction because only one propagator appears.

Summary

Where the bridge is used

Quantum many-body

Diffusion and path-integral Monte Carlo: ground-state energies of molecules and solids from weighted random walks in 3N3N3N dimensions, where no grid could ever be laid down. Limited by the sign problem for fermions.

Finance

Black-Scholes is a backward Kolmogorov equation, and the option price is an expectation over risk-neutral paths. High-dimensional baskets and path-dependent payoffs are priced by Monte Carlo for exactly the reason above.

Elliptic PDEs and beyond

Walk-on-spheres for Laplace and Poisson problems; first-passage formulations of escape and reaction rates; committor functions in rare-event theory. Anywhere a boundary-value problem can be written as "average over paths until they leave".

The pattern to recognise

Whenever a linear PDE has the shape ∂tu=Lu−Vu\partial_t u=\mathcal{L}u-Vu∂t​u=Lu−Vu with L\mathcal{L}L the generator of some process, its solution is an expectation over that process with weight e−∫Ve^{-\int V}e−∫V. Identify the process, simulate it, average. The dimension of the problem stops mattering and the statistical error takes its place.

The trade is always the same: exponential cost in dimension replaced by 1/N1/\sqrt N1/N​ statistical error. Below about four dimensions the PDE wins; above it, there is no contest.
