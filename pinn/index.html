neural networks

A network whose output is the solution of your differential equation, found by penalising the equation's own residual. This set derives the method, trains it in front of you, and is equally specific about where it wins and where it has no business being used.

Every demonstration trains a real network in your browser — automatic differentiation, Adam, a few hundred parameters. Nothing here is a recording.

The idea in one equation

Write the differential equation so it vanishes on the solution, R[u]=0\mathcal{R}[u]=0R[u]=0. Replace the unknown function by a neural network. Then the equation becomes the loss function:

θ⋆=arg⁡min⁡θ 1N∑i=1N∣R[uθ](xi)∣2  +  λb (boundary terms)  +  λd (data, if any).\theta^\star=\arg\min_\theta\ \frac1{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big|\mathcal{R}[u_\theta](x_i)\big|^2 \;+\;\lambda_b\,(\text{boundary terms})\;+\;\lambda_d\,(\text{data, if any}).θ⋆=argθmin​ N1​i=1∑N​​R[uθ​](xi​)​2+λb​(boundary terms)+λd​(data, if any).

The derivatives inside R\mathcal{R}R come from automatic differentiation, so they are exact — no grid, no stencil, no step size. The points xix_ixi​ are places where the equation is checked, not nodes of a mesh. Nothing is discretised.

Two consequences follow immediately, and they are the whole subject. Because there is no mesh, cost need not grow as ndn^dnd — which is why the serious applications are in high dimension. And because the answer arrives through non-convex optimisation rather than a linear solve, none of the guarantees you are used to survive.

Physics picture

Rayleigh–Ritz with a very flexible trial function. You pick a parameterised family of candidate solutions and vary the parameters to minimise a functional — here the squared residual rather than an energy. The variational idea is old; the expressive trial family and exact cheap derivatives are new.

The sentence to remember

A PINN does not solve your equation. It minimises a non-convex function whose global minimum would solve your equation. Every failure mode on page 4 lives in the gap between those two statements.

Three results from these pages

It does work

On −u′′=f-u''=f−u′′=f with a manufactured solution, the PINN here reaches a relative L2L^2L2 error of 0.01%. The damped oscillator at ω=2\omega=2ω=2 reaches 0.06%. These are measured against the exact solutions, not against the training loss.

The ansatz is worth fifty-fold

Changing the hard-constraint envelope from x2x^2x2 to x2/(1+x2)x^2/(1+x^2)x2/(1+x2) took the error at ω=4\omega=4ω=4 from 69% to 1.4% with nothing else altered. Conditioning of the trial family, not network size, was the binding constraint.

And finite differences still win

The same Poisson problem, solved by a tridiagonal finite-difference solve: 0.0015% in 185 microseconds. The PINN needs about seventeen seconds to reach 0.01%. Five orders of magnitude, and it is not close.

The third result is not a criticism of PINNs. It is the reason the interesting applications are inverse problems, high dimensions, and equations for which no good classical method exists — not one-dimensional boundary-value problems.

Five habits worth having

Do

  1. Non-dimensionalise, and rescale the inputs to [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1], before anything else.
  2. Impose boundary and initial conditions hard whenever the geometry allows it.
  3. Check against an exact or converged classical solution on a problem where you can.
  4. Plot the residual across the domain, not just the loss against step number.
  5. Ask what a classical solver would cost before reaching for a network at all.

Don't

  1. Read a small training loss as a small error — smooth wrong answers have small residuals.
  2. Fix a failure by adding layers; fix the conditioning, the weighting or the ansatz.
  3. Train a long time interval in one shot; march it.
  4. Leave the loss weights at one and assume the optimiser will sort it out.
  5. Quote a recovered parameter without checking it is identifiable from the data you have.

Seven pages, each a single self-contained HTML file.
