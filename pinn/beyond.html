Part 6 of 7 · integral equations

Beyond differential equations

An integral equation has no stencil. There is nothing local to discretise, and the classical methods are a different toolkit entirely. This is the setting where the collocation formulation is not competing with something better — it is the natural thing to write down.

The formulation

A residual is a residual

Nothing in the PINN construction required R\mathcal{R}R to contain derivatives. Take a Fredholm equation of the second kind — or a Volterra equation, where the upper limit moves with xxx:

u(x)−λ∫ab ⁣K(x,y)u(y) dy=f(x),u(x)−∫ax ⁣K(x,y)u(y) dy=f(x).u(x)-\lambda\int_a^b\! K(x,y)u(y)\,dy=f(x),\qquad u(x)-\int_a^x\! K(x,y)u(y)\,dy=f(x).u(x)−λ∫ab​K(x,y)u(y)dy=f(x),u(x)−∫ax​K(x,y)u(y)dy=f(x).

The residual follows immediately. The one new ingredient is a quadrature rule, since the integral must be approximated:

R[uθ](xi)=uθ(xi)−λ∑m=1MwmK(xi,ym) uθ(ym)−f(xi).\mathcal{R}[u_\theta](x_i)=u_\theta(x_i)-\lambda\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_m K(x_i,y_m)\,u_\theta(y_m)-f(x_i).R[uθ​](xi​)=uθ​(xi​)−λm=1∑M​wm​K(xi​,ym​)uθ​(ym​)−f(xi​).

That is the whole method: the network is evaluated at the quadrature nodes as well as the collocation points, and everything else is unchanged. Note what has not happened — no matrix assembled, no linear system solved. The classical route discretises to an M×MM\times MM×M dense system; this never forms one.

Physics picture

Where the physics is non-local: Fredholm for scattering and boundary-integral formulations, Volterra for memory and causal response, Dyson- and Bethe–Salpeter-type equations in field theory. Nothing local, so nothing suited to a stencil.

A new cost, and a new error

Each residual needs MMM extra network evaluations, so a step costs O(NrM)O(N_rM)O(Nr​M) — a factor of thirty at M=32M=32M=32, the price of non-locality that classical methods also pay.

Worse, the quadrature error has nothing to do with the network and sits inside the loss, where the optimiser can neither see nor fix it. A bad rule caps your accuracy before training begins.

The absence of a dense linear solve is the structural advantage here, and it grows with dimension exactly as it does for PDEs.

Volterra

Moving limits, and a counter-intuitive result

Two PINNs on the same Volterra equation. One evaluates the moving-limit integral directly inside the loss, with its attendant quadrature error. The other differentiates the equation into an equivalent ODE system and solves that, so it has no quadrature error at all. The quadrature-free method is the less accurate of the two — the reformulation trades a controlled quadrature error for a harder optimisation problem, and the optimisation is what dominates.

This is page 2's error decomposition made concrete: removing a small approximation error is no help if it enlarges the optimisation error, and here it does.

Fredholm

The rule inside the loss is not a detail

Gauss–Legendre, trapezoid and Monte-Carlo quadrature for the integral inside the loss, all on M=32M=32M=32 nodes, all else identical. Stochastic nodes make the loss itself a random variable rather than a fixed function of the parameters — the optimiser is then descending a noisy surface, and it costs two orders of magnitude in final accuracy.

The practical rule: use a deterministic, high-order rule with fixed nodes. Resampling quadrature nodes each step is tempting by analogy with resampling collocation points, and it is not the same thing at all.

Near a singular value

The error lies along the null mode

As λ\lambdaλ approaches the singular value −12.549-12.549−12.549, the operator I−λKI-\lambda KI−λK becomes nearly singular and the solution is determined only up to its null mode φ\varphiφ. The middle panel is the argument: the deviation of the trained network from the exact solution lies exactly on top of c φc\,\varphicφ, at every λ\lambdaλ. The PINN is not failing randomly; it is failing in precisely the direction the Fredholm alternative says is undetermined.

A genuinely satisfying result: an ill-conditioned problem produces an error with the structure the theory predicts. It also means the diagnostic is available — project the residual onto the null mode and you can see the problem coming.

Nonlinear, and from physics

Gap equations and self-consistency

The examples so far are linear. The interesting physics is usually not. A gap equation has the self-consistent form

M(p)=m0+∫dq K(p,q) M(q)q2+M2(q),M(p)=m_0+\int dq\,\mathcal{K}\big(p,q\big)\,\frac{M(q)}{q^2+M^2(q)},M(p)=m0​+∫dqK(p,q)q2+M2(q)M(q)​,

where the unknown appears inside the integral, nonlinearly, on both sides. The classical route is fixed-point iteration — guess, substitute, repeat — and hope it converges, which near a phase transition it may not.

A PINN sidesteps the iteration: the self-consistency is the residual, so there is nothing to diverge and no need for the map to be a contraction. Three features make this the natural home for the method — a smooth unknown of one or two variables, no derivatives to ill-condition, and a non-local nonlinear constraint, which is exactly what a residual loss expresses well.

Where these appear

Dyson–Schwinger and Bethe–Salpeter in QCD; BCS and Eliashberg gap equations; Ornstein–Zernike in liquid-state theory; radiative-transfer kernels — all the same shape.

Why this is the good case

Page 4's objections are about differentiating the network and competing with a good classical solver. Here there are no derivatives, and the alternative is an iteration with its own convergence problems.

Still check

Gap equations often have multiple branches — the trivial M≡m0M\equiv m_0M≡m0​ solution among them. Every warning from page 4 about collapse onto trivial solutions applies with full force, and anchoring is essential.

Fold-type problems are the sharpest illustration: two runs with identical losses and different initialisations land on different exact branches, both correct, and only the initialisation chose.

Three practical rules

1. Use a deterministic high-order quadrature with fixed nodes. Monte-Carlo nodes cost two decades.
2. Converge the quadrature before blaming the network — refine MMM and check the answer stops moving.
3. Reformulating an integral equation into a differential one is not automatically an improvement; the Volterra result above goes the other way.
