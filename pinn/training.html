Training it: the craft

Page 2 argued that the error is almost entirely optimisation error. This page is what to do about it — in rough order of how much each is worth, which is not the order in which they are usually tried.

First, always

Non-dimensionalise, then rescale

A network initialised in the usual way produces outputs of order one and expects inputs of order one. If your domain is [0,106][0,10^{6}][0,106] seconds and your solution is of order 10−910^{-9}10−9, training is over before it starts — not because the problem is hard, but because every gradient is either negligible or enormous.

Two separate operations, both needed:

  • Non-dimensionalise the equation. Choose characteristic scales LLL, TTT, UUU and rewrite in x~=x/L\tilde x=x/Lx~=x/L, u~=u/U\tilde u=u/Uu~=u/U so every coefficient is O(1)O(1)O(1). This is physics, done once, on paper.
  • Rescale the network input. Map the domain to [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1] inside the model: t=(x−c)/st=(x-c)/st=(x−c)/s with ccc the centre and sss the half-width. This is bookkeeping, and the derivatives pick up the chain-rule factor 1/s1/s1/s automatically.

The engine behind these pages does the second for you; the first is your job. Both demonstrations here are already O(1)O(1)O(1) problems, which is why they converge at all.

Physics picture

Exactly what you would do before a perturbation expansion, and for the same reason: you want the small parameter to be visibly small and everything else to be order one. A PINN is less forgiving about it than a classical solver, because the optimiser has no pivoting to fall back on.

The commonest wasted week

Someone reports that PINNs "do not work" on their problem. The domain is in SI units, spanning eight orders of magnitude. Nothing else on this page will help until that is fixed.

Also scale the residual

If R=u′′+ω2u\mathcal{R}=u''+\omega^2uR=u′′+ω2u with ω=100\omega=100ω=100, the residual is 10410^4104 times the solution scale and the loss is 10810^8108 times. Adam is scale-invariant so this matters less than it looks — but the relative weighting against other loss terms is then badly off, which matters a lot.

Adam's per-parameter normalisation hides a great deal of bad scaling, which is why people get away with it for a while. It does not hide the imbalance between loss terms.

Constraints

Hard beats soft, almost always

When you cannot

Complicated geometry, or conditions that are themselves unknown. Then use soft constraints, and weight them properly — see the next slides. A middle route is to learn bbb as a separate small network, trained once on the geometry.

But the choice of bbb is not free

Any bbb vanishing on the boundary imposes the condition. They are not equally trainable, because bbb fixes what NθN_\thetaNθ​ has to be. Get it wrong and you have handed the network a badly conditioned target. That is the next slide, and it is the most useful measurement on these pages.

Lagaris, Likas and Fotiadis proposed the hard-constraint form in 1998. It predates the modern literature by twenty years and is still underused.

The general rule

Choose bbb so the function the network must represent is O(1)O(1)O(1) with O(1)O(1)O(1) derivatives across the whole domain. Match the required behaviour at the boundary, then saturate. Polynomial envelopes are fine on short domains and poor on long ones, for the reason in the first table.

Note what did not change

Same architecture, same optimiser, same learning rate, same collocation points, same seed. Only bbb. Fifty-fold. This is what "the error is optimisation error" means in practice.

Choosing a trial function whose variational parameters are all of comparable importance — the same instinct that makes you factor out the known asymptotic behaviour before fitting.

Weighting

Making the terms train at comparable rates

If constraints must be soft, the weights λb,λd\lambda_b,\lambda_dλb​,λd​ decide the answer. Leaving them at one is a choice, and usually a bad one. Four approaches, in increasing order of sophistication:

  • Fixed, tuned by hand. Sweep over powers of ten. Crude, often adequate, and at least you know what you did.
  • Gradient-norm balancing (Wang, Teng & Perdikaris 2021). Set λb∝∣∇θLr∣‾/∣∇θLb∣‾\lambda_b\propto \overline{|\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_r|}\big/\overline{|\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_b|}λb​∝∣∇θ​Lr​∣​/∣∇θ​Lb​∣​, updated every few hundred steps, so no term dominates the update.
  • NTK balancing (Wang, Yu & Perdikaris 2022). Use the traces of the per-term tangent kernels instead of gradient norms, so the terms converge at matched rates rather than matched gradient sizes.
  • Self-adaptive weights (McClenny & Braga-Neto 2023). Give every collocation point its own weight and train the weights by gradient ascent on the loss — a minimax problem that concentrates effort where the residual is stubborn.

The failure this prevents

With λb\lambda_bλb​ too small the network finds a smooth function with a tiny residual that ignores the initial condition — a perfectly good solution of the ODE, just not your solution. The loss looks excellent. This is the single commonest way to be fooled.

Or sidestep it

Every one of these schemes exists to fix a problem that hard constraints do not have. Weight tuning is the price of soft constraints; if you can impose them hard, you never pay it.

Physics picture

Choosing penalty parameters in a constrained minimisation, with the usual dilemma: too small and the constraint is ignored, too large and the problem becomes stiff and the optimiser crawls.

The self-adaptive scheme is the most powerful and the least predictable; start with gradient-norm balancing, which is four lines of code and usually enough.

Sampling, optimisers, architecture

The rest of the toolkit

Collocation points

Uniform grids are the worst choice beyond one dimension. Use Latin hypercube or Sobol sequences, and resample every few hundred steps — free, and it prevents overfitting a fixed set.

Adaptive refinement (RAR, Lu et al. 2021; RAR-D, Wu et al. 2023) adds points where the residual is largest: worth it for fronts and boundary layers, unnecessary for smooth solutions.

Optimiser

Adam to get close, then L-BFGS to finish. The second stage routinely gains one to two decades that Adam will not reach in any reasonable time.

Decay the learning rate; exponential decay from 10−310^{-3}10−3 is a fair default. These demonstrations use Adam alone at a fixed rate, which is why they stop where they do.

Architecture

Smooth activations, because you differentiate twice: tanh⁡\tanhtanh by default. ReLU is useless — its second derivative vanishes almost everywhere.

Depth 3–5, width 20–100 covers most 1D and 2D problems. Fourier features and SIREN help with high-frequency solutions but need frequencies matched to the problem — set badly, they hurt.

Order of attack, when it will not train

1. Check the scaling. 2. Impose the boundary conditions hard, and check the ansatz is well conditioned. 3. Balance the loss weights. 4. Plot the residual across the domain and look at where it is large. 5. Shorten the time interval and march. 6. Only then change the architecture.

Steps 1–3 solve most problems. Step 6 is where most people start and it almost never helps, for the reason given on page 2: the approximation error was never the binding constraint.
