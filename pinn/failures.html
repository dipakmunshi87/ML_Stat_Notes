When it fails

Not "sometimes needs tuning". Fails — confidently, with a falling loss curve and a plausible-looking answer that is wrong by 100%. Every failure here is shown with numbers, most of them measured for these pages.

Failure 1

Collapse onto a trivial solution

The residual loss does not know which solution you want. If the equation admits a solution that is trivially easy to represent — an equilibrium, the zero function, a constant — the optimiser will find it, because it makes the loss genuinely, correctly, extremely small.

The Van der Pol oscillator over four and a half limit cycles is the clean example:

x¨−μ(1−x2)x˙+x=0.\ddot x-\mu(1-x^2)\dot x+x=0 .x¨−μ(1−x2)x˙+x=0.

The equilibrium (x,x˙)=(0,0)(x,\dot x)=(0,0)(x,x˙)=(0,0) satisfies it exactly. A global PINN trained over t∈[0,30]t\in[0,30]t∈[0,30] slides onto it, and the numbers at iteration 62,400 are

Lresidual=2.21×10−7,max⁡[0,30]∥u−uRK45∥=2.83.\mathcal{L}_{\rm residual}=2.21\times10^{-7},\qquad \max_{[0,30]}\|u-u_{\rm RK45}\|=2.83 .Lresidual​=2.21×10−7,[0,30]max​∥u−uRK45​∥=2.83.

Seven orders of magnitude of loss reduction, and an O(1)O(1)O(1) error. The initial condition was imposed softly; it is the one thing the trivial solution cannot satisfy, and it was cheap to violate.

The loss will not warn you

It falls smoothly for sixty thousand iterations. Anyone monitoring convergence by watching the loss reports success. Only a comparison against something external reveals it.

The fix

Impose the initial condition hard: a trivial solution that cannot satisfy u(0)=u0u(0)=u_0u(0)=u0​ leaves the search space entirely rather than being merely penalised. For eigenvalue problems, anchor the normalisation explicitly.

Not unique to ODEs

If you remember one failure mode, make it this one. It is the easiest to fall into, the hardest to detect from training diagnostics, and the most embarrassing to discover late.

Failure 3

Long horizons and propagation failure

A PINN imposes the equation everywhere at once. Nothing in the loss says information flows forward in time: points at t=30t=30t=30 are weighted exactly like points at t=0t=0t=0, and the optimiser may fit the far future before it has the past right. Krishnapriyan et al. (2021) call the result propagation failure — correct near the initial condition, degrading with distance, or a compromise wrong everywhere.

Two remedies, both restoring causality:

  • Time marching. Train on [0,T1][0,T_1][0,T1​], then extend to [0,T2][0,T_2][0,T2​] keeping the weights, and so on. Measured here on the ω=4\omega=4ω=4 oscillator at 18000 steps: one-shot 64.1%, marched in four windows 40.3% — same budget, same architecture.
  • Causal weighting (Wang, Sankaran & Perdikaris 2022). Weight each time by exp⁡(−ϵ∑t′<tL(t′))\exp(-\epsilon\sum_{t'<t}\mathcal{L}(t'))exp(−ϵ∑t′<t​L(t′)), so a point is only taken seriously once everything before it is already satisfied. A continuous version of marching, in one training run.

The honest caveat

Marching improved the oscillator by a factor of 1.6, not a factor of 50. It is a real effect and not a cure; the envelope change on page 3 mattered far more. Report both, and be suspicious of papers that report only the one that worked.

Physics picture

The difference between a boundary-value formulation and an initial-value one. A time-stepper cannot get the future wrong before the past, because it never sees the future. A PINN can, and does.

Where it bites hardest

Chaotic and stiff systems, and anything with a sharp front. The Van der Pol collapse above is a long-horizon failure as much as a trivial-solution one — the two reinforce each other.

Marching has a cost: each window is a fresh optimisation, and errors from early windows are frozen in. It trades one failure mode for a milder one.

Failure 4

Terms that fight each other

With soft constraints, the gradients of the loss terms point in different directions, and early in training the residual gradient is typically orders of magnitude larger. The optimiser does the rational thing: it reduces the big term. The boundary condition drifts.

What makes this dangerous is that the result is a solution of the differential equation — just the wrong one. No internal inconsistency exists for a diagnostic to catch.

Symptoms:

  • loss plateaus at a small non-zero value and stops improving;
  • the solution is the right family of curves, wrong member;
  • the boundary term ends orders of magnitude above the residual term;
  • changing λb\lambda_bλb​ tenfold changes the answer materially — the decisive test, and it costs two training runs.
Diagnosis, in order

1. Print the loss terms separately, every time. An aggregate loss hides everything on this page.
2. Plot the residual against xxx at the end of training — not the loss against step. A residual concentrated in one region names the problem.
3. Evaluate the boundary conditions explicitly and report the violation in physical units.
4. Compare against a classical solver on a reduced problem you can afford to solve both ways.

The meta-failure

Every diagnostic above compares the PINN against something external. There is no purely internal check that a PINN has converged to the right solution. If you have nothing to compare against, you have no way to know — and that is a statement about the method, not about your diligence.

Point 4 is worth insisting on with collaborators: if the problem is worth a PINN, it is worth one reduced case solved classically as a control.

The common root

All four are the same statement from page 1: minimising the loss and solving the equation are different problems. Every remedy on this page works by removing wrong answers from the search space, or by changing which wrong answers are cheap.
