Why it works —
and what is proved

Less than you would hope, and the gap is not where most people expect. The approximation theory is in good shape. It is the optimisation that has no guarantees, and the optimisation is what fails.

Representation

Universal approximation settles almost nothing

The classical results (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991) say that for any continuous uuu on a compact set and any ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0 there is a network with

sup⁡x∈Ω∣u(x)−uθ(x)∣<ε.\sup_{x\in\Omega}|u(x)-u_\theta(x)|<\varepsilon .x∈Ωsup​∣u(x)−uθ​(x)∣<ε.

For PINNs we need more, because the loss contains derivatives: we need a network close to uuu in a Sobolev norm, so that uθ′u_\theta'uθ′​ and uθ′′u_\theta''uθ′′​ are close too. Those results exist as well (Hornik 1991 for derivatives; De Ryck, Lanthaler & Mishra 2021 for tanh⁡\tanhtanh networks specifically), with rates: a tanh⁡\tanhtanh network of width O(ε−d/k)O(\varepsilon^{-d/k})O(ε−d/k) approximates a CkC^kCk function to ε\varepsilonε in W1,∞W^{1,\infty}W1,∞.

So a good network exists. Three things that does not tell you:

  • that gradient descent will find it — the loss is non-convex;
  • that a small residual implies a small error — that needs stability of the PDE;
  • how large "O(ε−d/k)O(\varepsilon^{-d/k})O(ε−d/k)" is in practice — often astronomically large.
Physics picture

Compare completeness of a basis set. Knowing that plane waves are complete tells you a solution can be expanded; it tells you nothing about how many you need, or whether your variational search will find the coefficients. Universal approximation is a completeness theorem, no more.

The step that is often skipped

Small residual ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ small error requires the differential operator to be stable: ∥u−v∥≤C∥R[u]−R[v]∥\|u-v\|\le C\|\mathcal{R}[u]-\mathcal{R}[v]\|∥u−v∥≤C∥R[u]−R[v]∥. For well-posed elliptic and parabolic problems such a bound exists and CCC is computable in principle. For hyperbolic problems over long times, CCC grows, and a tiny residual is compatible with a useless solution.

The stability constant is the whole reason long-time integration fails. Page 4 shows what that looks like in practice on a Van der Pol oscillator.

Error budget

Three errors, and only one is studied

Write θ⋆\theta^\starθ⋆ for the parameters you actually obtain and θ^\hat\thetaθ^ for the best the architecture could do. The total error splits, schematically, as

∥u−uθ⋆∥⏟what you care about ≲ ∥u−uθ^∥⏟approximation+∥uθ^−uθ⋆∥⏟optimisation+Equad⏟finite collocation.\underbrace{\|u-u_{\theta^\star}\|}_{\text{what you care about}}\ \lesssim\ \underbrace{\|u-u_{\hat\theta}\|}_{\text{approximation}} +\underbrace{\|u_{\hat\theta}-u_{\theta^\star}\|}_{\text{optimisation}} +\underbrace{\mathcal{E}_{\rm quad}}_{\text{finite collocation}} .what you care about∥u−uθ⋆​∥​​ ≲ approximation∥u−uθ^​∥​​+optimisation∥uθ^​−uθ⋆​∥​​+finite collocationEquad​​​.
  • Approximation. Controlled by the theorems above. In practice negligible: a few thousand parameters can represent these solutions to far better than you will ever train to.
  • Optimisation. Uncontrolled. Non-convex, and empirically the dominant term by orders of magnitude. There is no theorem that says Adam gets near θ^\hat\thetaθ^.
  • Quadrature. You minimise an average over NNN points, not an integral. Bounded by Equad∼N−1/2\mathcal{E}_{\rm quad}\sim N^{-1/2}Equad​∼N−1/2 for random sampling, better for quasi-random.

Mishra & Molinaro (2022) make this rigorous: the generalisation error is bounded by the training residual plus a quadrature term, times the PDE's stability constant. Every ingredient is computable except the one that matters — how small the training residual will actually get.

Measured here

The oscillator demonstration uses 481 parameters. At ω=2\omega=2ω=2 it reaches 0.06%; the same network can represent that solution to roughly 10−710^{-7}10−7. Essentially the entire error is optimisation.

Why this matters practically

If your error is dominated by optimisation, making the network bigger is the wrong move — it enlarges the search space without improving the reachable optimum. Fix conditioning, weighting and the ansatz instead. Page 3 gives a fifty-fold improvement from exactly that.

Physics picture

Like a variational calculation where the trial family is ample but the minimiser gets stuck: the remedy is a better-conditioned parameterisation, not more parameters.

This decomposition is the most useful thing on the page. Before tuning anything, ask which of the three terms you are fighting; the answer is almost always the second.

The theorems

What the convergence results actually promise

Consistency. Shin, Darbon & Karniadakis (2020) showed that for linear elliptic and parabolic problems, if the training loss tends to zero as the number of collocation points grows, the network converges to the solution. A statement about minimisers, not about training.

Error bounds. Mishra & Molinaro (2022, 2023) bound the generalisation error by the training error plus a quadrature term, with a constant from the PDE's stability. Usable a posteriori: train, measure the residual, get a bound. Says nothing in advance.

Rates. De Ryck & Mishra (2022) give explicit sizes for Navier–Stokes and related problems showing the curse of dimensionality can be avoided for some function classes.

Every one of these is conditional on the training loss being small. None of them tells you it will be.

The honest summary

PINN theory is a posteriori. It converts a small measured residual into an error bound. That is genuinely useful — it means a converged PINN can be certified. It is not a guarantee that you will converge, and in the cases where PINNs disappoint, the training loss is not small.

Use it

The practical consequence: always report the residual across the domain, not the loss history. A residual that is uniformly small is evidence; a loss that has plateaued is not. Page 3 shows how to plot it.

The comparison to draw

A finite-element code comes with a priori convergence: halve hhh, know what happens to the error, before running anything. Nothing in the PINN literature offers that, and it is the single largest reason the two are not interchangeable.

Worth stating plainly to collaborators who expect solver-like guarantees: you can certify a PINN after the fact, but you cannot promise one in advance.

Why training stalls

The tangent kernel, and slow modes

For a wide network trained by gradient descent, the function evolves almost linearly in a fixed kernel. With residuals ri=R[uθ](xi)r_i=\mathcal{R}[u_\theta](x_i)ri​=R[uθ​](xi​), gradient flow gives

d rdt=− K r,Kij=⟨∇θR[uθ](xi), ∇θR[uθ](xj)⟩,\frac{d\,\mathbf r}{dt}=-\,\mathbf K\,\mathbf r,\qquad K_{ij}=\big\langle \nabla_\theta \mathcal{R}[u_\theta](x_i),\,\nabla_\theta \mathcal{R}[u_\theta](x_j)\big\rangle,dtdr​=−Kr,Kij​=⟨∇θ​R[uθ​](xi​),∇θ​R[uθ​](xj​)⟩,

so in the eigenbasis of K\mathbf KK each component decays as e−λkte^{-\lambda_k t}e−λk​t. Training speed is set by the eigenvalues, and they are wildly unequal. Two consequences, both visible in the demonstrations:

  • Spectral bias. Rapidly oscillating eigenvectors have small λ\lambdaλ, so high-frequency structure is learned exponentially more slowly.
  • Term imbalance. Residual and boundary terms have their own kernels at very different scales; if λr≫λb\lambda_r\gg\lambda_bλr​≫λb​ the optimiser works on the residual and drifts off the boundary condition. Wang, Yu & Perdikaris (2022) set λ\lambdaλ from the kernel traces to fix exactly this.
Physics picture

A stiff relaxation problem. The kernel is a rate matrix; λmax⁡/λmin⁡\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}λmax​/λmin​ sets the wait for the slowest mode. Everything you know about stiff systems applies — including that preconditioning beats brute force.

Seen on these pages

At ω=4\omega=4ω=4 the oscillator sits near 50% error for thousands of steps, then drops to 1.4%. A slow mode finally relaxing — not a bug, and not something more layers would have fixed.

The NTK picture is a wide-network idealisation and PINNs are not always wide. It is still the most useful mental model available for why training behaves as it does.

Summary

Four things to carry forward

QuestionAnswer
Can a network represent the solution?Yes, with rates, in Sobolev norms
Will training find it?No theorem says so
Does a small residual mean a small error?Yes — times the stability constant
Which error term dominates?Optimisation, almost always
Do bigger networks help?Rarely; better conditioning does
Can a converged PINN be certified?Yes, a posteriori, from the residual
The one-line version

The theory says a good network exists and that if you find it you can prove it is good. It is silent on the part that actually fails.
