The asymmetry

Why this direction is different

The forward problem — all coefficients known, find uuu — has excellent classical methods, and a PINN loses to finite differences by five orders of magnitude.

The inverse problem is not like that. Given sparse noisy observations ukobsu^{\rm obs}_kukobs​ and unknown coefficients μ\muμ, the classical approach is nested: guess μ\muμ, solve the forward problem completely, compare, adjust, repeat. Every step costs a full solve, and the gradient ∂u/∂μ\partial u/\partial\mu∂u/∂μ needs an adjoint — derived and implemented afresh for each new equation.

A PINN removes the nesting. Make μ\muμ a trainable parameter in the same optimiser:

L(θ,μ)=λrNr∑i∣Rμ[uθ](xi)∣2+λdNd∑k∣uθ(xk)−ukobs∣2.\mathcal{L}(\theta,\mu)=\frac{\lambda_r}{N_r}\sum_i\big|\mathcal{R}_\mu[u_\theta](x_i)\big|^2 +\frac{\lambda_d}{N_d}\sum_k\big|u_\theta(x_k)-u^{\rm obs}_k\big|^2 .L(θ,μ)=Nr​λr​​i∑​​Rμ​[uθ​](xi​)​2+Nd​λd​​k∑​​uθ​(xk​)−ukobs​​2.

One optimisation, not two nested ones. The solution and the constant are found together, each constraining the other, and ∂L/∂μ\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mu∂L/∂μ comes from the same automatic differentiation that already produced ∂L/∂θ\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta∂L/∂θ. No adjoint to derive.

The four structural advantages

No adjoint. The hardest part of classical inverse problems disappears.
No mesh. Data at arbitrary scattered locations is natural, not an interpolation problem.
Gaps are free. The physics term fills regions with no data at all.
Noise is regularised. The residual term stops the solution chasing noisy points.

Physics picture

A fit in which the model is the differential equation rather than a functional form. You are not fitting a curve and inferring a constant from it; you are asking which constant makes a physically admissible curve pass near the data.

The absence of an adjoint is the practical selling point. Anyone who has hand-derived adjoint equations for a nonlinear system knows what is being saved.

Setting it up

One scalar, one extra term

The demonstration on the next slide solves this problem. A damped oscillator is observed at 24 points with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.04, and the damping ratio ζ\zetaζ is unknown:

u′′+2ζωu′+ω2u=0,u(0)=1, u′(0)=0,ζ unknown.u''+2\zeta\omega u'+\omega^2u=0,\qquad u(0)=1,\ u'(0)=0,\qquad \zeta\ \text{unknown}.u′′+2ζωu′+ω2u=0,u(0)=1, u′(0)=0,ζ unknown.

The setup:

  • ζ\zetaζ becomes a single trainable parameter, initialised at 000 — deliberately far from the truth, and at the edge of the physical range.
  • The initial conditions are imposed hard, as always.
  • The data term is weighted 20 times the residual term, because 24 points must compete with 128 collocation points.
  • Everything else is unchanged from page 1: same architecture, same optimiser, same engine.

Measured result: ζ=0.2538\zeta=0.2538ζ=0.2538 against a true value of 0.25000.25000.2500, from 24 points at 4% noise. The estimate is within 1.5% of the truth, and the recovered solution passes through the data without chasing it.

What this number is not

It is a point estimate with no uncertainty attached. The optimiser returns one value of ζ\zetaζ and says nothing about how well determined it is. That is the subject of slide 5, and it is the main thing standing between this method and a defensible measurement.

Initialising ζ\zetaζ at zero is deliberate: starting it near the answer would make the demonstration much less interesting and much less honest.

The hard part

Identifiability and error bars

The optimiser will always return a number. Two questions it does not answer:

Is the parameter identifiable? Different μ\muμ can give solutions your data cannot distinguish. Here ζ\zetaζ and ω\omegaω separate only because the record spans several periods; over a quarter period they trade off almost exactly and the optimiser returns a confident wrong pair. The diagnostic is the curvature of the loss in μ\muμ — flat means unidentifiable, and scanning is cheap.

What is the uncertainty? A point estimate is not a measurement. Three routes:

  • Profile the loss. Fix μ\muμ on a grid, retrain, plot L(μ)\mathcal{L}(\mu)L(μ); the width where it rises by the noise level is an interval.
  • Bootstrap the data. Resample, retrain, take the spread of μ^\hat\muμ^​.
  • Bayesian PINNs (Yang, Meng & Karniadakis 2021). Priors on θ\thetaθ and μ\muμ, posterior by HMC — a genuine posterior, at one to two orders of magnitude more cost.
The mistake to avoid

Quoting ζ=0.2538\zeta=0.2538ζ=0.2538 without an interval. The number would look identical whether the data constrained ζ\zetaζ to ±0.001\pm0.001±0.001 or ±0.2\pm0.2±0.2. Profiling costs a handful of runs and is the difference between a demonstration and a measurement.

Optimiser variability is real

Retrain with a different seed and μ^\hat\muμ^​ moves. That spread is part of your error budget and has no counterpart in classical inverse methods — report across several seeds.

This slide is the one most often skipped in the literature, and the one a referee will ask about first.

In the wild

Where this is actually used

Flow from images

Hidden fluid mechanics (Raissi, Yazdani & Karniadakis, Science 367, 1026, 2020): recover velocity and pressure fields from images of a passive dye, by imposing Navier–Stokes on the unobserved quantities. Pressure is never measured and never could be — it is inferred entirely from the physics term.

Material and medium properties

Elastic moduli from displacement fields, thermal conductivity from temperature measurements, permeability in porous flow, reaction rates in kinetics. The common shape: a field you can measure sparsely, a constant you cannot measure at all, and an equation linking them.

Instrument and interference models

Signal-extraction problems where a physical model of the contaminant exists but its parameters do not: orbits, beam shapes, emission spectra inferred jointly with the astrophysical signal. The data are sparse and irregular, which is the regime that suits collocation.

The pattern worth recognising

PINNs win when the forward solve is expensive, the data are sparse and scattered, part of the state is unobservable, and an adjoint would be painful to derive. They lose when a good classical solver exists and you simply want the solution. Most problems are the second kind — which is why the honest answer to "should I use a PINN?" is usually no, and why the cases where it is yes are worth recognising.

The hidden-fluid-mechanics result is the strongest single argument for the method: a quantity that is not measurable at all, recovered because the equation says what it must be.

Checklist before quoting a recovered parameter

1. Scan the loss in μ\muμ — is the minimum sharp?
2. Retrain with three seeds — how much does μ^\hat\muμ^​ move?
3. Profile or bootstrap — what is the interval?
4. Inject a known μ\muμ into synthetic data with your real noise and cadence — do you get it back?
5. Only then, quote the number, with the interval and the method beside it.
