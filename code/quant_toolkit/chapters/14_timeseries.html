Part III Statistics for quantitative research and trading

14Time series: stationarity, unit roots, cointegration and filtering

Prices wander; returns do not. Keeping that distinction straight is the difference between a statistical arbitrage and a spurious regression, and the tools of this chapter — autocorrelation, unit-root tests, cointegration, the Kalman filter — are how one tells which is which.

14.1 Stationarity and the autocorrelation function

A process is (weakly) stationary if its mean and variance are constant in time and its autocovariance $\gamma(k) = \mathrm{Cov}(x_t, x_{t-k})$ depends only on the lag $k$. The autocorrelation function (ACF) is $\rho(k) = \gamma(k)/\gamma(0)$. For independent returns $\rho(k) = 0$ for all $k > 0$, and the sample ACF of $T$ such observations has standard error $1/\sqrt T$: with 1,000 days, any $|\hat\rho(k)| < 0.06$ is noise. The workhorse stationary model is the autoregression of order one, $$x_t = \phi\,x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t,\qquad |\phi| < 1,$$ whose variance is $\sigma_\varepsilon^2/(1-\phi^2)$ and whose ACF decays geometrically, $\rho(k) = \phi^k$. It is the discrete-time Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process of Chapter 8 with $\phi = e^{-\kappa\Delta t}$; the half-life of a shock is $\ln 2/\ln(1/\phi)$. Moving-average terms ($x_t = \varepsilon_t + \theta\varepsilon_{t-1}$, with an ACF that cuts off after lag 1) and their combination into ARMA models complete the Box–Jenkins toolkit; in finance the AR(1) and its multivariate cousin the VAR do most of the work.

Worked example: AR(1) against a random walk

1,000 observations of an AR(1) with $\phi = 0.7$ have sample ACF ({{v:c14_acf_ar}}) at lags 1–4 against the theoretical ({{v:c14_acf_theory}}); the sample variance is {{v:c14_ar_var_sim}} against $1/(1-0.49) = {{v:c14_ar_var}}$. OLS of $x_t$ on $x_{t-1}$ gives $\hat\phi = {{v:c14_phi_hat}} \pm {{v:c14_phi_se}}$. A random walk of the same length has sample ACF ({{v:c14_acf_rw}}) at lags 1–4 and still {{v:c14_acf_rw20}} at lag 20: the autocorrelation does not decay, because the series has no mean to return to.

14.2 Unit roots and the Dickey–Fuller test

At $\phi = 1$ the AR(1) becomes the random walk $x_t = x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$: a unit root. Its variance grows as $\sigma_\varepsilon^2 t$, it has no stationary distribution, and shocks are permanent. Log prices are, to a first approximation, unit-root processes; returns, their first differences, are stationary. The test for a unit root regresses $\Delta x_t$ on $x_{t-1}$ and examines the $t$-statistic of the coefficient (equivalently $(\hat\phi - 1)/\mathrm{se}$). Under the null $\phi = 1$ that statistic does not have a Student-$t$ distribution — the regressor is itself non-stationary — but the Dickey–Fuller distribution, whose 5% critical value is about $-2.9$ (with an intercept) rather than $-1.96$. For the AR(1) sample the statistic is {{v:c14_df_ar}}, rejecting a unit root overwhelmingly; for the random walk it is {{v:c14_df_rw}} (with $\hat\phi = {{v:c14_rw_phi}}$), and the null stands. The asymmetry matters: an estimated $\hat\phi$ of 0.98 on a short sample is consistent with both a slowly mean-reverting series and a random walk, and the test has little power to separate them. Many "mean-reverting" strategies are bets on $\phi < 1$ that the data cannot actually support.

14.3 Spurious regression

Regress one random walk on another, independent one. Granger and Newbold (1974) showed that the result is a high $R^2$ and an enormous $t$-statistic, both meaningless: two independent random walks regressed on each other in our simulation give $t = {{v:c14_spur_t}}$ and $R^2 = {{v:c14_spur_R2}}$. The reason is that the residual is itself a random walk, so the OLS standard errors (which assume stationary residuals) are wrong by an unbounded factor. Any regression between price levels, index levels, cumulative anything, is suspect unless the residual is checked for stationarity. The rule is to difference first — regress returns on returns — except in the one case where levels are legitimately related, which is the next section.

14.4 Cointegration and pairs trading

Two unit-root series $y_t$ and $z_t$ are cointegrated if some linear combination $y_t - \beta z_t$ is stationary (Engle and Granger, 1987). Each wanders, but they wander together: two share classes of one company, a stock and its ADR, a futures contract and its underlying, or — the hope of every pairs trader — two companies in the same business. The Engle–Granger procedure regresses $y$ on $z$ in levels (legitimate here precisely because the residual is stationary), then tests the residual for a unit root with a Dickey–Fuller test at stricter critical values (about $-3.4$ at 5%, since $\beta$ was estimated). If the spread is stationary it is modelled as an AR(1)/OU process and traded: short the spread when it is high, long when it is low, close when it returns to its mean.

Worked example: a simulated cointegrated pair

We generate two prices sharing a common random walk, with a log-spread following an AR(1) of daily mean-reversion 5% (half-life {{v:c14_sp_halflife_true}} days). The true hedge ratio is 1; the levels regression estimates {{v:c14_hedge}}. The OLS estimate in levels is super-consistent (it converges at rate $T$ rather than $\sqrt T$) but in a finite sample it is biased towards zero whenever the common trend has not wandered far relative to the spread's own variance — which is exactly the regime of a short backtest. The estimated spread has $\hat\phi = {{v:c14_sp_phi}}$, i.e. a half-life of {{v:c14_sp_halflife}} days, and an Engle–Granger statistic of {{v:c14_eg_t}}: cointegration is confirmed. A simple rule — enter at $\pm1.5\sigma$, exit at $\pm0.25\sigma$, with the bands estimated on the first half and traded on the second — produces {{v:c14_pairs_trades}} round trips and an annualised Sharpe of {{v:c14_pairs_sharpe}} ({{v:c14_pairs_sharpe_cost}} after 5 bp costs per trade). This is a best case: the pair is cointegrated by construction, forever. Real pairs break (one company changes its business, is acquired, or fails), and the spread then becomes a random walk at exactly the moment the strategy is most heavily invested in it. The risk of a pairs trade is not the volatility of the spread but the probability that the relationship ends.

14.5 The Kalman filter: hedge ratios that move

Hedge ratios, betas and spreads drift. The Kalman filter is the optimal (minimum-variance, linear) way to track a quantity that evolves as a random walk and is observed through noisy linear measurements. With state $\beta_t = \beta_{t-1} + \eta_t$ (process noise $Q$) and observation $y_t = \beta_t x_t + \epsilon_t$ (measurement noise $R$), each step first predicts ($\hat\beta_{t|t-1} = \hat\beta_{t-1}$, $P_{t|t-1} = P_{t-1} + Q$) and then updates with the gain $K_t = P_{t|t-1}x_t/(x_t^2P_{t|t-1} + R)$: $$\hat\beta_t = \hat\beta_{t|t-1} + K_t\big(y_t - \hat\beta_{t|t-1}x_t\big),\qquad P_t = (1 - K_tx_t)\,P_{t|t-1}.$$ It is Bayesian updating with Gaussian prior and likelihood (Chapter 3) run recursively, and the gain is the precision-weighting of new data against the accumulated estimate. The ratio $Q/R$ sets the memory: large $Q$ means a fast, noisy tracker; small $Q$ means a slow, smooth one. Tracking a beta that oscillates between 0.5 and 1.5 over 1,000 days, the filter achieves an RMS error of {{v:c14_kf_rmse}} against {{v:c14_roll_rmse}} for a 60-day rolling regression, and it does so with no window to choose, with a running estimate of its own uncertainty $P_t$, and with one multiply–add per observation. The same recursion with a vector state tracks a whole set of factor exposures, a yield curve's level/slope/curvature, or the unobserved "true" price behind bid–ask bounce. Its nonlinear extensions (the extended and unscented filters, and particle filters for non-Gaussian problems) are what a physicist knows from signal processing and data assimilation.

14.6 Vector autoregressions and Granger causality

With several series the AR(1) becomes the VAR, $\mathbf x_t = \mathbf A\mathbf x_{t-1} + \boldsymbol\varepsilon_t$, which is estimated equation by equation with OLS. Its uses in finance are lead–lag analysis (does one market's return predict another's next period? the off-diagonal entries of $\mathbf A$), impulse responses, and the error-correction form $\Delta\mathbf x_t = \boldsymbol\Pi\mathbf x_{t-1} + \dots$ in which the rank of $\boldsymbol\Pi$ counts the cointegrating relationships (Johansen's test). "Granger causality" — $z$ Granger-causes $y$ if lagged $z$ improves the forecast of $y$ given lagged $y$ — is a statement about predictability, not causation, and in liquid markets lead–lag relationships survive for minutes, not days, because the people who find them trade them away.

What to carry forward

Stationarity
Returns yes, prices no. ACF of an AR(1) decays as $\phi^k$; of a random walk, not at all.
Unit roots
Dickey–Fuller critical values, not $t$; short samples cannot distinguish $\phi = 0.98$ from 1.
Cointegration
Stationary combination of non-stationary series; the one legitimate levels regression; pairs break.
Kalman filter
Recursive Bayesian tracking; precision-weighted gain; the right tool for drifting betas.
