0Overview: what this note is, and how to read it

The finance industry runs on a surprisingly compact body of mathematics. This note builds all of it, from first principles, in the order in which each idea becomes necessary.

Ask a quant what they actually use and the honest list is short: discounting and compounding; probability with heavy tails; estimation theory and the brutal arithmetic of signal-to-noise; linear algebra, above all the covariance matrix and its eigenvectors; calculus, ordinary differential equations and one partial differential equation (the heat equation, which turns out to be Black–Scholes in disguise); stochastic calculus; numerical methods; time-series statistics; optimisation under constraints; and a set of ideas imported wholesale from physics — diffusion, Langevin dynamics, the Fokker–Planck equation, path integrals, random matrices, entropy and scaling. Everything else is these tools, applied with judgement to a particular desk's problem.

The note is organised as a single continuous argument in four parts. Part I (Chapters 1–6) is the mathematical foundation: the time value of money, probability with fat tails, statistical estimation, linear algebra, calculus and the heat equation, and regression. Part II (Chapters 7–13) is the stochastic world of derivatives: random walks, Brownian motion and Itô's lemma, the Black–Scholes equation and its solution, Greeks and hedging, volatility smiles and the models that produce them, interest-rate models, and the numerical methods (trees, finite differences, Monte Carlo) used to price what has no closed form. Part III (Chapters 14–20) is the statistics of quantitative research and trading: time series and cointegration, volatility models, random matrix theory and covariance cleaning, portfolio theory and bet sizing, risk measures, machine learning for signals, and the multiple-testing problem that makes most backtests lie. Part IV (Chapters 21–22) steps back: it collects the physics that underlies the whole subject, and then compresses the entire note onto one page. An appendix gives the code that produced every number and figure.

How the worked examples work

Every number quoted in the text — a bond price, a Sharpe-ratio standard error, an option price, a Monte Carlo estimate — is computed by the code in the appendix with fixed random seeds, and the figures are produced by the same code. Nothing is drawn from live market data; the point is the mathematics and the mechanism, which do not change from year to year, rather than any particular market's numbers, which do. Where a real-world magnitude matters (how fat equity tails are, how long it takes to establish that a strategy works) the examples use realistic parameters and say so.

Notation and conventions

Prices are $S_t$, interest rates $r$, volatilities $\sigma$, time $t$ measured in years. Returns are log-returns $x_t = \ln(S_t/S_{t-1})$ unless stated otherwise. A standard normal variable is $Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$, with density $\varphi(z)$ and distribution function $\Phi(z)$. Vectors are bold lower case ($\mathbf w$ for portfolio weights), matrices bold upper case ($\boldsymbol\Sigma$ for a covariance matrix). Expectations are $\mathbb E[\cdot]$; under the risk-neutral measure, which we meet in Chapter 9, they are $\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[\cdot]$. A "basis point" (bp) is 0.01%. Trading years have 250 days (some desks use 252; it changes nothing that matters). $\mathrm d W_t$ is the increment of a Brownian motion, and the single most important identity in the note is $(\mathrm d W_t)^2 = \mathrm d t$.

A reader with an undergraduate physics or mathematics background has everything required. A reader who knows the derivatives material can start at Part III; a reader who wants the physics perspective first can start at Chapter 21 and work backwards. Chapter 22 is a one-page summary of everything and is worth reading twice: once now, to see the shape of the subject, and once at the end.
