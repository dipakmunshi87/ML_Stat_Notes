12Interest-rate models: Vasicek, CIR and the term structure

Rates are harder than stocks: the underlying is a whole curve, the rate is both the thing being modelled and the thing used to discount, and bonds have a fixed maturity. The affine models solve all three problems with one trick — the PDE collapses to ODEs.

12.1 The short rate and the bond-pricing equation

Model the instantaneous rate $r_t$ as a diffusion, $\mathrm dr = a(r,t)\,\mathrm dt + b(r,t)\,\mathrm dW$ under the risk-neutral measure. A zero-coupon bond paying 1 at $T$ is worth $$P(t,T) = \mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}\Big[\exp\Big(-\int_t^T r_s\,\mathrm ds\Big)\,\Big|\,r_t\Big],$$ the expected discount factor along random rate paths. By Feynman–Kac this $P(r,t)$ satisfies the backward equation with the discounting term, $$\frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + a\frac{\partial P}{\partial r} + \tfrac12 b^2\frac{\partial^2P}{\partial r^2} - rP = 0,\qquad P(r,T) = 1 ,$$ which is the Black–Scholes equation with $r$ as the state variable and the subtlety that $r$ appears both as the drift's argument and in the $-rP$ term. The yield is $y(t,T) = -\ln P/(T-t)$; the model's whole term structure, for every maturity, follows from the current $r_t$ and the parameters.

12.2 Vasicek: the OU short rate

Vasicek (1977) took the OU process, $\mathrm dr = \kappa(\theta - r)\,\mathrm dt + \sigma\,\mathrm dW$. Try the affine ansatz $P = e^{A(\tau) - B(\tau)r}$ with $\tau = T - t$. Substituting into the PDE and collecting the terms proportional to $r$ and the terms free of $r$ gives two ODEs, $$B' = 1 - \kappa B,\qquad A' = -\kappa\theta B + \tfrac12\sigma^2B^2,\qquad A(0) = B(0) = 0,$$ with solutions $$B(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\kappa\tau}}{\kappa},\qquad A(\tau) = \Big(\theta - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa^2}\Big)\big(B(\tau) - \tau\big) - \frac{\sigma^2B(\tau)^2}{4\kappa}.$$ $B(\tau)$ is the bond's sensitivity to the short rate — its duration with respect to $r$ — and it saturates at $1/\kappa$ instead of growing like $\tau$: because the rate mean-reverts, a 30-year bond is barely more exposed to today's short rate than a 10-year one. (For $\kappa = 0.3$, $B(5) = {{v:c12_B5}}$ and $B(10) = {{v:c12_B10}}$ against naive durations of 5 and 10.) The long-run yield is $y_\infty = \theta - \sigma^2/2\kappa^2$, which for the example parameters is {{v:c12_yinf}}%: slightly below $\theta$, because Jensen's inequality (the convexity of $e^{-\int r}$) makes random rates worth more than certain ones.

Worked example: a 5-year bond under Vasicek

With $\kappa = 0.3$, $\theta = 5\%$, $\sigma = 1\%$ and $r_0 = 5\%$ the 5-year discount factor is $P = {{v:c12_P5_5}}$ against the naive $e^{-0.05\times5} = {{v:c12_P5_5_naive}}$; the small excess is the convexity value. A Monte Carlo with 50,000 rate paths gives {{v:c12_P5_5_mc}}. The 10-year yield is {{v:c12_y10_2}}%, {{v:c12_y10_5}}% and {{v:c12_y10_8}}% for $r_0 = 2\%, 5\%, 8\%$: starting below $\theta$ the curve is upward-sloping, starting above it is inverted, and in all cases it converges towards $y_\infty$. 12.3 Cox–Ingersoll–Ross and the square root

CIR (1985) replaced $\sigma\,\mathrm dW$ by $\sigma\sqrt r\,\mathrm dW$. The volatility of the rate vanishes as $r\to0$, so with $2\kappa\theta \ge \sigma^2$ (the Feller condition again; it holds here) the rate never reaches zero. The model stays affine — the ansatz $e^{A - Br}$ still works, with a Riccati equation $B' = 1 - \kappa B - \tfrac12\sigma^2B^2$ that is solvable in closed form — and the rate's distribution becomes non-central chi-squared rather than Gaussian. The same square-root process is the variance process in Heston, which is why the Heston formula looks like a bond price: it is the same Riccati machinery. The general statement (Duffie–Kan 1996) is that any model with affine drift and affine squared diffusion has exponential-affine bond prices and a closed-form characteristic function; the affine family is the set of models in which the PDE becomes ODEs, and essentially every tractable model in rates, credit and volatility belongs to it.

12.4 Fitting the curve: Hull–White and HJM

One-factor models with constant parameters cannot reproduce an arbitrary initial curve, which a desk must do exactly (the curve is observable, and mispricing a bond against it is arbitrage). Hull–White makes $\theta(t)$ time-dependent and chooses it to match today's forwards; the model remains Gaussian and affine. Heath–Jarrow–Morton go further and model the whole forward curve $f(t,T)$ directly as a field, $\mathrm df(t,T) = \alpha(t,T)\,\mathrm dt + \sigma(t,T)\,\mathrm dW$, with the no-arbitrage condition fixing the drift completely in terms of the volatility: $\alpha(t,T) = \sigma(t,T)\int_t^T\sigma(t,u)\,\mathrm du$. In HJM there is nothing to calibrate but the volatility structure, and the market-standard LIBOR/SOFR market models are HJM written in terms of discretely compounded forward rates so that caps and swaptions have Black-formula prices. Multi-factor versions (two or three Brownian motions driving level, slope and curvature — the PCA of Chapter 4 applied to yield changes, where three components explain over 95% of the variance) are the production tools.

12.5 Interest-rate derivatives in one paragraph

A swap exchanges fixed for floating payments and is worth zero at inception when the fixed rate equals the par swap rate, a weighted average of forwards. A cap is a strip of call options on the floating rate; a swaption is an option to enter a swap. In a Gaussian (Vasicek/Hull–White) model all of these have closed forms via Jamshidian's trick, which decomposes an option on a coupon bond into options on zeros. Under the market model each caplet is priced by Black's formula with a lognormal forward rate. The pricing is standard; the risk is in the curve-building and in the correlation between forwards, which is where multi-factor models earn their keep.

12.6 Credit as a rate: hazard rates and credit default swaps

The same machinery prices default risk. Model default as the first jump of a Poisson process with intensity (hazard rate) $h_t$: the probability of surviving to $T$ is $Q(T) = \mathbb E\big[\exp(-\int_0^T h_s\,\mathrm ds)\big]$, which is the bond-pricing formula with $h$ in place of $r$. A defaultable zero-coupon bond with zero recovery is then worth $P(T)\,Q(T) = \mathbb E[e^{-\int(r_s + h_s)\mathrm ds}]$: the hazard rate is a spread added to the discount rate, and all the affine technology of Section 12.3 applies to it — a CIR hazard rate gives closed-form survival probabilities. With recovery $R$ paid at default, the price acquires a second term, and the credit spread over the risk-free yield is approximately $h(1-R)$: a 2% annual default probability with 40% recovery is a spread of about 120 bp. A credit default swap is the insurance contract: the buyer pays a running premium $s$ until default or maturity and receives $(1-R)$ at default. Its fair premium equates the present values of the two legs, $$s\sum_i\Delta_i\,P(T_i)\,Q(T_i) = (1-R)\int_0^T P(t)\,\big(-\mathrm dQ(t)\big),$$ and the "credit triangle" $s\approx h(1-R)$ is the flat-hazard approximation. Inverting quoted CDS spreads for a term structure of hazard rates is bootstrapping a curve, exactly as for discount factors, and portfolios of credits are priced with correlated default times — which is where the Gaussian copula of Chapter 2 entered, and failed. The lesson of this section is structural: once one has seen that a bond is $\mathbb E[e^{-\int r}]$, a defaultable bond, a survival probability, a CDS and (with $h$ replaced by a dividend yield or a convenience yield) a commodity forward are all the same formula with different names for the exponent.

What to carry forward

Bond PDE
Black–Scholes in $r$ with a $-rP$ term; $P = \mathbb E^{\mathbb Q}[e^{-\int r}]$.
Affine ansatz
$P = e^{A(\tau) - B(\tau)r}$ turns the PDE into Riccati ODEs; $B$ saturates at $1/\kappa$.
Convexity
Random rates raise bond prices (Jensen); $y_\infty = \theta - \sigma^2/2\kappa^2$.
HJM
Model the forward curve; no arbitrage fixes the drift from the volatility.
