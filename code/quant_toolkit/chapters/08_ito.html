8Itô calculus: SDEs, Itô's lemma and the Fokker–Planck equation

Stochastic calculus is ordinary calculus with one extra rule, $(\mathrm dW)^2 = \mathrm dt$. That rule produces a correction term in the chain rule, and that correction term is responsible for volatility drag, for the $\sigma^2/2$ in Black–Scholes, and for the difference between the mean and the median of every asset's future price.

8.1 Stochastic differential equations

A diffusion process is written $$\mathrm dX_t = a(X_t, t)\,\mathrm dt + b(X_t, t)\,\mathrm dW_t ,$$ meaning: over a short interval the change in $X$ is a deterministic drift $a\,\mathrm dt$ plus a random shock of standard deviation $b\sqrt{\mathrm dt}$. The equation is shorthand for the integral $X_t = X_0 + \int_0^t a\,\mathrm ds + \int_0^t b\,\mathrm dW_s$, where the second integral is the Itô integral — a limit of sums $\sum b(X_{t_i})\,(W_{t_{i+1}} - W_{t_i})$ in which the integrand is evaluated at the start of each interval. That choice (as opposed to the midpoint, which gives the Stratonovich integral used in physics) is the right one for finance because a trading decision at $t_i$ cannot use information from $t_{i+1}$: the Itô integral is the gain of a non-anticipating trading strategy, and it has the key property $\mathbb E\big[\int b\,\mathrm dW\big] = 0$ (it is a martingale). The three SDEs that matter most are:

8.2 Itô's lemma

Let $f(x,t)$ be smooth and $X_t$ a diffusion. Taylor-expand $f$ to second order, as in Chapter 5: $$\mathrm df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\mathrm dt + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\mathrm dX + \tfrac12\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(\mathrm dX)^2 + \dots$$ Now use the multiplication table. $(\mathrm dX)^2 = (a\,\mathrm dt + b\,\mathrm dW)^2 = b^2(\mathrm dW)^2 + O(\mathrm dt^{3/2}) = b^2\,\mathrm dt$. The second-order term is of order $\mathrm dt$ — the same order as the drift — and must be kept, while all higher terms vanish. The result is Itô's lemma: $$\boxed{\;\mathrm df = \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + a\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \tfrac12 b^2\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\Big)\mathrm dt + b\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\mathrm dW\;}$$ It is the chain rule plus an extra drift $\tfrac12 b^2 f''$, the Itô correction, which is positive for convex $f$: a convex function of a noisy variable drifts upwards on average even if the variable itself does not (Jensen's inequality in differential form). Everything that distinguishes stochastic from ordinary calculus is in that term.

8.3 Solving GBM: the volatility drag

Apply the lemma to $f = \ln S$ with $\mathrm dS = \mu S\,\mathrm dt + \sigma S\,\mathrm dW$ (so $a = \mu S$, $b = \sigma S$, $f' = 1/S$, $f'' = -1/S^2$): $$\mathrm d\ln S = \Big(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\Big)\mathrm dt + \sigma\,\mathrm dW .$$ The log-price is an arithmetic Brownian motion with drift $\mu - \sigma^2/2$, so $$S_T = S_0\exp\!\Big[\big(\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2\big)T + \sigma W_T\Big],\qquad \ln S_T \sim \mathcal N\!\Big(\ln S_0 + (\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2)T,\ \sigma^2T\Big).$$ The price is lognormal. Its mean is $\mathbb E[S_T] = S_0e^{\mu T}$ (the $-\sigma^2/2$ in the exponent is exactly cancelled by the $+\sigma^2/2$ from the lognormal mean formula of Chapter 2) but its median is $S_0e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)T}$. The gap $\sigma^2/2$ is the volatility drag: a typical path grows more slowly than the average path, because the average is pulled up by a few very large outcomes. For $\mu = 10\%$, $\sigma = 20\%$, $T = 1$ the drag is {{v:c8_drag}}% a year, the mean of $S_T$ is {{v:c8_ES}} and the median {{v:c8_median}}; simulating $10^5$ paths gives {{v:c8_ES_sim}} and {{v:c8_median_sim}}, and $\mathbb E[\ln S_T] = {{v:c8_ElogS}}$ against the theoretical {{v:c8_ElogS_th}}. A leveraged ETF, which multiplies $\sigma$ by its leverage, multiplies the drag by its square; that is why a $3\times$ fund can lose money while its index is flat.

8.4 The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

For $\mathrm dx = \kappa(\theta - x)\,\mathrm dt + \sigma\,\mathrm dW$, apply Itô to $f = e^{\kappa t}x$ (the integrating factor from the deterministic ODE): $\mathrm d(e^{\kappa t}x) = \kappa\theta e^{\kappa t}\mathrm dt + \sigma e^{\kappa t}\mathrm dW$, which integrates to $$x_t = \theta + (x_0 - \theta)e^{-\kappa t} + \sigma\int_0^t e^{-\kappa(t-s)}\,\mathrm dW_s .$$ The mean decays to $\theta$ with half-life $\ln 2/\kappa$ ({{v:c8_ou_halflife}} years for $\kappa = 5$); the variance, by the Itô isometry $\mathbb E[(\int g\,\mathrm dW)^2] = \int g^2\,\mathrm dt$, is $\frac{\sigma^2}{2\kappa}(1 - e^{-2\kappa t}) \to \sigma^2/2\kappa$ ({{v:c8_ou_statsd}}% standard deviation in the stationary state here). The OU process is the Gaussian stationary process; its autocorrelation is $e^{-\kappa\tau}$, which makes it the continuous-time AR(1) of Chapter 14, and it is the model behind the Vasicek rate model (Chapter 12), the variance process of Heston (Chapter 11, as a square-root variant), and every pairs-trading spread (Chapter 14). Physicists know it as the velocity of a Brownian particle with friction: the Langevin equation (Chapter 21).

8.5 From paths to densities: Fokker–Planck and Kolmogorov

The SDE describes one path. The probability density $p(x,t)$ of $X_t$ obeys the Fokker–Planck (forward Kolmogorov) equation $$\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[a(x,t)\,p\big] + \tfrac12\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\big[b^2(x,t)\,p\big],$$ a drift–diffusion equation that reduces to the heat equation when $a = 0$, $b = \sigma$. It is derived by taking expectations of Itô's lemma for an arbitrary test function and integrating by parts. Its adjoint, the backward Kolmogorov equation, governs how the expected value of a future payoff $u(x,t) = \mathbb E[g(X_T)\,|\,X_t = x]$ depends on the current state: $$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + a\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \tfrac12 b^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = 0,\qquad u(x,T) = g(x).$$ The operator in brackets is the Itô drift operator from the lemma: $u$ is a martingale, so its drift must vanish. This backward equation, with a discounting term added and $a, b$ taken from the risk-neutral GBM, is the Black–Scholes equation. We have now seen the same structure three times — Einstein's density equation, the heat equation, the Kolmogorov pair — and the next chapter puts a price on it.

8.6 Simulating SDEs: Euler–Maruyama and its errors

The simplest discretisation is $X_{k+1} = X_k + a(X_k)\Delta t + b(X_k)\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_k$ with $Z_k\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. Its weak error (error in expectations) is $O(\Delta t)$ and its strong error (error in the path) is $O(\sqrt{\Delta t})$; the Milstein scheme adds the Itô correction $\tfrac12 bb'(Z_k^2 - 1)\Delta t$ and improves the strong order to 1. For GBM one should not discretise at all: simulate $\ln S$ exactly using the closed-form solution, which is what the $10^5$-path check above does. For processes without closed forms (Heston, CIR) the discretisation must also respect positivity of the variance, and the "full truncation" trick of replacing $v$ by $\max(v,0)$ inside the coefficients is the standard practical fix (used in Chapter 11's Monte Carlo check).
