19Machine learning for signals

Machine learning in finance is ordinary machine learning applied to the worst dataset in the world: tiny signal, enormous noise, shifting distributions, and an adversary — the rest of the market — that removes any pattern as soon as it is found. The methods are standard; the discipline around them is not.

19.1 Why it is hard

Three facts set the problem apart from image or language modelling. The signal-to-noise ratio is minute: a very good daily return-prediction model has an $R^2$ of around 1%, so the loss function is almost entirely noise and a model that fits the training data well has, by construction, fitted noise. The data are not stationary: the mapping from features to returns drifts as the economy changes and as other participants trade the same patterns, so training data from ten years ago may be actively misleading. And the data are not independent: observations are serially correlated, targets built from multi-day returns overlap, and cross-sectional observations on the same day share a common market shock, so the effective sample size is far smaller than the row count and every standard cross-validation procedure overstates performance. None of this makes the methods useless — an information coefficient (correlation between forecast and outcome) of 0.03 applied across thousands of stocks and hundreds of days is a profitable business, by the fundamental law of active management $\mathrm{IR} \approx \mathrm{IC}\sqrt{\text{breadth}}$ — but it means the validation methodology matters more than the model.

19.2 Cross-validation that leaks

Random $k$-fold cross-validation assumes exchangeable observations. With a target that is a five-day forward return, the observations at $t$ and $t+1$ share four of their five days, so if $t$ is in the training fold and $t+1$ in the test fold, the model has effectively seen the test label. Persistent features make it worse: a feature that is 0.9-autocorrelated lets the model memorise the local level of the target. The fix is walk-forward validation (train on $[0, s)$, test on $[s, s + \Delta)$, roll forward), or purged $k$-fold in which the training observations whose labels overlap the test period are removed and an embargo gap is added after each test fold (López de Prado 2018). Combinatorial purged CV generates many train/test splits that respect these rules and gives a distribution of out-of-sample performance rather than one number.

Worked example: a 1% signal in 20 persistent features

We build 3,000 days of 20 autocorrelated features and a return whose predictable part (a linear term, a saturating term and an interaction) explains {{v:c19_true_R2}}% of its variance. With overlapping five-day targets, a gradient-boosting model evaluated by random 5-fold cross-validation reports an out-of-sample $R^2$ of {{v:c19_kfold_R2}}% — several times the true predictability, entirely leakage. The same model evaluated walk-forward reports {{v:c19_wf5_R2}}%. The first number would be presented to a committee; the second is the truth.

On one-day targets, walk-forward with annual refits: ridge regression achieves an out-of-sample $R^2$ of {{v:c19_lin_R2}}%, information coefficient {{v:c19_lin_IC}}, and — trading the sign of its forecast — an annualised Sharpe of {{v:c19_lin_SR}}; gradient boosting achieves $R^2 = {{v:c19_gb_R2}}$%, IC {{v:c19_gb_IC}}, Sharpe {{v:c19_gb_SR}}. An oracle that knows the true signal gets a Sharpe of {{v:c19_oracle_SR}}. Both models have negative out-of-sample $R^2$ — their squared errors exceed those of predicting the mean — and both are profitable, because the sign of the forecast is right slightly more than half the time. In finance a negative $R^2$ and a positive Sharpe are the normal state of affairs, and the IC, not the $R^2$, is the number to track.

19.3 Overfitting, regularisation and the bias–variance trade-off

A flexible model fitted to noise learns the noise. Five hundred deep trees fitted to 1,000 days reach an in-sample $R^2$ of {{v:c19_is_R2_deep}}% and an out-of-sample $R^2$ of {{v:c19_oos_R2_deep}}%: perfect memory, worse than useless. The expected out-of-sample error of any estimator decomposes into bias squared (what the model cannot represent), variance (how much it changes from one training sample to another) and irreducible noise; in finance the noise term is 99% of the total and the variance term is the enemy, so the winning models are heavily regularised: ridge and lasso penalties, shallow trees with slow learning rates and subsampling, early stopping, dropout and weight decay in neural networks, and — most effective of all — averaging many weak models (bagging, ensembles). The linear model with shrinkage remains hard to beat on daily data; nonlinear learners earn their keep when features interact (the $F_2 F_3$ term above), at higher frequencies where microstructure effects are genuinely nonlinear, and on alternative data (text, images, transaction records) where the feature extraction is itself the model.

19.4 Features, targets and the pipeline

Most of the work is upstream of the learner. Features must be point-in-time: computed only from data available at the moment of the forecast, with the lags of reporting and revision respected (a company's quarterly earnings become known weeks after the quarter ends; index membership lists are restated). They should be stationary — returns, ratios, $z$-scores and ranks rather than levels — and in the cross-section they are usually neutralised against market, sector and size so that the model predicts relative performance. Targets should be defined by the way the signal will be traded: forward returns over the holding period, volatility-scaled, perhaps relative to a benchmark, and with the transaction cost of acting on the forecast subtracted so the model learns what is worth trading. Feature importance (permutation importance, SHAP values) is used less to interpret than to detect leakage: a feature that is far more important than any economic story would justify is usually a look-ahead error. The pipeline is then: generate candidate features, test each against a null distribution built from shuffled or synthetic data (Chapter 20), combine the survivors in a regularised model, validate walk-forward with purging, and size the resulting forecasts with the tools of Chapter 17.

19.5 Deep learning and language models

Neural networks add representation learning: convolutional and recurrent architectures for order-book and tick data, where the structure is too rich for hand-made features; attention and transformer models for sequences of events; autoencoders for nonlinear factor models (Gu, Kelly and Xiu 2021 show modest but real gains over linear factor models in the equity cross-section). Large language models have changed the economics of text: sentiment, event extraction and the classification of filings, transcripts and news, which once needed teams of analysts and bespoke NLP, are now a prompt — and the signal in them decays as fast as everyone adopts them. The same optimisation and regularisation machinery underlies all of it (the optimisation note that precedes this one covers it in full), and the same validation discipline applies. A network trained on a decade of daily returns has perhaps 2,500 effective observations per asset; it will fit them perfectly and generalise exactly as well as its regularisation forces it to. The deep-learning results that have held up are those where the data are genuinely large (high-frequency, cross-sectional across thousands of names, or alternative) and where the model's capacity has been matched to the signal rather than to the engineer's ambition.

What to carry forward

Signal-to-noise
$R^2 \sim 1\%$; IC of 0.03–0.05 is a business; track IC, not $R^2$.
Validation
Walk-forward or purged CV with embargo; random $k$-fold leaks through overlapping labels and persistent features.
Regularise
Variance is the enemy; shrinkage, shallow ensembles, early stopping; linear is hard to beat on daily data.
Pipeline
Point-in-time, stationary, neutralised features; cost-aware targets; importance as a leak detector.
