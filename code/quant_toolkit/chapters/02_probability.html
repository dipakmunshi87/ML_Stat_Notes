2Probability for markets: returns, moments and fat tails

Prices are random. The question is how random: which distribution, with which tails, aggregating how. Getting this wrong is the single most expensive mistake in the history of the industry, so we take it slowly.

2.1 Returns: simple versus log

The simple return over a period is $R_t = S_t/S_{t-1} - 1$; the log-return is $x_t = \ln(S_t/S_{t-1}) = \ln(1+R_t)$. They agree to first order ($\ln(1+R) \approx R$ for small $R$) but differ in two ways that matter. Log-returns add over time: the return over $n$ periods is $\sum x_t$, whereas simple returns compound, $\prod(1+R_t)-1$. Simple returns add across assets: a portfolio's simple return is the weighted sum of its constituents' simple returns, which is not true of log-returns. So time-series work (volatility, random walks, option pricing) uses log-returns and portfolio work uses simple returns, with the conversion $R = e^{x}-1$ between them. A log-return of $+0.10$ is a simple return of {{v:c2_simple_ex}}%; a fall of 50% is a log-return of {{v:c2_log_ex}}, and the asymmetry is the point: log-returns are symmetric about zero in a way that simple returns are not (a $-50\%$ move needs $+100\%$ to recover).

2.2 Moments: mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis

For a random variable $X$ with density $p(x)$, the mean is $\mu = \mathbb E[X]$ and the central moments are $m_k = \mathbb E[(X-\mu)^k]$. The variance is $\sigma^2 = m_2$, the skewness is $m_3/\sigma^3$ (asymmetry; equity returns are usually negatively skewed, crashes being sharper than rallies) and the excess kurtosis is $m_4/\sigma^4 - 3$ (the 3 being the Gaussian value, so that excess kurtosis measures how much fatter than Gaussian the tails are). For daily equity-index returns the excess kurtosis is typically between 5 and 20; for a Gaussian it is zero. That one number says the Gaussian is the wrong model for daily returns, and much of risk management is about what to use instead.

Volatility is the annualised standard deviation of returns. If daily log-returns are independent with standard deviation $\sigma_d$, then over $n$ days the variance is $n\sigma_d^2$ (variances of independent variables add), so the standard deviation grows as $\sqrt n$: the square-root-of-time rule. A daily $\sigma_d = 1\%$ is an annual volatility of $1\% \times\sqrt{250} = {{v:c2_ann_vol}}\%$. This $\sqrt t$ scaling is diffusion — the same law that governs a random walk or heat spreading in a bar — and we will derive it properly in Chapter 7. It fails when returns are autocorrelated (trending or mean-reverting), which is one way to test for those.

2.3 The Gaussian and why it is the default

The normal density $\varphi(z) = e^{-z^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ is the default for three reasons. It is the maximum-entropy distribution for a given mean and variance (Chapter 21), i.e. the least-informative assumption once you know the scale. It is stable under addition: sums of Gaussians are Gaussian. And the central limit theorem says that a sum of many independent variables with finite variance tends to a Gaussian whatever their individual distributions. A daily return is the sum of thousands of individual trades, so the Gaussian is a natural first guess, and the lognormal model for prices ($\ln S_T$ Gaussian) is the model behind Black–Scholes. For a lognormal with $\ln S \sim \mathcal N(\mu, s^2)$ the mean is $e^{\mu + s^2/2}$ and the median is $e^{\mu}$: with $\mu = 0.08, s = 0.2$ these are {{v:c2_logn_mean}} and {{v:c2_logn_med}}. The gap, $s^2/2$, is the "volatility drag" that will reappear in Itô's lemma.

2.4 Fat tails: the Student-$t$ and the power law

The CLT needs finite variance and independence, and it says nothing about how fast the tails converge. Real returns have fat tails: extreme moves occur far more often than the Gaussian predicts. The simplest useful model is the Student-$t$ distribution with $\nu$ degrees of freedom, whose density decays as a power law $p(x) \propto |x|^{-(\nu+1)}$ rather than as $e^{-x^2/2}$. Its variance is finite only for $\nu > 2$ (and equals $\nu/(\nu-2)$ for a unit-scale $t$), its kurtosis is finite only for $\nu > 4$, and moments of order $\ge\nu$ do not exist at all. Fitted values for daily equity returns are typically $\nu \approx 3$–$5$, which means the fourth moment is on the edge of existing: sample kurtosis estimates are then dominated by the few largest observations and never settle down, which is exactly what one sees in practice.

Worked example: same volatility, different world

We simulate {{v:c2_N}} daily returns (40 years) from a Gaussian with $\sigma = 1\%$ and from a Student-$t$ with $\nu = 4$ scaled to the same $\sigma = 1\%$. The sample excess kurtosis is {{v:c2_kurt_g}} for the Gaussian and {{v:c2_kurt_t}} for the $t$ (whose population kurtosis is infinite at $\nu=4$). The largest loss in the Gaussian sample is {{v:c2_max_g}}$\sigma$; in the $t$ sample it is {{v:c2_max_t}}$\sigma$. Moves beyond $4\sigma$ occur {{v:c2_n4_g}} times in the Gaussian sample and {{v:c2_n4_t}} times in the $t$ sample.

The probability of a move beyond $5\sigma$ on a given day is {{v:c2_p5_g}} under the Gaussian — once every {{v:c2_yrs5_g}} years — and {{v:c2_p5_t}} under the $t$, once every {{v:c2_yrs5_t}} years. Markets deliver $5\sigma$ days every few years. The Gaussian is not slightly wrong about tails; it is wrong by three or four orders of magnitude.

The same point in risk-management units: the one-day 99% value-at-risk (the loss exceeded one day in a hundred, Chapter 18) is {{v:c2_var_g}}% under the Gaussian and {{v:c2_var_t}}% under the $t$ — not so different, because at the 99% point the two distributions have not yet parted company. At 99.9% they have: {{v:c2_var_g_999}}% versus {{v:c2_var_t_999}}%. The further into the tail a risk question goes, the more the choice of distribution dominates everything else.

2.5 Aggregation: the tails thin out, slowly

Summing fat-tailed returns over longer horizons does move them towards the Gaussian (the CLT does apply, since $\nu = 4$ has finite variance), but slowly. The 20-day sums of our $t$-returns have excess kurtosis {{v:c2_kurt_20d}} against {{v:c2_kurt_t}} for the daily series. Monthly returns look nearly Gaussian; daily returns do not; intraday returns are wilder still. A model has to be matched to the horizon at which it will be used. For infinite-variance distributions (the Lévy-stable family with tail index $\alpha < 2$) the CLT fails altogether and sums converge to a stable law with the same $\alpha$; Mandelbrot proposed these for cotton prices in 1963, and although the consensus is that financial tails have $\alpha \approx 3$–$4$ (finite variance, infinite or borderline kurtosis), the stable family is the right mental model for "the biggest event dominates the sum".

2.6 Dependence: correlation and its failure

For two returns the covariance is $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]$ and the correlation $\rho = \mathrm{Cov}/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y) \in [-1,1]$. For jointly Gaussian variables $\rho$ captures the whole dependence; for anything else it does not. Two things go wrong in markets. Correlations are not constant: they rise sharply in crises, when diversification is needed most. And linear correlation is blind to tail dependence — the probability that one asset has an extreme move given that another has. The modern tool for separating the marginal distributions from the dependence structure is the copula: by Sklar's theorem any joint distribution can be written as $F(x,y) = C\big(F_X(x), F_Y(y)\big)$ for a copula $C$ on the unit square. The Gaussian copula with Gaussian marginals is the multivariate normal; the Gaussian copula has zero tail dependence, which is why its use to price portfolios of mortgage tranches in the 2000s, with a single correlation parameter, was a disaster. The $t$-copula does have tail dependence and is the usual minimum upgrade.

What to carry forward

Log-returns
Add over time; use for dynamics. Simple returns add across assets; use for portfolios.
$\sqrt t$ scaling
Variance is linear in time for independent increments: the signature of diffusion.
Kurtosis
Daily returns are fat-tailed ($t$ with $\nu\approx 3$–$5$); the Gaussian under-prices tail events by orders of magnitude.
Correlation
Only the whole story for Gaussians; tail dependence and crisis correlation are separate facts.
