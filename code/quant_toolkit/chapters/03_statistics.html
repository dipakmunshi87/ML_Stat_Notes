3Statistics: estimation, uncertainty and the arithmetic of signal-to-noise

In physics one can usually repeat the experiment. In finance there is one history, it is short, and the quantity one most wants — the expected return — is the hardest thing in it to measure. This chapter is about knowing how little you know.

3.1 Estimators and their standard errors

Given $T$ observations $x_1,\dots,x_T$ assumed independent with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the sample mean $\hat\mu = \frac1T\sum x_t$ is unbiased and has variance $\sigma^2/T$, so its standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt T$. This innocent formula is the central fact of empirical finance. An asset with annual expected return $\mu = 8\%$ and volatility $\sigma = 20\%$ has a signal-to-noise ratio $\mu/\sigma = 0.4$ per year. To establish that $\mu > 0$ at the conventional two-standard-error level one needs $\sqrt T \ge 2\sigma/\mu$, i.e. $$T \ge \Big(\frac{2\sigma}{\mu}\Big)^2 = {{v:c3_T_needed}}\ \text{years}.$$ After one year the standard error of the mean is {{v:c3_se_1y}}%, larger than the mean itself; after ten years it is still {{v:c3_se_10y}}%. Sampling more often does not help: daily data give $250$ times as many points but each with $1/250$ of the variance per unit time, and the standard error of the annualised mean over a fixed window is $\sigma/\sqrt{T_{\rm years}}$ regardless. The mean is determined by the endpoints: $\hat\mu = (\ln S_T - \ln S_0)/T$ for log-returns. Everything in between is noise.

Variance is a different story. The sample variance $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum(x_t-\hat\mu)^2$ has, for Gaussian data, a relative standard error of $\sqrt{2/T}$ in the variance and $1/\sqrt{2T}$ in the standard deviation, and here $T$ counts observations, so sampling faster does help. One year of daily data pins the volatility down to about {{v:c3_se_vol_rel}}%; one year of daily data pins the mean down to nothing. This asymmetry — volatility is measurable, expected return is not — shapes the whole industry. It is why option pricing (which needs $\sigma$ but, as we will see, not $\mu$) is a science while asset allocation (which needs $\mu$) is partly an art.

3.2 The Sharpe ratio and its sampling distribution

The Sharpe ratio $\mathrm{SR} = (\mu - r_f)/\sigma$ is the signal-to-noise ratio of a strategy, annualised. Its estimator $\widehat{\mathrm{SR}} = \hat\mu/\hat\sigma$ inherits the noise of $\hat\mu$: for Gaussian returns and $T$ years of data (Lo 2002), $$\mathrm{se}\big(\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}\big) \approx \sqrt{\frac{1 + \tfrac12 \mathrm{SR}^2}{T}} .$$ For a true $\mathrm{SR} = 0.4$ this is {{v:c3_se_sr_1}} after one year, {{v:c3_se_sr_3}} after three and {{v:c3_se_sr_10}} after ten. A three-year track record with a Sharpe of 0.4 is statistically indistinguishable from zero. (Non-Gaussian returns make it worse: skewness and kurtosis add terms to the numerator.)

Worked example: 20,000 three-year track records

We simulate 20,000 independent three-year daily return histories with true annual $\mu = 8\%$, $\sigma = 20\%$ (true SR $= {{v:c3_sr_true}}$) and compute the sample Sharpe of each. The standard deviation across histories is {{v:c3_sr_sd_sim}}, matching the formula. {{v:c3_sr_frac_neg}}% of the histories show a negative Sharpe and {{v:c3_sr_frac_gt1}}% show a Sharpe above 1.0 — all from the same strategy. An allocator who hires the top decile and fires the bottom decile after three years is mostly selecting on luck.

3.3 Maximum likelihood and the Fisher information

The general machine for estimation is maximum likelihood. Given a model $p(x\,|\,\theta)$ and independent data, the log-likelihood is $\ell(\theta) = \sum_t \ln p(x_t\,|\,\theta)$ and $\hat\theta_{\rm ML}$ maximises it. Two properties make it the default. It is asymptotically efficient: no unbiased estimator does better. And its uncertainty is computable from the curvature of $\ell$ at the maximum, the Fisher information $$\mathcal I(\theta) = -\mathbb E\Big[\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial\theta\,\partial\theta^\top}\Big], \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\hat\theta) \ge \mathcal I^{-1}$$ (the Cramér–Rao bound, attained asymptotically by ML). For the Gaussian mean, $\mathcal I_{\mu\mu} = T/\sigma^2$, giving back $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu) = \sigma^2/T$: with $\sigma = 0.2$ and $T = 10$ this is {{v:c3_crlb_mu}}. A physicist knows this matrix from Fisher forecasting of survey constraints; it is the same object, and the same intuition — a sharply peaked likelihood means a well-determined parameter — transfers intact.

Worked example: fitting the tail index

Maximum-likelihood fitting of a Student-$t$ to the 40-year simulated series of Chapter 2 recovers $\hat\nu = {{v:c3_nu_hat}}$ (true value 4) and a scale of {{v:c3_sc_hat}}$\sigma$ (true value $\sqrt{(\nu-2)/\nu} = 0.707$). Note what the fit does not use: the sample kurtosis. For $\nu$ near 4 the kurtosis is a terrible statistic (it barely exists), whereas the likelihood uses the whole shape of the distribution and converges normally. Method-of-moments estimators are often the worst choice precisely where the tails matter.

3.4 Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests

A $95\%$ confidence interval for a parameter is $\hat\theta \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{se}(\hat\theta)$ when the estimator is approximately normal. A hypothesis test of $H_0: \mu = 0$ uses the $t$-statistic $\hat\mu/\mathrm{se}(\hat\mu)$ and rejects when $|t| > 1.96$ (at the 5% level). A trading signal that has averaged 3 bp a day with a 1% daily standard deviation over 500 days has $t = {{v:c3_tstat}}$ and $p = {{v:c3_pval}}$: no evidence at all, even though "3 bp a day" is 7.5% a year and would be a fine strategy if real. The $t$-statistic is the universal currency for "is this effect real", and Chapter 20 will show why even $t = 3$ is not enough once one has searched over many candidate strategies.

3.5 The bootstrap: uncertainty without formulas

When no formula for a standard error exists — for a quantile, a drawdown, a Sharpe ratio under fat tails — resample. The bootstrap draws $T$ observations with replacement from the data, recomputes the statistic, and repeats $B$ times; the spread of the $B$ values estimates the sampling distribution. For time series the draws must respect dependence (block bootstrap: resample contiguous blocks). As an example, the empirical one-day 99% VaR of the first three years of the $t$-returns is {{v:c3_var_hat}}%, with a bootstrap 95% interval of [{{v:c3_var_lo}}%, {{v:c3_var_hi}}%]: a tail quantile estimated from 750 points, 7 or 8 of which lie beyond it, is uncertain by about $\pm10\%$ of its value, and that is before model error.

3.6 Bayesian estimation: shrinkage as a prior

Given that expected returns are so poorly determined by data, it is natural to combine data with prior belief. Bayes' theorem, $p(\theta\,|\,x) \propto p(x\,|\,\theta)\,p(\theta)$, does exactly that, and for a Gaussian likelihood with a Gaussian prior the posterior mean is a precision-weighted average: $$\hat\mu_{\rm post} = \frac{T/\sigma^2}{T/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2}\,\hat\mu + \frac{1/\tau^2}{T/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2}\,\mu_0 ,$$ where $\mu_0$ and $\tau^2$ are the prior mean and variance. Data pull the estimate towards the sample mean, the prior pulls it towards $\mu_0$, and the weights are the inverse variances. This is shrinkage, and it recurs everywhere in finance under other names: the Black–Litterman model (shrink expected returns towards those implied by the market portfolio), Ledoit–Wolf covariance estimation (shrink the sample covariance towards a structured target, Chapter 16), ridge regression (shrink coefficients towards zero, Chapter 6). Whenever one has many noisy estimates, shrinking them towards a common value lowers total error — the James–Stein result — and the finance industry is a machine for producing many noisy estimates.
