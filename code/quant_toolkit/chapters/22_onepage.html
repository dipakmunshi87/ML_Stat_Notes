The five ideas underneath

No arbitrage. Identical cash flows have identical prices. It prices bonds from a curve, forces put–call parity, and — through continuous hedging — removes the expected return from option prices and replaces it with the risk-neutral measure. Every pricing formula in the note is a statement of replication cost.

Diffusion. Variance grows linearly in time; $(\mathrm dW)^2 = \mathrm dt$. It gives the $\sqrt t$ scaling of volatility, Itô's correction term, the heat equation, the Gaussian smoothing of payoffs, and the realised variance that makes volatility — unlike drift — measurable.

Signal-to-noise. $\sigma/\sqrt T$. Expected returns are the least measurable quantity in the subject, which is why estimation error dominates portfolio construction, why shrinkage appears everywhere, why Sharpe ratios need decades, and why backtests lie.

Fat tails and clustering. The Gaussian is wrong in the tails by orders of magnitude; volatility is predictable even when returns are not. Everything in risk management, from the choice of $\nu$ to expected shortfall to extreme-value extrapolation, follows from taking this seriously.

The spectrum. Covariance matrices have a few informative eigenvalues and a bulk of noise with a computable shape. Factor models, random-matrix cleaning and constrained optimisation are three ways of refusing to trade the noise.

A quant who can derive each formula in the table from the idea beside it, and can say what number it produces for the canonical example of its chapter, has the toolkit. The rest is the particular market, the particular data, and the judgement — which this note cannot supply — about when the model is the wrong one.
