Learning the distribution
Generative AI, language models, diffusion & Bayesian inference, one idea

A surprising amount of modern machine learning is one task wearing different hats: learn a probability distribution from examples, then use it. An image generator learns the distribution of pictures. A language model learns the distribution of the next word. A Bayesian inference engine learns the distribution of parameters that could have produced your data. The machine that does this is a neural density estimator, and it is the thread running through all of them.

A probability density, from scratch

A probability density p(x) says how much probability sits near each value of x. It must be non-negative and integrate to one: ∫ p(x) dx = 1. You almost never get p(x) handed to you — instead you get samples drawn from it (measurements, pixels, words) and must work backwards to the density that produced them. That backwards step is density estimation.

p(x) ≥ 0,    ∫ p(x) dx = 1,    P(a < x < b) = ∫ab p(x) dx a density is a shape whose area is probability — the histogram of infinitely many samples, infinitely finely binned

Interactive: sample a density into existence

The true density below (blue curve) is a mixture of two bumps — think “two kinds of object.” Press Stream and samples rain down; their histogram (grey) climbs toward the true curve. With a handful of samples the estimate is noisy; with thousands it locks on. That convergence is the whole game.

Blue curve: the true density p(x). Grey bars: the histogram of the samples so far (normalised to an area of 1). Watch the bars converge to the curve.
The key move. Samples are all you ever see; the density is what you want. Every method in this deck is a different way to turn a pile of samples into a usable p(x) — one you can both evaluate and sample from.

The two things a density model must do

“Having” a distribution means being able to do two different jobs, and models differ in which they make easy:

Evaluate

Given a point x, return the number p(x) — how probable is this exact image / sentence / parameter value? Needed for likelihoods, for comparing models, for anomaly detection (“p(x) is tiny ⇒ weird”).

Sample

Produce a fresh x drawn from p(x) — a new face, a paragraph, a plausible parameter. This is what “generative” means: run the learned distribution forward to make new data.

A plain neural network outputs a number or a label. A density model is built so its output is a whole distribution — and crucially one that stays valid: non-negative and integrating to one. The architectures in this deck are exactly the tricks that guarantee that while keeping one or both jobs cheap.

TOY EXAMPLE

Evaluate vs sample on a coin-and-Gaussian

Let p(x) = ½ N(x; −1, 0.5) + ½ N(x; +1, 0.5). Two jobs, two operations:

Evaluate at x = 0:   p(0) = ½(0.108) + ½(0.108) = 0.108
Sample:   flip a fair coin → heads: draw N(−1, 0.5); tails: draw N(+1, 0.5)

Sampling here is a two-step recipe (pick a bump, then draw from it) — a mixture. Evaluating is a plug-in. Notice they use the model in opposite directions; some neural models make one direction far cheaper than the other.

Remember the pair. As each model appears, ask: is evaluate easy, is sample easy, or both? Flows do both; diffusion samples beautifully but evaluates awkwardly; energy models evaluate up to a constant but sample slowly.

The pre-neural toolkit

Before neural anything, two methods already estimate a density from samples. They are worth knowing because the neural versions are their scalable descendants.

Histogram

Chop x into bins, count samples per bin, normalise. Simple and assumption-free — but blocky, and it dies in high dimensions (bins grow as (resolution)d).

Kernel density estimate

Drop a little smooth bump (a “kernel”) on every sample and add them up. Smooth and consistent, but you must choose the bandwidth h, and it needs all the data at evaluation time.

p̂(x) = (1 / Nh)   ∑i   K( (x − xi) / h ) the kernel density estimate: a bump K of width h on each of the N samples, averaged

Interactive: the bandwidth is everything

Nine samples sit on the axis (ticks). Each wears a Gaussian bump; their sum (blue) is the estimate. Drag the bandwidth h: too small and the estimate is spiky (it memorises the samples); too large and it is a featureless blob (it over-smooths). The sweet spot in between is the eternal bias–variance trade-off.

Grey ticks: the samples. Thin curves: the per-sample kernels. Blue: their sum, the density estimate. Small h → spiky; large h → over-smoothed.
Why go neural. Histograms and KDE are perfect in 1–2 dimensions and hopeless in 100. Images live in tens of thousands of dimensions; you cannot bin or carry all the data. Neural density estimators are what replace them there.

Why the classic tools break

In high dimensions, space is almost all empty and almost all “edge.” A histogram with 10 bins per axis needs 10d bins — 10100 for a small image. No dataset fills that. KDE needs every sample at evaluation and its bumps become vanishingly local. The data you have is a razor-thin sliver of the space, so you cannot estimate the density by filling the space; you must learn its structure.

TOY EXAMPLE

Where does the volume go?

Take the unit cube in d dimensions and ask what fraction of its volume lies in the outer 5% shell near the surface:

fraction in the shell = 1 − 0.90d
d = 1:  10%   |   d = 10:  65%   |   d = 100:  99.997%

By d = 100 essentially all the volume is in a thin skin, and points are all roughly the same (large) distance apart. “Nearby samples” — the thing histograms and KDE rely on — stops meaning anything. A neural network sidesteps this by sharing structure across the space instead of partitioning it.

What a neural density estimator buys you

A neural network is a flexible, parameter-efficient function approximator. Wire one up so that its output is guaranteed to be a valid distribution, train it to make the observed samples probable, and you get a density that generalises — it puts probability on plausible new points it never saw, because it learned the shape rather than memorising locations. The rest of the deck is four ways to do exactly that.

Mixtures

a network outputs the weights, means and spreads of a sum of Gaussians

Flows

bend a simple distribution through invertible neural maps

Autoregressive / diffusion

build the sample up piece by piece, or denoise it into being

The gentlest neural density: a learned mixture

The simplest flexible density is a mixture of Gaussians: a weighted sum of bumps. Enough bumps can approximate almost any shape. A mixture density network is just a neural net whose outputs are the mixture’s parameters — the weights πk, means μk and widths σk — possibly as a function of some input. Train it and it learns where to place the bumps.

p(x) = ∑k   πk   N(x; μk, σk),    ∑k πk = 1 a valid density for free: non-negative weights that sum to one, each bump already normalised

Interactive: watch a mixture learn the data

The grey samples come from a two-bump distribution. Press Run and a two-component mixture is fitted by the classic EM algorithm: each step re-assigns points softly to bumps and moves each bump to its points. Watch the two components (light) slide into place and their sum (blue) snap onto the data. The number bottom-right is the log-likelihood — it only ever goes up.

Grey bars: data histogram. Light curves: the two Gaussian components. Blue: their weighted sum. Each EM step raises the log-likelihood until the fit stops improving.
Strengths & limits. Mixtures are cheap and interpretable and give you both jobs (evaluate and sample) easily. But in very high dimensions you need unwieldy numbers of components; that is where flows and autoregressive models take over.

Bend a simple distribution into a complex one

A normalizing flow starts from something trivial — a standard Gaussian — and pushes it through an invertible transformation g. Because g is invertible, you can track exactly how probability mass stretches and squeezes, so the output is a genuine, normalised density you can evaluate and sample. The bookkeeping is the change-of-variables formula:

pY(y) = pX(g−1(y))   | d g−1/dy | the Jacobian factor is the “conservation of probability” — stretch the axis, thin the density

Sampling is trivial: draw x from the Gaussian, output y = g(x). Evaluating is the formula above. A single 1D map can only reshape (skew, sharpen, spread); the power comes from stacking many, which the next page shows in 2D.

Interactive: a Gaussian through an invertible map

The base is a standard normal (grey). The transform is g(x) = sinh(αx)/α — invertible for any α, and the identity at α = 0. Drag α and watch the transformed density (blue) sharpen its peak and grow heavy tails. Same Gaussian underneath; the map does all the shaping, and the area stays exactly one.

Grey: the base standard normal pX. Blue: the transformed density pY from the change-of-variables formula. The Jacobian keeps the blue area equal to one at every α.
Why flows are prized. They give an exact likelihood (unlike diffusion or GANs) and fast sampling — which is exactly what simulation-based inference wants from a posterior. They are the workhorse of neural posterior estimation.

Depth makes flows universal

One invertible map reshapes; a stack of them, each simple, composes into an arbitrarily complex warp — the same reason deep networks beat shallow ones. Modern flows (RealNVP, MAF, neural spline flows) use clever invertible layers whose Jacobian is cheap to compute, so you can stack dozens. The result can turn a featureless Gaussian blob into a ring, two moons, or the manifold of natural images.

Interactive: morph a Gaussian into a target

Left is a cloud of points from a 2D Gaussian; right is the target shape. Press Morph and the flow carries every point from blob to target and back — a normalizing flow is exactly this learned, invertible transport of probability mass. Switch the target between a ring and two moons.

Each dot is one sample carried by the flow. At the blob end it is a plain Gaussian; at the target end it matches the data. A real flow learns this map so both directions — sample and evaluate — are exact.
The mental picture. A flow is a smooth, reversible re-arrangement of probability — a change of coordinates in which your complicated data looks like a plain Gaussian. Training finds the coordinates; sampling runs them backwards.

Factorise the joint into a chain of conditionals

Any joint distribution factorises exactly, with no approximation, by the product (chain) rule:

p(x1, x2, …, xn) = p(x1)   p(x2 | x1)   p(x3 | x1, x2)   …   p(xn | x<n) predict each coordinate from the ones before it — a density built out of ordinary next-step predictors

An autoregressive model learns each conditional p(xi | x<i) with a single network and multiplies them. Evaluating a density becomes a sequence of ordinary predictions; sampling becomes: draw x1, feed it back to draw x2, and so on. This is how PixelCNN generates images pixel by pixel — and, with “coordinates” being words, exactly how a language model works.

TOY EXAMPLE

A sentence as a product of conditionals

The probability a model assigns to a short phrase is literally the chain rule, one token at a time:

p(“the sky is dark”) =
  p(the) × p(sky | the) × p(is | the sky) × p(dark | the sky is)

Training maximises this probability on real text; generating text samples it left to right. The next page turns this into something you can watch run.

The bridge to language models. An LLM is an autoregressive density estimator over token sequences. “Learning to write” is learning p(next token | everything so far); “generating” is sampling that density one token at a time.

A language model is a next-symbol density

Strip a large language model to its essence and it is doing the autoregressive trick on text: it models p(next token | the tokens so far), a valid distribution over the vocabulary at every step. Train it to make real text probable; then generate by repeatedly sampling the next token and feeding it back. The only differences from the toy below are scale (billions of parameters, tokens not characters) and a Transformer doing the conditioning — the principle is identical.

Interactive: a tiny character-level language model

This model was “trained” (its counts tallied) on a short passage. It predicts the next character from the previous few. Press Generate: it samples one character at a time, feeds it back, and writes. The bars show p(next char | context) — the very distribution being sampled. Raise the context and the text gets more word-like; raise the temperature and it gets wilder (flatter distribution); lower it and it gets repetitive (peakier).

Top: the text being generated (the growing context is highlighted). Bottom: p(next character | context), with the sampled character marked. This is an autoregressive density over sequences — the same object as an LLM, shrunk to characters.
So “generative AI” = density estimation. Text, images and audio generators all learn p(data) and sample it. An LLM is the autoregressive-over-tokens case; a diffusion image model (next page) is another case of the same goal.

Destroy structure, then learn to undo it

Diffusion models — the engines behind modern image generators — learn a density by a beautiful trick. Forward: gradually add noise to data until it becomes pure Gaussian static; this direction is easy and needs no learning. Reverse: train a network to remove a little noise at each step; run it from static back to data and you have generated a fresh sample. The thing the network learns is the score — the gradient of the log-density, ∇ log pt(x) — which points “toward where the data is.”

forward: add noise  →  pt smooths toward a Gaussian   |   reverse: x ← x + ε ∇ log pt(x) + noise generation is following the learned score back up the density, from noise to a sample

Interactive: forward noising and reverse denoising

The target is a two-bump density. Press Noise and a cloud of points diffuses out until it is a shapeless blob (forward). Press Denoise and the points follow the score back down into the two bumps (reverse) — generating samples of the target from noise. The histogram tracks where the points are.

Dots: a cloud of points. Grey curve: the target two-bump density. Forward spreads the cloud into noise; reverse follows the score ∇ log p back into the two bumps — that is generation.
Evaluate vs sample, again. Diffusion samples superbly but its exact likelihood is awkward — the opposite trade-off from a flow. Same overall goal (learn p(x)), different corner of the design space.

From modelling data to answering questions

Everything so far learned p(x) — the distribution of the data itself. Turn the knob one notch and you get a conditional density p(θ | x): the distribution of some quantity of interest θ given observed data x. Every architecture in this deck has a conditional version — a mixture density network, a conditional flow, a conditional diffusion model — where the network simply takes x as an extra input and outputs the distribution over θ.

learn   qφ(θ | x)   ≈   p(θ | x) a neural density estimator with an input: give it data x, it returns the whole distribution over θ

Why does this matter? Because p(θ | x) is exactly what Bayesian inference wants: the posterior — what the data tell you about the unknown θ. If a neural density estimator can learn a conditional distribution, it can learn a posterior. That is the door from generative modelling into inference, and the next pages walk through it.

Generative use

x = an image, θ = its class or caption; or x = a prompt, θ = the continuation. Conditioning is how “text-to-image” and “prompted” models work.

Inference use

x = your measured data, θ = the physical parameters that produced it. The learned q(θ|x) is the posterior — error bars included.

Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior

Bayesian inference asks: given data x, what do we believe about the parameters θ? The answer is the posterior p(θ | x), assembled from the likelihood p(x | θ) (how probable the data is for each θ) and the prior p(θ) (what we believed beforehand):

p(θ | x) = p(x | θ)   p(θ)   /   p(x) Bayes’ theorem — the posterior is the prior reshaped by how well each θ explains the data
TOY EXAMPLE

Inferring a mean, by hand

One measurement x = 1.6 of an unknown mean θ, with known noise σ = 1, and a prior θ ~ N(0, τ=2). The posterior is Gaussian with a tidy closed form:

precision = 1/σ2 + 1/τ2 = 1 + 0.25 = 1.25
posterior mean = (x/σ2) / precision = 1.6 / 1.25 = 1.28
posterior sd = 1/√precision = 0.89  →  θ = 1.28 ± 0.89

The data pull θ up from the prior mean 0 toward 1.6; the prior holds it back a little. We will reproduce this same answer on the next page without ever using this formula — purely from simulations.

The catch: intractable likelihoods

That worked because p(x | θ) had a formula. In real science the “likelihood” is often a simulator — a physics code, an instrument model, a stochastic process — that you can run (given θ, produce plausible x) but whose probability you cannot write down. Classical Bayesian computation (MCMC) needs to evaluate the likelihood, so it stalls. This is where neural density estimation returns as the hero.

When the parameters have a population

Often you are not inferring one θ but many related ones — the true masses of many clusters, the effects in many trials, the properties of many galaxies — drawn from a shared population. A hierarchical Bayesian model (HBM) infers the individual parameters and the population that generated them at the same time, so each estimate borrows strength from the rest.

φ ~ hyperprior
  └─ for each object i:
     θi ~ p(θi | φ)    (population)
     xi ~ p(xi | θi)    (measurement)

An HBM is itself a generative model: it says exactly how to simulate data — draw the population, draw each object, draw each measurement. And a generative model you can sample from but perhaps not write a likelihood for is precisely what simulation-based inference consumes. HBMs and neural density estimation meet here: the hierarchy defines the simulator; the neural density estimator learns the posterior over its parameters.

One-sentence link. HBM says what to infer (a population behind noisy, latent data); SBI says how when the likelihood is intractable (simulate, then learn the posterior with a neural density estimator). A companion deck covers HBMs in full.

Infer without a likelihood — just simulate

Simulation-based inference (SBI, also “likelihood-free” inference) needs only a simulator: a black box that turns parameters θ into data x. The recipe:

1. draw many θ from the prior
2. for each, run the simulator to get x  →  a pile of (θ, x) pairs
3. train a conditional neural density estimator q(θ | x) on those pairs
4. feed in the real data x0  →  out comes the posterior p(θ | x0)

No likelihood is ever evaluated. The simulator supplies examples; the neural density estimator — the very object from the generative-AI pages — learns the posterior from them. This is neural posterior estimation. (Older SBI, called ABC, keeps only simulations whose x lands near x0; the toy below shows that intuition, which the neural version scales up.)

Interactive: a posterior built from simulations

Simulator: x = θ + noise (σ = 1); prior θ ~ N(0, 2). Set the observed x₀ and press Simulate: dots are (θ, x) pairs; those landing in the band near x₀ are “accepted,” and their θ values (histogram, right) build the posterior. The blue curve is the exact answer from the last page — watch the simulation-only histogram converge to it.

Left: (θ, x) simulations; green = accepted (x within the band of x₀). Right: histogram of accepted θ — the posterior — against the exact Gaussian (blue). Narrow band = more exact but fewer accepted.
The pay-off. Any simulator you can run becomes a Bayesian likelihood. The neural version replaces “keep near-misses” with “learn q(θ|x),” so it uses every simulation and works in many dimensions.

Train once, infer forever

There is a subtle, powerful bonus. The conditional estimator q(θ | x) takes the data x as an input. So once it is trained, you can feed it any dataset and read off the posterior instantly — no re-running the sampler. This is amortized inference: pay a large training cost once, then each new inference is a single fast network evaluation.

Classical (per-dataset)

MCMC re-runs from scratch for every new dataset — hours each. Accurate, but it does not reuse effort.

Amortized (per-problem)

Train q(θ|x) once over the whole prior; then a new dataset’s posterior is one forward pass — milliseconds. Ideal when you must fit thousands of objects.

WHY IT MATTERS

The same idea you already met

An LLM is amortized too: it was trained once, and answering a new prompt is a forward pass, not a retraining. Amortized posterior estimation is that trick pointed at inference — the network has “pre-solved” the inference problem for every dataset the prior can produce, and just looks up the answer for yours.

The trade. Amortization front-loads all the cost and risk into training and into how well the simulator matches reality. Get those right and inference becomes essentially free — which is what makes population-scale science feasible.

The one check that matters

A generated image only has to look right. A posterior has to be right — its error bars must mean what they say, or every scientific conclusion drawn from it is wrong. So SBI comes with a mandatory test: simulation-based calibration (SBC). The logic is simple: if you draw a true θ* from the prior, simulate data, and infer a posterior, then across many repeats the rank of θ* within its posterior samples should be uniform. Anything else exposes a miscalibrated model.

Interactive: a calibration histogram forming

Each trial: draw a truth, infer a posterior, record where the truth falls among the posterior samples (its rank). Press Run and the rank histogram fills. Well-calibrated → flat. Toggle to Overconfident (too-narrow posteriors) → a ∪-shape: the truth keeps landing in the tails because the posterior is too sure of itself.

Bars: how often the true value lands at each rank within its posterior. Flat = calibrated (honest error bars). ∪-shaped = overconfident. Dashed line: the flat target.
The discipline. This is the line between a pretty generative model and a trustworthy inference. “It samples nice pictures” is not enough; “its 90% interval contains the truth 90% of the time” is the bar.

Where to go next

Libraries

sbi and LAMPE for neural posterior/likelihood estimation; nflows / zuko for normalizing flows; Pyro, NumPyro and PyMC for probabilistic programming; diffusers for diffusion models. Most are a few lines to a working posterior.

Good defaults

Start with a masked autoregressive flow or neural spline flow for the density; always run simulation-based calibration; check posterior predictive samples look like real data; and watch that your simulator actually matches the world.

Pitfalls worth naming

  • Model misspecification. If the simulator is wrong, an amortized posterior is confidently wrong — and it will not tell you. Calibration on real-adjacent data and posterior-predictive checks are your guardrails.
  • Mode collapse / missed modes. A flexible density can still miss a far-off mode it rarely saw in training; widen the prior and the simulation budget.
  • Calibration is not optional. A great-looking sample says nothing about whether the uncertainty is honest. Test the ranks.
  • Summary statistics. High-dimensional x is often compressed to summaries before inference; a bad summary throws away the very information the posterior needed.
The mindset. Treat every generative model as a density and ask the two questions — can I evaluate it, can I sample it — and, for inference, the third: are its probabilities calibrated? Those three questions organise the entire field.
